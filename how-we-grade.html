How we grade

Every connection on this site carries a grade. This page explains exactly what those grades mean, how they are decided, and how often we get it wrong on the first try.

If you only read one line: a connection appears on ManyRoots because someone checked the primary research and it survived an adversarial review — or it does not appear at all.

Cited, or absent. There is no third option.

There is no “traditionally believed to support” tier with nothing behind it, and no connection that exists because a product needed one. If we could not find and verify the evidence, the connection is simply not drawn.

The five grades

A connection’s thickness and pattern carry its grade, so you can read the strength of the evidence before you read a word of text.

Strong27 connections

Multiple randomised trials or meta-analyses agreeing, or first-line status in a clinical practice guideline.

Moderate84 connections

Real trial or meta-analysis evidence, positive overall, but modest in size or mixed across studies.

Early69 connections

Small, few, or preliminary human trials only. Interesting, not established.

Traditional use9 connections

Documented traditional or historical use, but no adequate human trial for this condition. Shown honestly rather than dressed up or left out.

Not supported19 connections

Adequate evidence exists and it is null or negative, or guidelines recommend against it. These stay visible — that is the point.

When two grades are arguable, we take the lower one. That rule is applied deliberately and it is why several popular things on this site are graded lower than you will see them graded elsewhere.

How a connection gets made

  1. Research

    An agent researches one intervention for one condition directly against PubMed in a single session. Nothing is written from memory, and every number quoted has to come from an abstract actually retrieved.

  2. Adversarial review

    A second, independent agent is told to refute the first. It resolves every cited identifier back to the real paper, checks that the numbers match, searches specifically for null and negative trials the first agent may have skipped, and challenges the grade in both directions.

  3. Verdict

    Pass, fixed, or fail. Fixed entries ship corrected. A failed entry is not a to-do item — it is an absent connection. The connection does not get drawn.

  4. Recorded

    Every verdict and every correction is stored with the entry, which is how the numbers on this page are produced rather than claimed.

What that process actually catches

These are real corrections from the current data, not illustrations.

Invented numbers, deleted

A researcher wrote that a major Lancet trial of sleep restriction therapy showed effect sizes at 3 and 12 months. The paper reports only its 6-month endpoint. Both numbers were fabricated; the verifier removed them.

Sleep · sleep restriction therapy · PMID 37573859

Publication bias, caught

A duloxetine entry cited a positive back-pain trial and omitted the negative trial from the same authors, the same sponsor and the same drug programme, in which duloxetine did not beat placebo at endpoint. The negative trial was added.

Chronic pain · duloxetine · PMID 19469829

Grade lowered

Ramelteon was graded moderate on a 2017 guideline. The verifier found the 2023 European Insomnia Guideline, which names it as not recommended at the highest evidence level, and the grade dropped to not supported.

Sleep · ramelteon · PMID 38016484

Grade raised

Skullcap was written up as having no human sleep trial at all. That was false — trials existed. The verifier corrected the claim and moved the grade up. The check is not biased toward pessimism; it is biased toward what is there.

Sleep · skullcap

Safety claim refuted

A wild lettuce entry stated that human harm data did not exist. A published case report of poisoning did exist. The false reassurance was removed and the case report added.

Sleep · wild lettuce · PMID 21686920

Overclaim corrected

Arnica was graded early, implying preliminary human support for chronic pain. No placebo-controlled trial of topical arnica exists in any chronic pain condition. It was moved to traditional use.

Chronic pain · arnica

The same thing can be graded differently

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance. The evidence for something can be decent in one condition and absent in another, so the same node carries a different verdict on each connection.

What ManyRoots does not do

  • We sell nothing. No products, no supplements, no affiliate links, no sponsored placements, no brand names on ingredient pages.
  • We do not rank by popularity. A connection’s weight comes from its evidence, never from how many people search for it or how large its market is.
  • We do not hide the failures. Widely used things that the evidence does not support stay on the map, clearly labelled. Removing them would make the map a sales tool.
  • We do not give you a plan. No personalised protocols, no “your journey”, no dosing instructions dressed as advice.
  • We do not diagnose. ManyRoots is a map of evidence, not a clinician.

Where this can be wrong

A page about rigour that claims none of its own limits is not being rigorous. Here are the real ones.

  • Grades are a snapshot. Evidence moves. A grade reflects the literature at the date it was checked, and some of these will be out of date before they are revisited.
  • Abstract-level checking has a ceiling. Verification resolves citations and confirms reported figures against published abstracts. It does not re-analyse raw trial data or detect fraud inside a study that was published cleanly.
  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. “Traditional use” and “not supported” mean the trials are missing or came out null — not that a thing is proven useless. Several genuinely useful things are under-studied because nobody could profit from studying them.
  • Averages hide people. Every effect size here is an average across a trial population. Individuals vary, and a modest average can still contain people who responded very well and people who got nothing.
  • Our scope is narrow by choice. Only a small number of conditions are covered so far. A connection that is missing may simply not have been researched yet.
  • Machines drafted this, and machines check it. The review step catches a great deal — 190 corrections so far — but it is not a substitute for clinical peer review, and errors will survive it. If you find one, we want to be told.
