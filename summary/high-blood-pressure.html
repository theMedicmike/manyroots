Before anything else — when to stop and get care

emergencyHypertensive emergency: a very high reading TOGETHER WITH symptoms of organ damageCALL 911 NOW (or your local emergency number). If your blood pressure is around 180/120 or higher AND you have any of these at the same time: • chest pain or pressure • trouble breathing or sudden breathlessness • a sudden, severe headache unlike your usual ones • any change in your vision • weakness or numbness on one side of your body • trouble speaking, slurred words, or confusion ...this is a hypertensive emergency. That combination means damage may be happening right now — to your brain, heart, kidneys or eyes. Do not drive yourself. Do not sit and re-check the reading first. Do not take an extra dose of your medication and lie down to see if it settles. Do not finish reading this page. Call for an ambulance.
emergencyStroke signs at ANY blood pressure readingCALL 911 IMMEDIATELY if you see any ONE of these — at any blood pressure, including a normal one: F — FACE drooping on one side. Ask them to smile. A — ARM weakness. Ask them to raise both arms; does one drift down? S — SPEECH difficulty. Slurred, garbled, or the wrong words coming out. T — TIME. Note the exact time symptoms started and tell the ambulance crew. Every minute counts. Time is brain. Do not lie down to 'sleep it off.' Do not wait to see if it passes. Do not wait for a blood pressure reading, and do not be reassured by a normal one — a normal reading does not rule out a stroke. Do not drive yourself or the person to hospital; call the ambulance, because treatment can start on the way.
emergencyChest pain or pressure — never write it off as 'just my blood pressure'CALL 911 FOR CHEST PAIN. Now — not after you check your blood pressure. Call for pressure, tightness, squeezing or heaviness in the chest; pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back; chest pain with sweating, nausea or breathlessness; or a sudden severe tearing pain in the chest or between the shoulder blades. Never tell yourself it is 'just' high blood pressure, and never assume a normal reading means your heart is fine. Blood pressure readings cannot diagnose or rule out a heart attack or a tear in the aorta. Sit down, stay on the line with the dispatcher, and let them decide what it is.
urgent🔴 NEVER stop blood pressure medication abruptly, and never swap it for a supplement🔴 READ THIS BEFORE YOU CHANGE ANYTHING. Do not stop, skip, halve or swap your blood pressure medication because of anything you read here — or anywhere else online. Nothing on ManyRoots is a replacement for a prescribed blood pressure medicine. Not a supplement, not a tea, not a food, not a breathing practice. Stopping suddenly can drive your blood pressure HIGHER than it was before you ever started treatment. This is called rebound hypertension, it is real, and it is worst with clonidine and with beta blockers — deaths have been reported after abrupt clonidine withdrawal. If you want to change something — side effects, cost, or you would simply rather try something else first — that is a completely legitimate conversation, and it is a conversation with your prescriber, who can taper you down safely. Bring this page to that appointment if it helps. Just do not do it alone, and do not do it overnight. If you have ALREADY stopped abruptly: contact your prescriber or an urgent care service today.
urgentNew high blood pressure in pregnancy or after birth — possible pre-eclampsiaPREGNANT OR GAVE BIRTH IN THE LAST 6 WEEKS? Different rules apply to you. If your blood pressure is newly 140/90 or above, contact your midwife, obstetrician or maternity triage line TODAY — not at your next scheduled appointment. GO TO THE EMERGENCY DEPARTMENT OR CALL 911 if high blood pressure comes with any of these: • a bad or persistent headache • changes in your vision — flashing lights, blurring, blind spots • pain under the ribs on your right side, or in the upper abdomen • sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet • a seizure, or feeling suddenly confused This can be pre-eclampsia, which can escalate quickly and affects the baby as well as you. Pre-eclampsia can also appear for the first time AFTER delivery, so do not assume you are past it because the baby has arrived.
urgentA reading of 180/120 or higher with NO symptoms — same-day call, not a panicA very high reading with NO symptoms is serious, but it is usually not an ambulance. Do this instead: 1. Sit quietly, feet flat on the floor, back supported, arm resting at heart level. Wait five minutes. Do not talk. 2. Re-check. Take two or three readings a minute apart and use the average, not the scariest one. 3. If it is still around 180/120 or higher, contact your clinician the SAME DAY — a phone line, an urgent appointment, or an out-of-hours service. Do not wait for your next routine appointment. 4. If ANY of the emergency symptoms above appear at any point — chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, vision change, one-sided weakness, trouble speaking, confusion — stop and call 911. One high reading is not a diagnosis, and going to the emergency department for a number alone will not usually change what happens to you. Doing nothing about it for six months, however, will.
routine🔴 High blood pressure usually has NO symptoms — feeling fine proves nothing🔴 YOU CANNOT FEEL YOUR BLOOD PRESSURE. High blood pressure almost always causes no symptoms whatsoever. Feeling well is not evidence that your blood pressure is well. This is exactly why it is called the silent killer — and why the only way to know is to measure it. Specifically, do not use these as your warning system: • HEADACHES. Studied in the general population, high blood pressure shows no clear association with headache at all. • NOSEBLEEDS. Slightly more common in people with hypertension, but far too weak and too common a symptom to tell you anything about your own reading. • Flushing, dizziness, a 'pounding' feeling, or feeling stressed. None of these track your actual numbers. If you have not had your blood pressure measured in the last year, that — not how you feel — is the thing to act on this week.
routineEveryday things that quietly push blood pressure up or blunt your medicationTell your clinician or pharmacist EVERYTHING you take — including things you would not think of as medication. These are the common culprits: • ANTI-INFLAMMATORY PAINKILLERS (ibuprofen and similar NSAIDs) taken regularly. Not the odd tablet — the daily habit for a bad back or arthritis. Ask about alternatives before you stop anything you were prescribed. • DECONGESTANTS containing pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine — cold and flu tablets, sinus remedies. The average effect is small, but it is larger with higher doses and fast-acting formulations, so read the box and ask the pharmacist. • LIQUORICE. Real liquorice root, not just the flavour: some sweets, some herbal teas, some traditional remedies. Regular heavy intake is a recognised cause of raised blood pressure and low potassium, and at the extreme it lands people in hospital. • HEAVY ALCOHOL. If you drink more than two drinks a day, cutting down measurably lowers blood pressure — the more you currently drink, the bigger the drop. None of these are reasons to stop a prescribed medicine on your own. They are reasons to have a five-minute conversation.
routineHome monitoring only helps if the device is validated, the cuff fits, and the technique is rightA wrong number at home is worse than no number, because you and your clinician will both act on it. IF YOU MONITOR AT HOME: • Use a VALIDATED UPPER-ARM cuff. Not a wrist device, not a smartwatch, not a fingertip clip. Wrist monitors read higher and disagree with arm monitors often enough to mislabel people as hypertensive. • MEASURE YOUR ARM and buy the matching cuff size. A standard cuff on a large arm can add nearly 20 mm Hg to your systolic reading — enough to invent a diagnosis that is not there. A standard cuff on a small arm hides one that is. • TECHNIQUE: empty your bladder; sit for five minutes; back supported, feet flat, legs uncrossed; cuff on bare skin; arm resting at heart level; do not talk during the reading. • Take two or three readings a minute apart, morning and evening, and record ALL of them. Do not delete the ones you dislike. • Bring the device itself to your next appointment and ask them to check it against theirs. And never make a treatment decision off a single reading — yours or anyone else's.
