Before anything else — when to stop and get care

emergencyThoughts of suicide, a plan, an intent to act, or you have already hurt yourselfStop reading and get help now. If you are thinking about killing yourself, have a plan, feel you might act, or have already hurt yourself: call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — free, confidential, 24/7. Veterans: call 988 and press 1, or text 838255. If you are in immediate danger, or you have already taken something or injured yourself, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency room. If you can, do not be alone right now: ask someone to come and stay with you, and put distance between yourself and anything you could use to hurt yourself — pills, weapons, car keys. Nothing on this page is a substitute for making that call, and no supplement, protocol or reading is worth the wait.
emergencySudden calm, resolve or 'peace' after weeks of feeling terrible — giving things away, writing notes, saying goodbye, putting affairs in orderIf you have suddenly felt calm or settled after weeks of feeling awful, and you are giving possessions away, writing notes, saying goodbye, or getting your affairs in order — treat that as an emergency, not as getting better. Call or text 988 now (Veterans: call 988 and press 1). If you are watching someone else do this, stay with them, ask them directly whether they are thinking of suicide, and call 988 or 911 with them. Asking the question does not put the idea in their head.
emergencyThoughts of harming yourself or your baby after giving birth, or confusion, feeling sped-up, suspicion, or not sleeping at allGet help immediately. If you have had a baby in the past year and you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby — or you feel confused, unusually 'high' or sped up, suspicious of people around you, or you have not slept for days — call or text 988, or call 911 or go to an emergency room, and tell them you have recently given birth. If you can, have someone stay with you and the baby until help arrives. Having these thoughts does not make you a bad mother, and it does not mean your baby will be taken from you. Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency and it responds to treatment — telling someone is what protects both of you.
emergencySigns of serotonin syndrome after combining serotonin-acting medicines or supplementsCall 911 or go to an emergency room now, and tell them exactly what you have taken and when — bring the bottles if you can. Signs to act on: agitation or confusion, a racing heart, high blood pressure, sweating, shivering, fever, muscle twitching, jerking or stiffness, loss of coordination, or diarrhoea — appearing within hours of starting or increasing a serotonin-acting drug or supplement, or combining two. Common combinations that cause it include an SSRI or SNRI taken with St John's Wort, tramadol, a triptan migraine medicine, lithium, or certain other antidepressants.
urgentPsychosis alongside depression — hearing voices, seeing things others do not, or fixed beliefs others say are not trueThis needs a psychiatric assessment today, not a supplement. If you are hearing voices, seeing things other people do not see, or holding beliefs others tell you are not true — for example that you are being watched or followed, that you have committed an unforgivable crime, that you are already dead or your body is rotting, or that you deserve to be punished — call your doctor or a mental health crisis line today and say exactly what you are experiencing, or go to an emergency room. If you feel unsafe, or the voices are telling you to hurt yourself or anyone else, call or text 988 or call 911 now. Depression with psychotic features is a recognised, treatable form of the illness, and it needs a specific treatment plan.
urgentNot eating or drinking, or no longer able to care for yourself or your dependantsThis is a medical situation, not a willpower problem. If you have stopped eating or drinking, have not been able to get out of bed for days, are not taking essential medicines, or cannot manage basic care for yourself, your children or your pets — call your doctor's office today and use those exact words, or go to urgent care. If you have gone more than about a day without fluids, or you feel faint, dizzy on standing, confused, or you cannot stay awake, go to an emergency room now. If someone you love has stopped eating and drinking and has become still, silent or unresponsive, do not wait it out — take them in.
urgentFeeling worse, agitated, or newly suicidal in the first weeks after starting, stopping or changing an antidepressant — especially if you are under 25Contact your prescriber today and say exactly this: 'I started/changed my antidepressant on [date] and I feel more agitated, restless, or I'm having new thoughts of suicide.' Do not just stop the medicine on your own — abrupt stopping brings its own problems and makes it harder to work out what is happening. If the thoughts feel like something you might act on, call or text 988 or go to an emergency room first, then call the prescriber. If the person affected is a teenager or young adult, do not leave the check-in to them: make the call yourself and stay close during the first few weeks and after every dose change.
urgentAlready taking an antidepressant and now feeling unusually high, wired, sleepless or recklessContact your prescriber today. If you are on an antidepressant and you now feel unusually high or wired, do not need sleep and do not feel tired, have racing thoughts or pressured speech, or you are spending, driving, or taking risks in a way that is not like you — say so. This can be a switch into mania and the medication plan may need to change. If your judgement or safety is affected, if you are spending money you do not have, or if the people around you are frightened, go to an emergency room or call 988.
urgentTaking St John's Wort alongside an SSRI/SNRI, hormonal birth control, warfarin, HIV medicines, or transplant drugsTalk to your pharmacist and prescriber this week, and bring the bottle. Do not combine St John's Wort with an SSRI, SNRI, tricyclic, triptan, tramadol, lithium or any other serotonin-acting medicine unless a prescriber has cleared it — the combination can cause serotonin syndrome. St John's Wort also makes many prescriptions weaker or unreliable, including hormonal birth control (breakthrough bleeding and unplanned pregnancies have been reported), warfarin, HIV medicines such as indinavir and nevirapine, the transplant drugs ciclosporin and tacrolimus (organ rejection has been reported), digoxin, simvastatin, methadone and some chemotherapy. Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own to make room for a supplement — and if you are already taking both, do not stop either one abruptly; get advice first.
urgentDrinking more or using drugs to get through the day or to sleepTell whoever is treating your depression — this is not a side issue, and it changes what will and will not work. Alcohol and sedatives can deepen depression, disrupt the sleep they seem to help, and lower the threshold for acting on suicidal thoughts while you are intoxicated. If you are drinking heavily every day, do not stop on your own either: abrupt alcohol withdrawal can be medically dangerous and needs supervision. Ask your doctor about a safe plan for both at once.
routineAny history of mania or unusually high, elevated periods — before starting an antidepressant or St John's WortBefore you start an antidepressant — or St John's Wort — tell your prescriber if you have ever had a period of feeling unusually high, wired or invincible; needing very little sleep and not feeling tired; talking much faster than usual; or spending, driving or taking risks in a way that was not like you. Ask directly: 'could this be bipolar?' Family history counts too. This is not a reason to avoid treatment — it is a reason to get the right treatment, because the plan for bipolar depression is different from the plan for unipolar depression.
routineWanting to come off an antidepressantPlan it with your prescriber rather than stopping suddenly. Roughly one in six to seven people get discontinuation symptoms — dizziness, 'electric shock' or 'brain zap' sensations, nausea, irritability, flu-like feelings, vivid dreams or sleep disruption — and a slow, planned taper is how you reduce them. Some medicines are harder to stop than others. Stopping abruptly can also let the depression itself return, which is easy to mistake for withdrawal and hard to sort out on your own. Ask for a written taper schedule and a check-in date.
