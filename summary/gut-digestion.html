Before anything else — when to stop and get care

emergencyVomiting blood (or material that looks like coffee grounds), passing a large amount of blood from the bowel, or feeling faint, dizzy, clammy or short of breath alongside any bleeding.Stop here and call 911 (or your local emergency number) now. Do not drive yourself, do not wait to see whether it settles, and do not eat or drink until you have been assessed. If you take a blood thinner, aspirin, or an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen or naproxen, tell the crew or the emergency team and bring the packets if you can.
emergencyFever with severe abdominal pain; a belly that is rigid, board-like, or exquisitely painful to touch; or pain with vomiting and no bowel movement and no passing of gas.Go to the emergency department now, or call 911 if the pain is severe or you cannot get there safely. Do not eat or drink, do not take a laxative, do not use an enema, and do not take painkillers to see whether it passes — each of those can worsen an obstruction or a perforation and can mask what the surgical team needs to be able to feel.
emergencySevere, new, or worsening abdominal pain or vomiting in pregnancy — especially pain high in the abdomen or under the right ribs.Contact your maternity unit or obstetric team the same day. Go to the emergency department or call 911 if the pain is severe or constant, if it comes with headache, visual changes or sudden swelling, or if you cannot keep fluids down. Do not treat this as reflux or indigestion, and do not start a supplement or an elimination diet in pregnancy without your maternity team's input.
urgentAny blood in or on the stool, blood on the paper, or stools that are black and tarry — even once, even if you are sure it is haemorrhoids.Book a doctor's appointment now, and use the words "rectal bleeding" or "black stools" when you book — that phrase changes how quickly you are seen. Do not start a new diet, supplement, fibre regimen or cleanse while you wait. If the bleeding becomes heavy, or you feel faint, go to the emergency department instead.
urgentLosing weight without trying, or new gut symptoms that started after age 50.Ask your doctor for a workup, not a diet trial. Say how much weight you have lost and over what period, and that the symptoms are new. Ask directly whether you need a stool test or a colonoscopy, and hold off on any elimination diet until that conversation has happened.
urgentPain or diarrhoea that wakes you from sleep.Tell your doctor specifically that symptoms wake you at night. That single detail points away from IBS and can change the tests you are offered. Ask about a stool test for inflammation (faecal calprotectin), and — if diarrhoea is persistent and watery — ask whether you need a colonoscopy with biopsies even if the bowel looks normal.
urgentAnaemia, or persistent fatigue, breathlessness or pallor alongside gut symptoms.Ask for blood tests — at minimum a full blood count and ferritin — before you try to fix the fatigue with supplements or a diet. If you are told you are iron deficient, ask what the plan is to find out why, not only how to replace the iron.
urgentGet tested for coeliac disease BEFORE you remove gluten — including before starting low-FODMAP, which cuts wheat too. The order is not optional.If you are thinking about going gluten-free, or starting a low-FODMAP diet, ask your doctor for coeliac blood tests FIRST, while you are still eating gluten normally. If you have already stopped, do not quietly start eating gluten again on your own — tell your clinician you have been gluten-free and for how long, and let them decide whether you need a supervised gluten challenge before testing.
routineA parent, sibling or child with bowel cancer, coeliac disease, or inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's or ulcerative colitis) — and you now have new gut symptoms.Tell your clinician the family history explicitly: who, what condition, and at what age they were diagnosed. It changes which tests you should have, when you should have them, and the age at which screening should start. Write it down before the appointment — this is the detail people most often forget to mention, and it is the one that most often changes the plan.
routineStaying on a restrictive elimination diet long-term without a dietitian — and treating low-FODMAP as a permanent way of eating rather than a temporary test.If you are more than 4-6 weeks into a low-FODMAP or elimination diet, stop expanding the restriction and ask for a referral to a registered dietitian to run the reintroduction phase. If your list of safe foods keeps shrinking, if restriction has started to feel like the only thing keeping you safe, or if you now avoid eating out or eating with other people, say that out loud to a clinician. That is a recognised risk of these diets, not a personal failing, and it is something they are meant to screen for.

Every option, graded

OptionEvidenceKindWhat the evidence says
Linaclotide and other secretagogues Strong Conventional medicine Linaclotide is a capsule that pulls fluid into the bowel and quietens pain-signalling nerves, and it is the best-evidenced prescription option for IBS when constipation is the problem.
Low-FODMAP diet Strong Food This is the eating plan with the most research behind it for IBS, and gut specialists often suggest it first.
Amitriptyline and other tricyclic antidepressants Moderate Conventional medicine Amitriptyline is an old antidepressant used here at a much smaller dose than for depression, where it works on the nerve signalling between gut and brain rather than on mood.
Important: Do not stop abruptly. The dose used for gut symptoms is far lower than the antidepressant dose - do not read one across to the other.
Antispasmodics Moderate Conventional medicine Antispasmodics are muscle-relaxing pills meant to calm the cramping and squeezing in the gut that drives IBS pain.
Rifaximin Moderate Conventional medicine Rifaximin is an antibiotic that stays almost entirely inside the gut instead of being absorbed into the body, given as a two-week course rather than long-term.
Enteric-coated peppermint oil Moderate Herb Peppermint oil in a capsule designed to open further down the gut is the best-studied herb for IBS, and when many small trials are pooled it looks helpful for cramping pain and overall…
Probiotics Moderate Nutrient Probiotics are live bacteria sold as capsules, powders, drinks and yoghurts.
Soluble fibre Moderate Nutrient Not all fibre behaves the same way in IBS, and this is one of the few places where the distinction really changes what happens to you.
Cognitive behavioural therapy Moderate Practice CBT for IBS teaches practical skills for handling the stress, worry and avoidance that get tangled up with gut symptoms, and it can be delivered face to face, by phone, or through a…
Gut-directed hypnotherapy Moderate Practice Gut-directed hypnotherapy is a guided relaxation and imagery technique, taught over several sessions, that aims to calm the conversation between the brain and the gut.
Loperamide Early Conventional medicine Loperamide is the familiar over-the-counter anti-diarrhoea medicine, and it does reliably firm up stools, cut how often you go, and reduce the urgent dash to the toilet.
Fermented foods Early Food Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi and kombucha are popular for gut health, and pooled research does show a small overall benefit for IBS symptoms.
Ginger Early Herb Ginger is a long-standing home remedy for an unsettled stomach, and it is one of the cheapest and best-tolerated things people try.
Iberogast / STW 5 herbal combination Early Herb Iberogast is a liquid blend of nine plant extracts — a newer version has six — sold in Europe for gut symptoms.
Turmeric / curcumin Early Herb Turmeric and its active compound curcumin are widely sold for gut problems, but the research in IBS specifically is thin and points in different directions.
Vitamin D Early Nutrient Low vitamin D is common in people with IBS, which sparked the idea that topping it up might calm gut symptoms.
Mindfulness meditation Early Practice Mindfulness training teaches you to notice body sensations, including gut sensations, with curiosity rather than alarm, usually as an eight-week course with daily home practice.
Regular exercise and physical activity Early Practice Moving your body regularly - walking, cycling, Pilates, general aerobic exercise - appears to take the edge off IBS symptoms for many people, and it is cheap, available and good for you…
NSAIDs Not supported Conventional medicine If you have indigestion, stomach pain, or you have noticed black stools or blood, look at your painkillers first.
Important: Black or tarry stools, blood in the stool, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds means a gastrointestinal bleed - an emergency, seek care now. Otherwise, if an NSAID was prescribed for you, don't stop or change it on your own; talk to your prescriber.
Opioids for chronic non-cancer pain Not supported Conventional medicine Opioids constipate almost everyone who takes them for long, and unlike some side effects this one does not reliably fade.
Important: Do not stop a prescribed opioid on your own to fix your bowels. After long use, reducing the dose needs a plan made with a prescriber.
