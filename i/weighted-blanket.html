Weighted blankets

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

A weighted blanket is a low-risk thing to try, and several small studies in adults suggest some people sleep better and feel calmer under one. But those studies are small, short, mostly done in people with psychiatric diagnoses rather than ordinary insomnia, and nobody can be fooled about whether their own blanket is heavy, so the results rest heavily on how people say they slept. The most carefully controlled trial of all — which measured sleep with a wrist monitor rather than by asking — found no benefit at all, even though the people in it still said they preferred the heavy blanket.

Safety

Across the RCTs and both 2024 meta-analyses, no serious adverse events were reported with weighted blanket use, so the short-term safety signal is reassuring as far as it goes, and the phase III crossover trial in children found the blankets well tolerated over its study period. The dominant risk is mechanical rather than physiological: entrapment or suffocation for anyone who cannot independently lift the blanket or move out from under it. That includes infants and young children, people with significant mobility or upper-body strength limitations, anyone sedated, and anyone who could not call for help. Long-term safety is simply unstudied; the sleep trials ran 2 weeks to 1 month, with one 12-month open follow-up phase in a single study. Note that subjective preference and measured benefit come apart here: in the best-controlled trial, children and parents favoured the weighted blanket even though wrist-monitor recordings showed no sleep improvement at all, so liking it is not evidence it is working. A 2024 scoping review found that no practice guidelines for weighted blanket use exist and called their development urgent.

Who should be careful: Do not use with infants or young children, or with any person who cannot independently remove the blanket. Standard clinical cautions, based on the mechanism rather than on trial data: obstructive sleep apnea and other respiratory compromise including COPD, claustrophobia or trauma-related sensitivity to restraint, circulatory disorders, uncontrolled hypertension, and skin or pain conditions in which sustained pressure is poorly tolerated. A published practice guideline referenced in the 2024 meta-analysis suggests weighted blankets may not be an evidence-informed practice for insomnia and sleep disturbance in children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder — consistent with the null phase III trial in that population.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions are known or plausible. The relevant interaction is with anything that reduces the ability to move or wake, including sedative-hypnotics, alcohol, opioids, and muscle relaxants, all of which increase the entrapment concern.

What the evidence says

Trial evidence for weighted blankets in insomnia is small, short, and drawn mostly from psychiatric populations, and the best-controlled trial is null. The largest adult RCT randomised 120 patients with major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, generalised anxiety disorder or ADHD to a weighted metal chain blanket or a light plastic chain blanket for 4 weeks and reported an Insomnia Severity Index advantage for the weighted blanket (p<.001, Cohen's d 1.90), with better sleep maintenance and no serious adverse events; a pilot RCT in 102 adults with clinical insomnia found greater PSQI improvement at 1 month (change -4.1 [SD 4.1] versus -2.0 [SD 3.2], P=0.006), though the actigraphy-recorded change in awakenings did not differ significantly (-1.4 versus +1.0, P=0.280). Against these sits the most rigorous trial in the field: a phase III randomised placebo-controlled crossover in 73 children with autism spectrum disorder and severe sleep problems (67 analysed) in which the weighted blanket did not increase actigraphic total sleep time and produced no group differences on any other objective or subjective sleep measure, despite children and parents preferring it. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (426 patients) found the pooled effect on insomnia was NOT statistically significant (MD -1.92, 95% CI -3.92 to 0.09, p=0.06), becoming significant (MD -2.78, 95% CI -4.44 to -1.12, p=0.001) only in a leave-one-out analysis of two remaining homogeneous studies, with half the included studies at high risk of bias from failure to blind participants. A randomised crossover trial in 45 outpatients with depression and insomnia found a ball blanket increased total night-time sleep by only 12.9 minutes (95% CI 1.21 to 24.63, p=0.031) and reduced ISI by 2.98 points, with no change in sleep-onset latency, number of awakenings, wake after sleep onset, PSQI, or medication use. A separate meta-analysis of 9 studies in 553 psychiatric patients confirmed a benefit for anxiety (SMD -0.47, 95% CI -0.68 to -0.25, p<0.001) but judged the sleep literature limited by outcome heterogeneity, few well-designed RCTs and small samples, with only one RCT at low risk of bias.

Doses studied: Nightly use for 4 weeks (weighted metal chain blanket versus light plastic chain control) to 1 month (weighted versus normal blanket); the ball-blanket crossover used 2 weeks per arm. The retrieved trial abstracts did not report standardised blanket weights, no trial compared different weights head to head, and no evidence-based guideline for selecting a weight was identified. A 2024 scoping review of 18 studies found that only 10 reported details of the intervention, only one reported on implementation, and no specific guidelines for use were included in any of them.

Why this grade: Several small randomised trials in adults exist and lean positive, but the pooled meta-analytic estimate for insomnia specifically was NOT statistically significant (MD -1.92, 95% CI -3.92 to 0.09, p=0.06) and reached significance only after leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Half of the included trials were rated high risk of bias; blinding is essentially impossible because participants can feel the weight of their own blanket; most participants were psychiatric inpatients or outpatients rather than adults with primary insomnia; and follow-up rarely exceeded 4 weeks. Decisively, the largest and best-controlled weighted-blanket sleep trial ever run — a 73-participant phase III placebo-controlled crossover with actigraphy as the primary outcome — found NO effect on total sleep time or on any other objective or subjective sleep measure, though it was conducted in autistic children rather than adults with insomnia. Deliberately not graded moderate: the single trial reporting a very large effect (Cohen's d 1.90) is an outlier against an otherwise null-to-small overall picture.

Sources (7)
  1. A randomized controlled study of weighted chain blankets for insomnia in psychiatric disorders.
    Randomised trialEkholm B, Spulber S, Adler M, 2020 (J Clin Sleep Med) · PMID 32536366 · positive

    120 patients with major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, generalised anxiety disorder or ADHD randomised 1:1 to a weighted metal chain blanket or a light plastic chain blanket for 4 weeks. Significant advantage for the weighted blanket on Insomnia Severity Index at 4 weeks (P<.001, Cohen's d 1.90), with significantly better sleep maintenance, higher daytime activity level, and reduced daytime symptoms of fatigue, depression and anxiety. No serious adverse events occurred. Effects were maintained during a 12-month open follow-up phase.

  2. Weighted blankets and sleep in autistic children--a randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialGringras P, Green D, Wright B, et al., 2014 (Pediatrics) · PMID 25022743 · null

    Phase III randomised, placebo-controlled crossover trial in children aged 5 to 16 years 10 months with confirmed autism spectrum disorder and severe sleep problems refractory to community interventions; a commercially available weighted blanket versus an otherwise identical usual-weight control blanket, each used for 2 weeks. 73 children randomised, 67 analysed. Using objective measures, the weighted blanket did NOT increase actigraphic total sleep time adjusted for baseline, and there were NO group differences in any other objective or subjective measure of sleep, including sleep-onset latency, sleep efficiency and behavioural outcomes. On subjective preference measures parents and children nonetheless favoured the weighted blanket, and blankets were well tolerated. This is the largest and most rigorously controlled weighted-blanket sleep trial available.

  3. Safety and effectiveness of weighted blankets for symptom management in patients with mental disorders: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisZhao Y, Zhang Q, Liu Z, Zhao Z, Zhu Q, 2024 (Complement Ther Med) · PMID 39447684 · mixed

    8 RCTs, 426 patients. The pooled estimate of three studies reporting insomnia found NO significant difference between weighted blanket and control (MD -1.92, 95% CI -3.92 to 0.09, p=0.06); significance appeared only in a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis of the two remaining homogeneous studies (MD -2.78, 95% CI -4.44 to -1.12, p=0.001). Anxiety showed a small decrease (SMD 0.40). Half the included studies were rated high risk of bias, primarily due to failure to blind participants for participant-reported measures. The review notes an existing practice guideline suggesting weighted blankets might not be an evidence-informed practice for insomnia and sleep disturbance in children and adolescents with autism spectrum disorder. No serious adverse events were reported.

  4. Effect of weighted blankets on sleep quality among adults with insomnia: a pilot randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialYu J, Du J, Yang Z, et al., 2024 (BMC Psychiatry) · PMID 39501163 · positive

    102 adults with clinical insomnia at three Chinese tertiary hospitals randomised 1:1 to a weighted or normal blanket for 1 month; 95 (93.1%) completed. PSQI change was -4.1 (SD 4.1) with the weighted blanket versus -2.0 (SD 3.2) with the normal blanket (P=0.006), with similar advantages for daytime sleepiness, stress, anxiety, fatigue and bodily pain (all P<0.05). Actigraphy showed -1.4 (SD 9.5) awakenings in the weighted group versus +1.0 (SD 7.9) in the control group, a difference that was NOT statistically significant (P=0.280). No severe adverse events. The authors state that validation in a larger sample is needed.

  5. The efficacy of ball blankets on insomnia in depression in outpatient clinics: A randomised crossover multicentre trial.
    Randomised trialKristiansen ST, Videbech P, Speed M, Dionysopoulos P, Bjerrum MB, Larsen ER, 2024 (J Sleep Res) · PMID 38740439 · mixed

    45 outpatients with unipolar depression, subjective insomnia and PSQI >5 slept 2 weeks with a weighted ball blanket and 2 weeks with a control duvet in randomised order, serving as their own controls. Total night-time sleep increased by only 12.9 minutes (95% CI 1.21 to 24.63, p=0.031); ISI fell 2.98 points (95% CI -5.45 to -0.50, p=0.020) and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale fell 2.78 points (95% CI -5.44 to -0.11, p=0.042). NO changes were observed in sleep-onset latency, number of awakenings, wake after sleep onset, PSQI, Major Depression Inventory, Beck Anxiety Index, or medication use.

  6. The effect of weighted blankets on sleep quality and mental health symptoms in people with psychiatric disorders in inpatient and outpatient settings: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisWong S, Fabiano N, Luu B, et al., 2024 (J Psychiatr Res) · PMID 39341068 · mixed

    9 studies of 553 psychiatric inpatients and outpatients (289 weighted blanket, 264 control); intervention length ranged from 5 minutes to one year. Anxiety improved versus placebo (SMD -0.47, 95% CI -0.68 to -0.25, p<0.001). Only four studies reported evidence for weighted blankets improving insomnia, total sleep time or sleep-onset latency. Risk of bias: one RCT low, three with some concerns, one high; three cohort studies rated 'fair' and one 'poor'. The authors conclude the literature is limited by heterogeneity of outcome reporting, lack of well-designed RCTs and small sample sizes, highlighting the need for higher-quality studies.

  7. Weighted Blankets as a Sleep Intervention: A Scoping Review.
    Journal articleDawson S, Charlton K, Ng L, et al., 2024 (Am J Occup Ther) · PMID 39163284 · mixed

    PRISMA-guided scoping review of overnight weighted blanket use across seven databases; 18 studies met inclusion criteria. Positive outcomes were reported for adults including improved sleep, reduced medication use and improved mood, but sleep outcomes were MIXED for children and adolescents, and methodological quality regarding effectiveness was variable. Ten studies included details of the intervention, only ONE reported on implementation, and NO specific guidelines for use were included in any study. The authors state that development of practice guidelines incorporating current research findings is urgently needed.
