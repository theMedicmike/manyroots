Herbs

Garlic (including aged garlic extract)

Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Garlic supplements do appear to lower blood pressure a little, and the effect shows up in people whose pressure is already high rather than in people with normal readings. The best current estimate, pooling more than a hundred trials, is around three to four points off the top number, which is real but smaller than the drops you would get from losing weight, cutting salt, or taking a prescribed blood pressure medicine. No study has yet shown that garlic prevents heart attacks or strokes, only that it nudges the number on the monitor.

Safety

Generally well tolerated in trials. A 2015 systematic review of seven randomised placebo-controlled hypertension trials reported no serious adverse events in any of them. The most consistently reported adverse effects are gastrointestinal discomfort and breath and body odour; the 2025 meta-analysis found adverse reactions slightly more frequent with garlic than control though not statistically significantly so. Reported harms across the pooled hypertension trials were mild and transient. Tolerability data are nonetheless less complete than efficacy data: the Cochrane review specifically found insufficient data reported on adverse events and on withdrawals due to adverse events. Blood pressure should be monitored rather than assumed controlled, and garlic should not be used to replace prescribed antihypertensive therapy.

Who should be careful: Should be stopped in advance of scheduled surgery or invasive dental procedures because of garlic's antiplatelet activity and the theoretical bleeding risk that follows from it; note this concern rests on mechanism and case reports rather than on trial evidence of harm. People with an active bleeding disorder, and those about to undergo a procedure, should not take concentrated garlic supplements without discussing it with their clinician. People with garlic or Allium allergy should avoid it. Not a substitute for prescribed antihypertensive medication, and not appropriate as sole management of stage 2 hypertension.

Interactions: The best-documented interaction is with the HIV protease inhibitor saquinavir: in ten healthy volunteers, garlic caplets cut saquinavir exposure sharply, reducing area under the curve by 51%, peak concentration by 54% and 8-hour trough levels by 49%, and levels had returned to only 60-70% of baseline after a 10-day washout. Anyone on HIV therapy should check with a pharmacist before use. Caution is also advised with anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs (for example warfarin, clopidogrel, aspirin, direct oral anticoagulants) because of potential additive bleeding risk from garlic's antiplatelet effect. Additive blood pressure lowering is possible when combined with antihypertensive drugs, which is usually desirable but warrants monitoring.

What the evidence says

Meta-analytic estimates are consistent in direction and shrinking in size as more trials accumulate. The largest and most recent synthesis, covering 108 RCTs and 7137 participants, reported a systolic reduction of 3.71 mmHg (95% CI -5.07 to -2.36) and diastolic of 1.97 mmHg (95% CI -2.86 to -1.08). An earlier 20-trial pool (970 participants) reported 5.1 +/- 2.2 mmHg systolic and 2.5 +/- 1.6 mmHg diastolic, rising to 8.7 +/- 2.2 mmHg systolic in the hypertensive subgroup, and a 17-trial meta-analysis found systolic -3.75 mmHg (95% CI -5.04 to -2.45) overall with a significant systolic reduction in hypertensive but NOT normotensive participants. A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs reported systolic -4.21 mmHg (95% CI -5.74 to -2.69) while concluding the overall impact on hypertension may be limited. The 2012 Cochrane review is the key check on all of this: it identified only two eligible randomised trials totalling 87 hypertensive patients, and although it reported that garlic reduced supine systolic and diastolic pressure by roughly 10-12 mmHg and 6-9 mmHg over placebo, it judged those confidence intervals imprecise and the difference to fall within the known variability of blood pressure measurement, and concluded there is insufficient evidence to determine whether garlic reduces mortality or cardiovascular morbidity. A 2023 triple-blind RCT of optimised aged black garlic added to existing antihypertensive drug therapy in grade I hypertension found only 1.8 mmHg systolic (95% CI 0.7 to 4.1) and 1.5 mmHg diastolic (95% CI 0.3 to 3.0) over 12 weeks. Every one of these measures blood pressure as a surrogate; no trial has demonstrated reduced heart attacks, strokes or deaths.

Doses studied: Preparations and doses varied widely across pooled trials and are not interchangeable. Specific verified examples: an optimised aged black garlic extract standardised to 0.25 mg/day S-allyl-cysteine for 12 weeks; and 200 mg garlic powder three times daily added to background hydrochlorothiazide-triamterene therapy. A 2025 meta-analysis reported an optimal S-allyl-cysteine range of 0.5-1.5 mg, with subgroup reductions seen at a garlic dosage of 2.4 mg and at an 8-week intervention length. The 108-trial 2026 meta-analysis found benefit concentrated in adults with unfavourable baseline risk factors.

Why this grade: Multiple independent meta-analyses of RCTs converge on a real, reproducible systolic reduction, though the size of that reduction shrinks as the evidence base grows: the largest synthesis to date (108 RCTs, 7137 participants) found 3.7 mmHg systolic and 2.0 mmHg diastolic, against the 5-8 mmHg reported by smaller earlier pools. It is held below 'strong' because no trial anywhere has reported cardiovascular events or death; because the Cochrane review found only two trials meeting strict criteria and judged the blood pressure difference to fall within the known variability of blood pressure measurement; because reductions in normotensive participants are absent; and because the best-controlled modern RCT added to existing drug therapy found only about 1.8 mmHg. It is held above 'early' because 108 pooled randomised trials is not a preliminary evidence base. Torn between strong and moderate, the lower grade was taken.

Sources (6)
  1. Effects of Garlic Supplementation on Cardiovascular Risk Factors in Adults: A Comprehensive Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Behrouz V, Zahroodi M, Clark CCT, Mir E, Atashi N, Rivaz R, 2026 · PMID 40580481 · positive

    The largest synthesis available: 108 RCTs and 7137 participants. Garlic reduced systolic blood pressure by 3.71 mmHg (95% CI -5.07 to -2.36) and diastolic by 1.97 mmHg (95% CI -2.86 to -1.08), alongside improvements in lipids, fasting glucose and inflammatory markers. Effects were concentrated in adults with unfavourable baseline risk profiles. Garlic did NOT influence apolipoprotein A-1, apolipoprotein B, alanine aminotransferase, interleukin-6, body mass index, weight or adiponectin (all P >= .05). Authors called for larger samples, longer follow-up and better methodological rigour before definitive conclusions.

  2. Garlic Lowers Blood Pressure in Hypertensive Individuals, Regulates Serum Cholesterol, and Stimulates Immunity: An Updated Meta-analysis and Review.
    Ried K, 2016 · PMID 26764326 · positive

    Meta-analysis of 20 trials with 970 participants found a mean decrease in systolic blood pressure of 5.1 +/- 2.2 mmHg (P<0.001) and diastolic of 2.5 +/- 1.6 mmHg (P<0.002) versus placebo; in the subgroup hypertensive at baseline the reductions were larger at 8.7 +/- 2.2 mmHg systolic (n=10 trials) and 6.1 +/- 1.3 mmHg diastolic (n=6 trials). Authors stated that future long-term trials are needed to elucidate any effect on cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Note this estimate is larger than the later 108-trial pooled figure.

  3. Effect of garlic on blood pressure: a meta-analysis.
    Wang HP, Yang J, Qin LQ, Yang XJ, 2015 · PMID 25557383 · mixed

    Pooled analysis of 17 trials found systolic reduction of 3.75 mmHg (95% CI -5.04 to -2.45) and diastolic of 3.39 mmHg (95% CI -4.14 to -2.65). The key negative result is in the subgroup analysis: a significant systolic reduction was seen in hypertensive participants (-4.4 mmHg, 95% CI -7.37 to -1.42) but NOT in normotensive participants, and within the hypertensive subgroup no significant diastolic reduction was seen until after sensitivity analysis removed heterogeneity (-2.68 mmHg, 95% CI -4.93 to -0.42).

  4. Garlic for the prevention of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in hypertensive patients.
    Stabler SN, Tejani AM, Huynh F, Fowkes C (Cochrane), 2012 · PMID 22895963 · mixed

    Cochrane review of garlic as monotherapy identified only two randomised placebo-controlled trials (87 hypertensive patients total). One trial (47 patients) found garlic reduced mean supine systolic pressure by 12 mmHg (95% CI 0.56 to 23.44, p=0.04) and diastolic by 9 mmHg (95% CI 2.49 to 15.51, p=0.007); the second could not be meta-analysed. Overall the reviewers estimated reductions of approximately 10-12 mmHg systolic and 6-9 mmHg diastolic over placebo, but concluded the confidence intervals were imprecise and that this difference falls within the known variability of blood pressure measurement, making the true impact difficult to determine. Neither trial reported clinical outcomes and insufficient data were provided on adverse events, so there is insufficient evidence to determine whether garlic reduces mortality or cardiovascular morbidity.

  5. Antihypertensive Effects of an Optimized Aged Garlic Extract in Subjects with Grade I Hypertension and Antihypertensive Drug Therapy: A Randomized, Triple-Blind Controlled Trial.
    Serrano JCE et al., 2023 · PMID 37686723 · mixed

    Randomised triple-blind placebo-controlled trial of aged black garlic extract (0.25 mg/day S-allyl-cysteine) for 12 weeks in grade I hypertension already on drug therapy found a systolic reduction of only 1.8 mmHg (95% CI 0.7 to 4.1) and diastolic of 1.5 mmHg (95% CI 0.3 to 3.0). Blood nitric oxide and antioxidant capacity rose and ACE activity fell, but NO changes in endothelial function or inflammatory cytokines were observed. This is the smallest effect in the modern literature and the closest test of garlic added on top of medication.

  6. Meta-analysis on the safety and efficacy of long-term garlic consumption as an adjunctive treatment for hypertension.
    Tang Y et al., 2025 · PMID 41393949 · mixed

    Meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found systolic reduction of 4.21 mmHg (95% CI -5.74 to -2.69) and diastolic of 3.13 mmHg (95% CI -4.42 to -1.84), but authors concluded the overall impact on hypertension may be limited and that significant reductions were confined to particular subgroups (obese, aged 50-60, higher baseline diastolic). Adverse reactions were slightly more frequent in the garlic group though not statistically significantly so, most commonly gastrointestinal discomfort and bad breath.
