Minerals & nutrients

Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)

Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Vitamin B6 is genuinely necessary for making serotonin and melatonin, which is why it turns up in so many sleep formulas — but when researchers have tested B6 on its own against a placebo, it has not improved sleep. One large trial found it increased how much dream content people remembered without improving sleep at all, and the B-complex arm of that same study actually reported worse sleep and more morning tiredness. Correcting a real deficiency is a different matter, but taking extra B6 for sleep is not supported, and high doses carry a real risk of nerve damage.

Safety

Vitamin B6 is the one nutrient in this group where supplementation carries a documented, serious risk. Excess pyridoxine causes sensory peripheral neuropathy — numbness, tingling, burning pain and unsteadiness — and more than 50 such cases were reported in the few years after 2014 alone. Mechanistically this is a paradox: pyridoxine, the inactive form used in most supplements, competitively inhibits the active form pyridoxal-5'-phosphate, so taking too much produces symptoms resembling deficiency. On dose, neuropathy is documented at intakes of 1000 mg/day or more (roughly 800 times the daily intake from food), with occasional reports of toxicity at 100-300 mg/day; US authorities set a no-observed-adverse-effect level of 200 mg/day and a safe upper limit of 100 mg/day. The doses used in the sleep research (40-240 mg/day) sit at or above that upper limit. Newer European guidance is far more conservative — the European Food Safety Authority's tolerable upper intake level is 12 mg/day — and a case of B6 toxicity has been reported in a patient whose only source was a multivitamin containing 6 mg/day. Because the sleep benefit is not supported, there is no favourable risk-benefit case for taking supplemental B6 for sleep.

Who should be careful: Do not take high-dose vitamin B6 without medical supervision and monitoring, because of the neuropathy risk; note that US and European authorities set very different upper limits (100 mg/day versus 12 mg/day), so the conservative figure is the safer guide. Stop and seek medical advice promptly if numbness, tingling, burning pain or balance problems develop. People with Parkinson's disease on levodopa and people taking phenytoin or phenobarbital should not supplement B6 without clinician input. Confirmed vitamin B6 deficiency is a legitimate reason to supplement, but that is a clinical diagnosis, not a self-assessment, and it is a separate question from whether B6 helps sleep.

Interactions: The following reflect established pharmacology rather than findings from the trials cited above. Vitamin B6 reduces the effectiveness of levodopa when taken without a decarboxylase inhibitor such as carbidopa, and can lower blood levels of phenytoin and phenobarbital. Some medications deplete vitamin B6 and clinicians sometimes prescribe supplementation for that specific reason, which is a different situation from taking it for sleep. Check with a pharmacist before adding B6 to any medication regimen.

What the evidence says

Evidence for vitamin B6 supplementation in insomnia and poor sleep in adults is null. In a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 100 participants (Adventure-Heart 2018), 240 mg pyridoxine hydrochloride before bed for five consecutive nights significantly increased the amount of dream content recalled but did not significantly affect dream vividness, bizarreness or colour, nor any other sleep-related variable; the exploratory B-complex arm showed significantly LOWER self-rated sleep quality and significantly HIGHER tiredness on waking. A factorial randomised double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in 47 adults (Garcia-Garcia and Baik 2021) is the cleanest test of B6 in isolation: the group receiving vitamin B6 100 mg alone (n = 14) showed no significant change in sleep duration or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score, and poly-gamma-glutamic acid alone (n = 16) showed none either; only the combined group (n = 17) improved (sleep duration +0.27 +/- 0.98 hours; PSQI global -0.52 +/- 1.58, both p < 0.05). A double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 80 mg/day for 8 weeks in 50 patients (Badrfam 2021) found no significant Pittsburgh-scale difference against placebo (p = .23), and significantly better cognitive status in the placebo group on the MMSE (p = .01). A double-blind placebo-controlled polysomnography study in 10 older adults (Saletu 1984) using piridoxilate at 300 and 600 mg found no significant changes in objective or subjective sleep variables and no protection against traffic-noise-induced sleep disruption, though piridoxilate is a distinct drug entity rather than a plain B6 supplement. Two independent two-sample Mendelian randomisation studies agree from genetic data: Zhang 2025 found vitamin B6 inversely associated with chronotype, indicating eveningness (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.86-0.96; multivariable OR = 0.750, 95% CI 0.648-0.868, p < 0.001), but established no causal relationship between any micronutrient and either sleep duration or insomnia; Luo 2025 found magnesium the only nutrient causally linked to insomnia risk (OR = 0.869, 95% CI 0.763-0.990), with no significant association for vitamin B6. The main contrary finding is a single-blind randomised trial in 75 people with restless legs syndrome (Jadidi 2022), where 40 mg/day vitamin B6 was one of three arms and sleep quality and disease severity differed significantly at two months (P = 0.001); RLS is a distinct condition, the design was single-blind, and the abstract does not report which arm drove the difference. Other trials reporting benefit — such as melatonin plus tryptophan plus vitamin B6 in children with chronic headache — used multi-ingredient formulations from which B6's contribution cannot be separated. None of this addresses correction of documented vitamin B6 deficiency, which is a distinct clinical situation.

Doses studied: 100 mg/day for 1 month (alone, in a factorial crossover trial); 240 mg pyridoxine hydrochloride at bedtime for 5 consecutive nights (double-blind placebo-controlled); 80 mg/day for 8 weeks (double-blind placebo-controlled, adjunctive to lithium); 40 mg/day for 2 months (single-blind, restless legs syndrome); 300-600 mg piridoxilate in a polysomnography study

Why this grade: Placebo-controlled trials that isolated vitamin B6 have consistently failed to show a sleep benefit. A 100-participant double-blind randomised trial of 240 mg pyridoxine found no significant effect on sleep-related variables, and its B-complex comparison arm reported significantly WORSE self-rated sleep quality and more tiredness on waking. A factorial crossover trial found vitamin B6 alone at 100 mg produced no significant change in sleep duration or PSQI, with benefit appearing only when B6 was combined with poly-gamma-glutamic acid. A double-blind placebo-controlled trial of 80 mg/day for 8 weeks found no Pittsburgh-scale difference against placebo. A polysomnography study using a pyridoxine-containing drug in older adults found no change in objective or subjective sleep. Two independent Mendelian randomisation analyses found no causal association between vitamin B6 and insomnia. The one contrary positive result comes from a single-blind trial in restless legs syndrome — a distinct condition — and the other apparently positive results come from multi-ingredient products from which B6 cannot be isolated. This meets the definition of adequate evidence that is null. Correcting a documented deficiency is a separate clinical question.

  1. Effects of poly-gamma-glutamic acid and vitamin B6 supplements on sleep status: a randomized intervention study.
    García-García C, Baik I, 2021 · PMID 34093972 · null

    Factorial randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 47 adults. Vitamin B6 100 mg alone (n = 14) produced no significant change in sleep duration or PSQI; poly-gamma-glutamic acid 600 mg alone (n = 16) also produced none. Only the combined group (n = 17) improved (sleep duration +0.27 +/- 0.98 h; PSQI global -0.52 +/- 1.58, both p < 0.05). The authors state explicitly that no significant changes were observed in groups A and B.

  2. Effects of Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) and a B Complex Preparation on Dreaming and Sleep.
    Adventure-Heart DJ, Madden NA, Delfabbro P, 2018 · PMID 29665762 · negative

    Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 100 participants across Australia, 240 mg pyridoxine hydrochloride before bed for 5 consecutive days. B6 significantly increased the amount of dream content recalled but did not significantly affect dream vividness, bizarreness or colour, nor any other sleep-related variable. Participants in the B complex arm showed significantly lower self-rated sleep quality and significantly higher tiredness on waking.

  3. The efficacy of vitamin B6 as an adjunctive therapy to lithium in improving the symptoms of acute mania in patients with bipolar disorder, type 1; a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, clinical trial.
    Badrfam R, Mostafavi SA, Khaleghi A, et al., 2021 · PMID 34662000 · null

    Double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial, 50 patients, vitamin B6 80 mg/day or placebo for 8 weeks alongside lithium, with the Pittsburgh Sleep Questionnaire among the assessments. No statistically significant difference between B6 and placebo on the Pittsburgh scale (1.04 +/- 0.20 vs 0.48 +/- 0.50; p = .23). On the MMSE, significant improvement in cognitive status was seen in the PLACEBO group relative to B6 (25.24 +/- 1.96 vs 24.40 +/- 3.25; p = .01).

  4. Therapeutic effects of magnesium and vitamin B6 in alleviating the symptoms of restless legs syndrome: a randomized controlled clinical trial.
    Jadidi A, Rezaei Ashtiani A, Khanmohamadi Hezaveh A, Aghaepour SM, 2022 · PMID 36587225 · positive

    Single-blind randomised trial in 75 patients with restless legs syndrome allocated to magnesium oxide 250 mg/day, vitamin B6 40 mg/day, or placebo. Sleep quality and disease severity did not differ between groups at baseline or at one month, but did differ significantly at two months (P = 0.001), and the authors conclude the supplements can improve sleep quality. CONTRARY EVIDENCE, but restless legs syndrome is a distinct condition from insomnia, the design was single-blind, and the abstract does not state which arm drove the difference.

  5. [Effect of nootropic drugs on normal and disturbed sleep of the elderly: controlled studies with pyridoxilate and street noise].
    Saletu B, Grünberger J, Lesch O, 1984 · PMID 6396969 · null

    Double-blind placebo-controlled polysomnography study in 10 older adults (mean age 62) across 13 laboratory nights, testing piridoxilate at 300 and 600 mg. Piridoxilate produced no significant changes in objective or subjective sleep variables and did not ameliorate traffic-noise-induced deterioration in sleep or awakening quality. German-language article with English abstract; the abstract describes piridoxilate as a nootropic drug, and it is a distinct drug entity rather than a plain vitamin B6 supplement.

  6. Genetically Supported Causality Between Micronutrients and Sleep Behaviors: A Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization Study.
    Zhang R, Luo L, Zhang L, et al., 2025 · PMID 39910798 · null

    Two-sample Mendelian randomisation across 15 micronutrients. Vitamin B6 was inversely associated with chronotype, indicating a preference for eveningness (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.86-0.96; multivariable OR = 0.750, 95% CI 0.648-0.868, p < 0.001). No causal relationships were established between any micronutrient level and either sleep duration or insomnia.

  7. Causal relations of trace elements and nutrients with insomnia: A Mendelian randomization study.
    Luo L, Zhou D, Su Q, 2025 · PMID 41305687 · null

    Two-sample Mendelian randomisation of 7 trace elements and 8 nutrients against insomnia using FinnGen summary statistics. Only magnesium showed a possible causal link (OR = 0.869, 95% CI 0.763-0.990, P < .05). No significant causal associations were observed for vitamin B6 or any other nutrient examined; MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO, Cochran Q and leave-one-out analyses found no pleiotropy or heterogeneity.

  8. Use of nutritional supplements based on melatonin, tryptophan and vitamin B6 (Melamil Tripto) in children with primary chronic headache, with or without sleep disorders: a pilot study.
    Bravaccio C, Terrone G, Rizzo R, et al., 2019 · PMID 31621274 · mixed

    Pilot study in 34 children with chronic headache randomised to melatonin alone or melatonin plus tryptophan plus vitamin B6 for two months. Mean night awakenings in the combination group fell from 7.4 +/- 8.1 to 4.0 +/- 4.4 to 3.3 +/- 2.9 (P = 0.041), versus 3.6 +/- 3.2 to 3.2 +/- 3.5 to 2.7 +/- 3.4 (P = 0.495) with melatonin alone. Illustrates the pattern that positive results for B6 come only from multi-ingredient formulations in which its individual contribution cannot be isolated.

  9. The vitamin B6 paradox: Supplementation with high concentrations of pyridoxine leads to decreased vitamin B6 function.
    Vrolijk MF, Opperhuizen A, Jansen EHJM, Hageman GJ, Bast A, Haenen GRMM, 2017 · PMID 28716455 · negative

    Since 2014, more than 50 cases of sensory neuronal pain due to vitamin B6 supplementation have been reported. In cell studies, pyridoxine (the inactive form used in most supplements) induced concentration-dependent cell death in SHSY5Y cells while the other B6 vitamers did not affect cell viability, and both pyridoxal-5-phosphate-dependent enzymes tested were inhibited by pyridoxine. The authors conclude that pyridoxine competitively inhibits the active pyridoxal-5'-phosphate, so symptoms of over-supplementation resemble those of deficiency.

  10. [How much vitamin B6 is toxic?].
    Katan MB, 2005 · PMID 16320662 · negative

    Vitamin B6 causes neuropathy at intakes of 1000 mg per day or more, which is about 800 times the daily intake from foods, with occasional reports of toxicity at 100-300 mg per day. US authorities set the no-observed-adverse-effect level at 200 mg per day and the safe upper limit at 100 mg per day. The author notes physicians should remain alert to high B6 intake as a cause of unexplained neuropathy.

  11. Vitamin B6 Toxicity Secondary to Daily Multivitamin Use: A Case Report.
    Paluszny A, Qiu S, 2023 · PMID 38098895 · negative

    Case report of a 73-year-old man with three years of progressive peripheral neuropathy and a serum vitamin B6 of 259.9 nmol/L (reference 20-125) whose only reported source was a daily multivitamin containing 6 mg of vitamin B6. Notes that toxicity is most often associated with doses above 50 mg/day but can occur at much smaller doses, and that this case falls below the European Food Safety Authority's newly established tolerable upper intake level of 12 mg/day.
