Vinegar with meals

Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

A tablespoon or two of vinegar diluted in water with a starchy meal reliably takes some of the edge off the blood-sugar spike that follows, and that has been shown in several small experiments in people with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Whether doing this every day improves your three-month HbA1c number is much less certain: the handful of longer studies are small, they disagree with each other by a wide margin, and it does nothing for meals that are already low in starch. It is cheap and mostly harmless if diluted, but it does wear tooth enamel and can upset the stomach - and it is not a substitute for diabetes medication.

Safety

Vinegar is among the most acidic things people consume. In an 8-week randomised trial, twice-daily diluted vinegar increased dental erosive wear scores by 18% while a vinegar pill did not (PMID 33297831), and in an in vitro study some vinegars removed about 20% of enamel minerals to depths of 30-45 micrometres over 4-8 hours (PMID 24839821); dilute in water, drink with the meal rather than sipping, do not brush immediately afterwards, and stop if teeth become sensitive. In a 12-week safety study in 27 people with type 2 diabetes, 50-56% of vinegar and pickle users reported at least one treatment-emergent adverse event by week 6 (the events recorded were bowel changes, burping/flatulence and acid reflux) versus 11% of pill users, though this difference was NOT statistically significant (P=.110); urinary pH fell 9% at week 12 (P=.023) and there was a non-significant trend to rising aspartate aminotransferase (P=.090), leading the authors to conclude that chronic vinegar ingestion may influence hepatic function and metabolic pathways beyond glucose (PMID 18361754). Concentrated acetic essence is a corrosive: it appears among the causes of grade III/IV chemical oesophageal burn in a series of 56 poisoning patients, so only diluted table vinegar (5-6%) should ever be swallowed (PMID 39584511). A case report describes hypokalaemia, hyperreninaemia and osteoporosis in a patient ingesting large amounts of cider vinegar (PMID 9736833). Do not use vinegar as a reason to skip, reduce or delay prescribed diabetes treatment: the HbA1c evidence is preliminary, wildly heterogeneous between reviews, and has never been tested against any complication.

Who should be careful: Gastroparesis and delayed gastric emptying: vinegar slows stomach emptying further (median gastric emptying rate 27% to 17% in 10 people with type 1 diabetes and diabetic gastroparesis, p<0.05), which the authors note may be a disadvantage for glycaemic control and insulin timing (PMID 18093343); this applies to anyone with delayed gastric emptying and to type 1 diabetes, which is otherwise outside this site's scope. Avoid with active reflux, oesophagitis, gastric ulcer, or already-eroded tooth enamel. Caution in anyone with low potassium or on potassium-wasting treatment (PMID 9736833).

Interactions: Additive glucose-lowering with insulin or sulfonylureas could cause hypoglycaemia if doses are not adjusted, particularly because vinegar delays gastric emptying and shifts the timing of the post-meal glucose rise (PMID 18093343); monitor after meals and involve the prescriber in any dose change. Potassium-lowering drugs (loop and thiazide diuretics, high-dose corticosteroids) and digoxin: large amounts of cider vinegar have been linked to hypokalaemia in a case report (PMID 9736833), and low potassium raises digoxin toxicity risk (general pharmacology guidance). Keep acidic vinegar drinks away from teeth-whitening treatments or acid-sensitive dental work (general guidance).

What the evidence says

Everything measured here is a SURROGATE - post-meal glucose, fasting glucose, HbA1c. There are no trials of vinegar on progression from prediabetes to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney outcomes or death. Acute effect: a meta-analysis of controlled trials found vinegar with a meal reduced postprandial glucose AUC (SMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.08 to -0.11, p=0.01) and insulin AUC (SMD -1.30, -1.98 to -0.62) (PMID 28292654). A 2022 review of 43 papers containing 46 studies (n=913, 44 in the meta-analysis) confirmed an acute glucose reduction in impaired glucose tolerance or type 2 diabetes (SMD -0.53, 95% CI -0.92 to -0.14; n=67) and a borderline effect in healthy volunteers (SMD -0.27, -0.54 to 0.00; n=186), but flagged significant heterogeneity, risk of bias and publication bias - including in the acute vinegar glucose category - and judged the evidence very uncertain (PMID 35388874). Mechanistic crossovers support a muscle-uptake pathway: in 11 adults with type 2 diabetes vinegar increased forearm glucose uptake and lowered plasma glucose, insulin and triglycerides (PMID 26064976), and in 8 adults with impaired glucose tolerance it increased forearm blood flow and muscle glucose uptake and lowered insulin and triglycerides (PMID 25626409). The effect is meal-dependent: in 16 patients with type 2 diabetes (two matched groups of 8) vinegar lowered the glucose incremental AUC after a high-glycaemic-index meal (181+/-78 vs 311+/-124 mmol.min/L, p=0.04) but not after an isocaloric low-glycaemic-index meal (229+/-38 vs 238+/-25, p=0.56) (PMID 20502468). Chronic HbA1c data are sparse, small and inconsistent. In a 12-week three-arm comparison in type 2 diabetes (n=27; the safety report of the same trial is PMID 18361754), HbA1c fell 0.16% with daily vinegar while rising 0.06% with a vinegar pill and 0.22% with dill pickles (p=0.018) (PMID 19269707). An 8-week Iranian trial of 30 mL/day apple cider vinegar reported a significant between-group HbA1c difference without giving the magnitude in its abstract (PMID 38028980). Pooled estimates diverge wildly: 9 apple-cider-vinegar studies gave fasting glucose -7.97 mg/dL (I2 75%) and HbA1c -0.50 (95% CI -0.90 to -0.09; I2 91%; the abstract does not state the unit) with no effect on LDL-C, HDL-C, fasting insulin or HOMA-IR - and the same review reported that in apparently healthy participants apple cider vinegar appeared to INCREASE fasting plasma glucose (PMID 34187442); a larger synthesis of 25 trials (33 arms, 1,320 adults) gave fasting glucose -21.20 mg/dL with I2 95.8% and HbA1c -0.91 with I2 98.9%, and no significant effect on BMI, HOMA-IR, serum insulin, triglycerides, LDL-C or HDL-C (PMID 37608660). Heterogeneity of that magnitude means the pooled glycaemic figures should not be read as a reliable expected effect. In 45 healthy adults at metabolic risk, 8 weeks of red wine vinegar lowered fasting glucose and reduced insulin resistance by 8.3% (versus a 9.7% increase in controls) but did not change body mass index, body weight, waist circumference or visceral fat (PMID 31647087).

Doses studied: Acute trials used about 15-30 mL (1-2 tablespoons) of vinegar containing roughly 5-6% acetic acid, diluted in water and taken immediately before or with a carbohydrate-rich meal (PMIDs 20502468, 25626409, 26064976). Chronic trials used 30 mL/day apple cider vinegar (PMIDs 38028980, 18093343), two tablespoons in a cup of water twice daily (3.6 g acetic acid; PMID 33297831), or 2,800 mg acetic acid/day as vinegar (PMID 18361754). Commercial vinegar pills contain only about 30-45 mg acetic acid and served as near-inert controls (PMIDs 18361754, 33297831).

Why this grade: There is a real, reproducible acute effect: meta-analyses of small crossover trials show vinegar taken with a carbohydrate meal blunts the post-meal glucose rise (SMD -0.60, PMID 28292654; SMD -0.53 in impaired glucose tolerance/type 2 diabetes, PMID 35388874). But the 2022 American Journal of Clinical Nutrition review judged that evidence very uncertain because of heterogeneity, risk of bias and publication bias; the effect disappears with low-glycaemic-index meals (PMID 20502468); and the trials measuring what matters clinically - HbA1c over weeks to months - are few, small and mutually inconsistent, with pooled estimates of -0.50 (I2 91%; PMID 34187442) and -0.91 (I2 98.9%; PMID 37608660) across overlapping literatures, and the single small chronic trial reporting a magnitude finding only -0.16 percentage points (PMID 19269707). One meta-analysis also found fasting glucose appeared to RISE in apparently healthy participants (PMID 34187442). No trial has examined diabetes prevention or any hard outcome. Small, few, preliminary human trials for the meaningful endpoint places this at early.

  1. Vinegar consumption can attenuate postprandial glucose and insulin responses; a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials.
    Meta-analysisShishehbor F et al., 2017 (Diabetes Res Clin Pract) · PMID 28292654 · positive

    Pooled controlled trials: vinegar with a meal reduced postprandial glucose AUC (SMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.08 to -0.11, p=0.01) and insulin AUC (SMD -1.30, 95% CI -1.98 to -0.62). Acute surrogate endpoints only; the authors note prior trials had conflicting results.

  2. The effects of SCFAs on glycemic control in humans: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisCherta-Murillo A et al., 2022 (Am J Clin Nutr) · PMID 35388874 · mixed

    43 papers containing 46 studies (n=913; 44 in the meta-analysis): vinegar lowered the acute glucose response in impaired glucose tolerance/type 2 diabetes (SMD -0.53, 95% CI -0.92 to -0.14; n=67) and marginally in healthy volunteers (SMD -0.27, -0.54 to 0.00; n=186), while acetate, propionate, butyrate and mixed SCFAs had no effect. Significant heterogeneity, risks of bias and publication bias were identified including in the acute vinegar glucose category; the authors state the evidence was very uncertain and urge caution.

  3. The Effects of Apple Cider Vinegar on Cardiometabolic Risk Factors: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Clinical Trials.
    Meta-analysisTehrani SD et al., 2025 (Curr Med Chem) · PMID 37608660 · mixed

    25 clinical trials (33 arms), 1,320 adults: fasting glucose -21.20 mg/dL (95% CI -32.31 to -2.21) with I2 95.8%, HbA1c -0.91 (95% CI -1.62 to -0.21) with I2 98.9%, total cholesterol -6.72 mg/dL. NO significant effect on BMI, HOMA-IR, serum insulin, triglycerides, LDL-C or HDL-C. Heterogeneity approaching 99% means the pooled glycaemic estimates are unstable and cannot be read as a reliable expected effect.

  4. The effect of apple cider vinegar on lipid profiles and glycemic parameters: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials.
    Meta-analysisHadi A et al., 2021 (BMC Complement Med Ther) · PMID 34187442 · mixed

    9 studies (10 arms) of at least 2 weeks: fasting glucose -7.97 mg/dL (95% CI -13.74 to -2.21; I2 75%), HbA1c -0.50 (95% CI -0.90 to -0.09; I2 91%; unit not stated in the abstract), total cholesterol -6.06 mg/dL (-10.95 to -1.17; I2 39%); NO significant effect on LDL-C, HDL-C, fasting insulin or HOMA-IR. The authors also report that in apparently healthy participants apple cider vinegar appeared to INCREASE fasting plasma glucose and HDL-C.

  5. Preliminary evidence that regular vinegar ingestion favorably influences hemoglobin A1c values in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Journal articleJohnston CS et al., 2009 (Diabetes Res Clin Pract) · PMID 19269707 · positive

    Three-arm comparison in type 2 diabetes: HbA1c fell 0.16% with daily vinegar but rose 0.06% with a vinegar pill and 0.22% with dill pickles (p=0.018). The abstract states no sample size or duration; the same three-arm trial is reported at n=27 over 12 weeks in the companion safety paper (PMID 18361754). Explicitly described by its authors as preliminary.

  6. Vinegar reduces postprandial hyperglycaemia in patients with type II diabetes when added to a high, but not to a low, glycaemic index meal.
    Randomised trialLiatis S et al., 2010 (Eur J Clin Nutr) · PMID 20502468 · mixed

    16 patients with type 2 diabetes in two matched groups of 8: vinegar lowered glucose incremental AUC after a high-GI meal (181+/-78 vs 311+/-124 mmol.min/L, p=0.04) but had NO effect after an isocaloric low-GI meal (229+/-38 vs 238+/-25, p=0.56); the insulin effect was only marginal even with the high-GI meal (p=0.056) and absent with the low-GI meal (p=0.98).

  7. Evidence That Daily Vinegar Ingestion May Contribute to Erosive Tooth Wear in Adults.
    Journal articleAnderson S et al., 2020 (J Med Food) · PMID 33297831 · negative

    8-week randomised trial in healthy adults: twice-daily diluted vinegar (two tablespoons in a cup of water; 3.6 g acetic acid) increased blinded basic erosive wear examination scores by 18% (p=.038), while scores did not change in the vinegar-pill control group (0.045 g acetic acid).
