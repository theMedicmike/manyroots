Caffeine

Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

Caffeine is genuinely good at making you feel more awake for a few hours, and there is decent trial evidence for that in shift workers and people short on sleep. What it has never been shown to do is help tiredness that has lasted weeks, because no proper study has tested it for that. And if you already drink it daily, most of what feels like a lift is cancelling out the slump from the last cup rather than adding anything new, while the caffeine still in your system quietly shortens the sleep that recovery depends on.

Safety

A systematic safety review concluded that up to 400 mg/day in healthy adults is generally not associated with overt adverse cardiovascular, behavioural, reproductive, acute or bone effects, with 300 mg/day the corresponding figure in pregnancy and 2.5 mg/kg/day in children and adolescents; 10 g was used as the adult lethality reference. Those conclusions apply to healthy people, the review explicitly noted limited data in those with pre-existing conditions, and most of its author team was drawn from a private toxicology consultancy rather than independent academic groups, so treat the thresholds as a reasonable guide rather than a settled safety ceiling. Regular use produces genuine physical dependence: withdrawal fatigue, low energy, poor concentration and headache have been documented from habitual intakes as low as 100 mg/day, with headache in about half of people, onset at 12-24 hours and symptoms lasting 2-9 days, so cutting down is better done gradually over one to two weeks than stopped abruptly. Because caffeine reduces total sleep time by around 45 minutes and deep sleep by around 11 minutes, using more of it to counter tiredness can deepen the sleep debt that is driving the tiredness.

Who should be careful: Caution or avoidance is sensible for people with anxiety or panic disorder, insomnia, arrhythmia or uncontrolled hypertension; carriers of ADORA2A variants associated with caffeine sensitivity report more anxiety and sleep disturbance at the same dose, and in people who are not habitual consumers a moderate dose has been shown to raise anxiety and jitteriness enough to cancel out its own alerting effect. In pregnancy the safety-review reference intake falls to 300 mg/day and several national bodies advise less. Caffeine is the wrong response to fatigue with red-flag features such as unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, bleeding or new breathlessness, which need medical assessment. If your fatigue reliably worsens for a day or more after exertion, treat caffeine as a way of overriding a warning signal rather than as a treatment, and discuss the pattern with a clinician.

Interactions: Caffeine is metabolized principally by CYP1A2, so prescription medicines that inhibit or induce that enzyme can substantially change how much caffeine is circulating and for how long; check with a pharmacist if you take regular medication. It compounds the heart rate, blood pressure and sleep effects of stimulants such as modafinil and methylphenidate. Its sleep effects persist for many hours after the alerting effect fades, so late-afternoon and evening intake is the most common way caffeine maintains the tiredness it is being used to treat.

What the evidence says

A Cochrane review of 13 randomised trials in people with jet lag or shift work disorder found caffeine reduced errors versus placebo and improved memory (SMD -1.08, 95% CI -2.07 to -0.09) and orientation/attention (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.83 to -0.27), with no benefit for verbal functioning and language (SMD 0.18, 95% CI -0.50 to 0.87), no trial measuring injury outcomes, most participants young and tested under simulated conditions, and a high risk of bias for allocation concealment and selective outcome reporting. No comparable trial has tested caffeine in adults with persistent unexplained fatigue, cancer-related fatigue or ME/CFS. How much of the alerting effect is net gain is contested: in a double-blind trial in 212 medium-high and 157 non-low caffeine consumers, caffeine improved sleepiness, mental alertness and reaction-time and memory performance in habitual consumers but, apart from decreasing sleepiness, had little effect in non/low consumers, where a caffeine-induced increase in anxiety and jitteriness offset the benefit - the authors concluded that tolerance means frequent consumption fails to enhance mental alertness and mental performance. Consistent with that, a critical review of 57 experimental and 9 survey studies validated fatigue, decreased energy/activeness, decreased alertness and drowsiness as caffeine-withdrawal symptoms, with a 50% incidence of headache, clinically significant distress or functional impairment in 13%, onset at 12-24 hours, peak at 20-51 hours and duration of 2-9 days, and a controlled study in 24 habitual coffee drinkers found that missing only the usual morning coffee produced decreased vigor and increased fatigue by midday with no change in psychomotor performance. Caffeine also degrades subsequent sleep: a meta-analysis of 24 studies found total sleep time reduced by 45 minutes, sleep efficiency by 7 percentage points, deep sleep (N3/N4) by 11.4 minutes and wake after sleep onset increased by 12 minutes, with modelled cut-offs of 8.8 hours before bed for a 250 mL coffee and 13.2 hours for a pre-workout serve. Individual response varies genetically, with CYP1A2 variation linked to cognitive effects and ADORA2A variation to anxiety and sleep disturbance across 22 reports.

Doses studied: No established dose for persistent fatigue. Reference intakes from a systematic safety review: up to 400 mg/day in healthy adults, 300 mg/day in pregnancy and 2.5 mg/kg/day in children and adolescents. For scale, a sleep meta-analysis modelled one 250 mL coffee at 107 mg and a standard pre-workout serve at 217.5 mg; the withdrawal-reversal trial used 100 mg followed by 150 mg, and withdrawal symptoms have been produced by abstinence from habitual intakes as low as 100 mg/day.

Why this grade: Randomised evidence shows caffeine improves alertness and cognitive performance under acute sleep loss, jet lag and shift work, but no trial has tested it in adults with persistent fatigue lasting weeks or longer, which is the condition here - the direct evidence is absent rather than merely thin, and the entry is graded early on the strength of adjacent acute-alertness trials only. Against even that, the largest test of the question in habitual consumers found that caffeine improved alertness and performance in regular users but did essentially nothing in non/low consumers, so much of the apparent lift is reversal of overnight withdrawal rather than net gain. Caffeine also measurably shortens and lightens the sleep that recovery from fatigue depends on. Graded early rather than moderate, taking the lower of two defensible grades.

Sources (7)
  1. Caffeine for the prevention of injuries and errors in shift workers (Cochrane review)
    Ker et al., 2010 · PMID 20464765 · positive

    Across 13 randomised trials in people with jet lag or shift work disorder, caffeine significantly reduced errors versus placebo and improved memory (SMD -1.08, 95% CI -2.07 to -0.09) and orientation/attention (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.83 to -0.27), but no trial measured injuries and there was no benefit for verbal functioning and language (SMD 0.18, 95% CI -0.50 to 0.87); one trial found significantly fewer errors with caffeine than with a nap, while other comparisons with naps, bright light and modafinil found no significant differences, and there was a high risk of bias for allocation concealment and selective outcome reporting.

  2. Faster but not smarter: effects of caffeine and caffeine withdrawal on alertness and performance
    Rogers et al., 2013 · PMID 23108937 · negative

    In a double-blind trial in 212 medium-high and 157 non-low caffeine consumers given caffeine (100 mg then 150 mg) or placebo after overnight abstinence, withdrawal produced greater sleepiness, lower mental alertness and poorer simple reaction time, choice reaction time and recognition memory; caffeine improved these measures in medium-high consumers but, apart from decreasing sleepiness, had little effect in non/low consumers because a substantial caffeine-induced increase in anxiety/jitteriness offset the benefit, and the authors concluded that tolerance to caffeine's effects on sleepiness means frequent consumption fails to enhance mental alertness and mental performance.

  3. A critical review of caffeine withdrawal: empirical validation of symptoms and signs, incidence, severity, and associated features
    Juliano and Griffiths, 2004 · PMID 15448977 · negative

    Reviewing 57 experimental and 9 survey studies, the authors validated fatigue, decreased energy/activeness, decreased alertness, drowsiness, difficulty concentrating and headache as caffeine-withdrawal symptoms; headache incidence was 50% and clinically significant distress or functional impairment 13%, onset was 12-24 hours after abstinence with peak at 20-51 hours and duration of 2-9 days, and symptoms occurred from habitual doses as low as 100 mg/day.

  4. Effects of brief caffeinated-beverage deprivation on mood, symptoms, and psychomotor performance
    Lane, 1997 · PMID 9264092 · negative

    In 24 habitual coffee drinkers tested at midday under counterbalanced ad libitum and abstinent conditions, deprivation equivalent to missing the usual morning coffee produced decreased vigor, increased fatigue and headache by the middle of the day, with no change in psychomotor performance.

  5. The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: a systematic review and meta-analysis
    Gardiner et al., 2023 · PMID 36870101 · negative

    Across 24 studies, caffeine reduced total sleep time by 45 minutes and sleep efficiency by 7 percentage points, increased sleep onset latency by 9 minutes and wake after sleep onset by 12 minutes, increased light sleep (N1) by 6.1 minutes and reduced deep sleep (N3 and N4) by 11.4 minutes; to avoid reductions in total sleep time, a 250 mL coffee (107 mg) should be consumed at least 8.8 hours and a 217.5 mg pre-workout serve at least 13.2 hours before bedtime.

  6. Systematic review of the potential adverse effects of caffeine consumption in healthy adults, pregnant women, adolescents, and children
    Wikoff et al., 2017 · PMID 28438661 · positive

    Screening over 5,000 articles and including 381, the review concluded that intake of up to 400 mg caffeine per day in healthy adults is generally not associated with overt adverse cardiovascular, behavioural, reproductive/developmental, acute or bone effects, with corresponding figures of 300 mg/day in healthy pregnant women and 2.5 mg/kg body weight/day in children and adolescents, and flagged that data are limited for people with pre-existing conditions; note that 8 of the 15 listed authors give affiliations at ToxStrategies, a private toxicology consultancy, so the review is not an independent academic assessment.

  7. Genetics of caffeine and brain-related outcomes: a systematic review of observational studies and randomized trials
    Kapellou et al., 2023 · PMID 37029915 · mixed

    Across 22 records (15 randomised controlled trials, 6 cross-sectional studies and 1 genome-wide association study) in healthy adults, CYP1A2 polymorphisms were associated with cognitive performance while ADORA2A variation was associated with anxiety and sleep disturbance, indicating that response to the same caffeine dose varies substantially between individuals.
