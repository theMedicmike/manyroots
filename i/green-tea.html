Green tea

Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure Early evidence · for anxiety & stress

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For high blood pressure

Green tea has been studied a lot for blood pressure, and pooling all the trials together does show a drop, but it is on the order of one to two points, which is small enough that you would not notice it. Drinking green tea is a fine habit; concentrated green tea extract supplements are a different matter, and carry a real risk of liver injury that plain brewed tea does not. One thing to raise with your pharmacist: green tea has been shown to cut the absorption of at least one blood pressure medicine, nadolol, by about 85 percent, and to blunt how well it works.

Safety

Brewed green tea as a beverage has a long record of safe consumption. Concentrated green tea EXTRACT is a different matter: the US Pharmacopeia's safety review documented a hepatocellular pattern of liver injury, with case reports linking hepatotoxicity to EGCG intakes ranging from 140 mg to about 1000 mg per day and marked variation between individuals in susceptibility. Taking repeated bolus doses on an empty stomach substantially increases catechin absorption and appears to raise risk, so extracts should be taken with food if used at all. The Cochrane review additionally listed gastrointestinal disorders, elevated liver enzymes, insomnia, raised blood pressure and skin reactions among adverse effects of green tea extract. Anyone taking a green tea extract who develops abdominal pain, dark urine, unusual fatigue or yellowing of the skin or eyes should stop it and seek medical attention promptly. Caffeine content in both tea and extracts can acutely raise blood pressure and disturb sleep.

Who should be careful: Do not use concentrated green tea extract if you have liver disease or a history of drug-induced liver injury. Avoid extracts in pregnancy and breastfeeding: the hepatotoxicity signal and the caffeine load are both reasons for caution, and adequate safety data in pregnancy are lacking (a reduction in folate absorption is also commonly cited, though that specific claim is less well established). Caffeine-sensitive individuals and people with anxiety or insomnia should be cautious. Do not take extracts on an empty stomach. Extracts are not appropriate for children.

Interactions: The most important interaction for anyone managing blood pressure: green tea markedly reduces absorption of the beta-blocker nadolol. In a human crossover study, 700 mL/day of green tea for 14 days cut nadolol peak concentration by 85.3% and total exposure by 85.0%, and significantly reduced nadolol's effect on systolic blood pressure, apparently by inhibiting the intestinal transporter OATP1A2. If you take nadolol or a related beta-blocker, discuss green tea with your pharmacist before drinking it regularly. Green tea also reduces the absorption of non-haem iron, so separate it from iron supplements and iron-rich meals. Caffeine adds to the effect of other stimulants and can counteract the intended blood pressure reduction. Interactions with warfarin (often attributed to vitamin K content, though the evidence for brewed green tea specifically is weak), with some statins, and with the chemotherapy agent bortezomib have been reported. Anyone on prescription medication should check with a pharmacist before taking green tea extract.

What the evidence says

The most recent and rigorous synthesis, a GRADE-assessed dose-response meta-analysis of 36 RCTs, found green tea reduced systolic pressure by just 1.08 mmHg (95% CI -1.98 to -0.18, I-squared 85%) and diastolic by 1.09 mmHg (95% CI -1.67 to -0.50, I-squared 74%), with no clear association between blood pressure change and either dose or duration, leading the authors to conclude green tea is better considered a complementary approach. Two independent earlier meta-analyses landed in the same trivial range: 13 studies giving systolic -2.08 mmHg (95% CI -3.06 to -1.05) and diastolic -1.71 mmHg (95% CI -2.86 to -0.56), and a separate 13-trial pool of 1367 subjects giving systolic -1.98 mmHg (95% CI -2.94 to -1.01) and diastolic -1.92 mmHg (95% CI -3.17 to -0.68). Importantly, a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs in 613 overweight and obese participants found green tea catechin reduced waist circumference and triglycerides and raised HDL cholesterol but showed NO significant effect on blood pressure. Individual trials in specifically hypertensive populations have been more favourable, with 379 mg green tea extract daily for 3 months in 56 obese hypertensive patients significantly lowering both systolic and diastolic pressure versus placebo (P<0.01), though effect sizes in mmHg were not reported. Counterbalancing all of this, the Cochrane review of green tea listed raised blood pressure among the adverse effects associated with green tea extract intake alongside elevated liver enzymes, and a human crossover study found green tea reduced plasma concentrations of the antihypertensive nadolol by about 85% and significantly reduced its effect on systolic blood pressure. All efficacy outcomes are surrogate blood pressure measurements rather than cardiovascular events.

Doses studied: 379 mg/day green tea extract for 3 months in obese hypertensive patients; 700 mL/day brewed green tea for 14 days in the nadolol interaction study. Subgroup analysis in the 36-RCT meta-analysis found stronger effects at intakes under 500 mg/day, in studies of 8 weeks or less, in participants with baseline systolic 120 mmHg or above, in Asian populations and in women, but no clear dose-response overall. Adverse liver events have been associated with EGCG intakes from 140 mg to about 1000 mg/day.

Why this grade: A large evidence base of 36 RCTs pooled in a GRADE-assessed dose-response meta-analysis shows a statistically significant but clinically trivial effect of about 1 mmHg systolic, with very high heterogeneity (I-squared 85%) and no clear dose-response relationship; an independent 13-RCT meta-analysis found about 2 mmHg. This is the weakest 'moderate' in this node: the direction is consistent enough to exceed 'early', which would misdescribe 36 randomised trials as preliminary, and the pooled confidence interval excludes zero so 'not_supported' does not apply, but the magnitude is roughly a quarter of garlic's and sits at or below the threshold of clinical meaningfulness. The null result in overweight and obese participants, and the fact that green tea EXTRACT carries raised blood pressure among its documented adverse effects, both argue against reading this grade generously.

  1. Effect of green tea supplementation on blood pressure in adults: a GRADE-assessed systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisRezaei M, Akhavan N, Fathi F, Alavi SM, Fadaii M, Dehzad MJ, Askarpour M, 2025 · PMID 40497293 · mixed

    Meta-analysis of 36 RCTs found green tea reduced systolic pressure by only 1.08 mmHg (95% CI -1.98 to -0.18, I-squared 85.0%) and diastolic by 1.09 mmHg (95% CI -1.67 to -0.50, I-squared 74.0%). Subgroup analyses showed stronger effects in participants with baseline systolic BP >=120 mmHg, intakes under 500 mg/day, studies of 8 weeks or less, Asian populations and women, but NO clear association was found between blood pressure changes and either tea dose or intervention duration. Authors concluded that given the heterogeneity and lack of clear dose-duration effects, green tea is better considered a complementary approach for managing blood pressure.

  2. Green tea ingestion greatly reduces plasma concentrations of nadolol in healthy subjects.
    Randomised trialMisaka S, Yatabe J, Muller F, Takano K, Kawabe K, Glaeser H et al., 2014 · PMID 24419562 · negative

    Ten healthy volunteers took a single 30 mg oral dose of the beta-blocker nadolol with green tea or water after 14 days of green tea (700 mL/day) or water. Green tea markedly decreased nadolol peak plasma concentration by 85.3% and area under the curve by 85.0% (both P<0.01) without altering renal clearance, and the effects of nadolol on SYSTOLIC BLOOD PRESSURE were significantly reduced by green tea. Mechanistic work showed nadolol is a substrate of the intestinal transporter OATP1A2 and that green tea inhibits OATP1A2-mediated uptake. This documents green tea actively working against blood pressure control in people taking this medication.

  3. Effects of green tea catechin on the blood pressure and lipids in overweight and obese population-a meta-analysis.
    Journal articleWang Y, Xia H, Yu J, Sui J, Pan D, Wang S et al., 2023 · PMID 38034724 · null

    Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs in 613 overweight and obese participants found green tea catechin reduced waist circumference by 1.37 cm (95% CI -2.52 to -0.22) and triglycerides by 0.18 mmol/L (95% CI -0.35 to -0.02), and raised HDL cholesterol by 0.07 mmol/L (95% CI 0.01 to 0.14). However it did NOT show a significant effect on blood pressure in this population.

  4. Effect of green tea consumption on blood pressure: a meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisPeng X, Zhou R, Wang B, Yu X, Yang X, Liu K, Mi M, 2014 · PMID 25176280 · positive

    Independent meta-analysis of 13 trials comprising 1367 subjects found green tea decreased systolic blood pressure by 1.98 mmHg (95% CI -2.94 to -1.01, P<0.001) and diastolic by 1.92 mmHg (95% CI -3.17 to -0.68, P=0.002). Subgroup analyses found the positive effect appeared only with low-dose green tea polyphenols, longer intervention durations, or after ruling out the confounding effect of caffeine. Confirms independently that the true effect sits in the 1-2 mmHg range rather than higher.

  5. Green tea catechins and blood pressure: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisKhalesi S, Sun J, Buys N, Jamshidi A, Nikbakht-Nasrabadi E, Khosravi-Boroujeni H, 2014 · PMID 24861099 · positive

    Meta-analysis of 13 studies found green tea consumption significantly changed systolic blood pressure by -2.08 mmHg (95% CI -3.06 to -1.05) and diastolic by -1.71 mmHg (95% CI -2.86 to -0.56) versus control. Subgroup analysis suggested greater reduction in studies including participants with baseline mean systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg or above, and in studies using green tea as an extract rather than a beverage. Changes in blood glucose and BMI did not reach statistical significance.

  6. Green tea extract reduces blood pressure, inflammatory biomarkers, and oxidative stress and improves parameters associated with insulin resistance in obese, hypertensive patients.
    Randomised trialBogdanski P, Suliburska J, Szulinska M, Stepien M, Pupek-Musialik D, Jablecka A, 2012 · PMID 22749178 · positive

    Double-blind placebo-controlled trial randomised 56 obese hypertensive subjects to 379 mg green tea extract daily or placebo for 3 months. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressure decreased significantly in the green tea group compared with placebo (P<.01), alongside reductions in fasting glucose, insulin, insulin resistance, TNF-alpha and C-reactive protein. Effect sizes in mmHg were NOT reported in the abstract, so the magnitude of the blood pressure change cannot be assessed from this trial.

  7. Green tea (Camellia sinensis) for the prevention of cancer.
    Meta-analysisFilippini T, Malavolti M, Borrelli F, Izzo AA, Fairweather-Tait SJ, Horneber M, Vinceti M (Cochrane), 2020 · PMID 32118296 · negative

    Cochrane review including 11 experimental studies allocating 1795 participants to green tea extract or placebo reported adverse effects of green tea extract intake including gastrointestinal disorders, elevation of liver enzymes, and more rarely insomnia, RAISED BLOOD PRESSURE and skin or subcutaneous reactions. This documents that concentrated green tea extract can act in the opposite direction on blood pressure in some individuals.

  8. United States Pharmacopeia (USP) comprehensive review of the hepatotoxicity of green tea extracts.
    Narrative reviewOketch-Rabah HA, Roe AL, Rider CV, Bonkovsky HL, Giancaspro GI, Navarro V et al., 2020 · PMID 32140423 · negative

    USP systematic safety review found toxicological studies show a hepatocellular pattern of liver injury from green tea extracts, with published adverse event case reports associating hepatotoxicity with EGCG intakes from 140 mg to approximately 1000 mg/day and substantial inter-individual variability in susceptibility, possibly genetic. Repeated oral bolus doses taken while fasting significantly increase catechin bioavailability. No evidence implicated solvent residues, pesticide residues, pyrrolizidine alkaloids or elemental impurities. USP added a cautionary labelling requirement to its Powdered Decaffeinated Green Tea Extract monograph.

For anxiety & stress

Green tea, and especially matcha, contains a calming amino acid called L-theanine alongside a modest amount of caffeine, and one small study found people given a particular matcha felt less anxious than those given a placebo version. But the more rigorous tests are not encouraging: when researchers gave L-theanine on its own to people with diagnosed anxiety, it did not beat a placebo. Treat green tea as a pleasant, low-risk habit that may take the edge off a stressful day, not as a proven remedy.

Safety

As a beverage in normal amounts (roughly 1-5 cups/day), green tea is well tolerated by most adults and has centuries of routine dietary use. The 4-week matcha RCT of 2.7 g/day reported no notable adverse effects, and the network meta-analysis found all reviewed medicinal herbs, L-theanine included, were well tolerated with a good safety profile compared with control groups.

Who should be careful: Green tea contains caffeine (about 71 mg in the 2.7 g/day matcha dose studied), which can worsen jitteriness, restlessness and insomnia in caffeine-sensitive people, the opposite of the intended effect. The panic-inducing effect of caffeine is documented at high doses (above roughly 400 mg); a randomized crossover trial found 150 mg did not raise subjective anxiety even in people with panic disorder, so ordinary tea drinking is not an established panic trigger, but people with panic-spectrum symptoms, significant insomnia, or uncontrolled heart-rhythm problems should still use caffeinated tea cautiously and avoid it later in the day. Pregnant people should keep total daily caffeine within the limit their clinician recommends.

Interactions: Caffeine in green tea adds to the effects of other caffeine sources and stimulants. Tea polyphenols can reduce absorption of non-heme (plant-based) iron when drunk with meals, which matters for people with iron-deficiency anemia; separating tea from meals reduces this. Note that concentrated green tea extract supplements are a different product from brewed tea or matcha: high-dose extracts have been associated with rare liver injury, and this entry covers the beverage, not extracts.

What the evidence says

Direct trial evidence for green tea as a food is limited to small studies: in a placebo-controlled trial in healthy volunteers (n=39), anxiety was significantly lower in the test-matcha group than the placebo-matcha group, an effect observed only when the molar ratio of caffeine plus EGCG to theanine plus arginine was below 2 (Unno 2018). A 4-week double-blind RCT of 2.7 g/day matcha (50.3 mg theanine, 301.4 mg catechins, 71.5 mg caffeine) in adults aged 27-64 found no significant differences versus control in objective EEG sleep parameters and only a non-significant trend on Beck Depression Inventory-II scores (p<0.1), alongside a significant shortening of EEG-measured wake-up time (p<0.05); critically, this trial reported no anxiety outcome at all (Baba 2024). Evidence for green tea's key amino acid L-theanine is null where it has been tested most rigorously in clinical anxiety: an 8-week double-blind RCT of adjunctive L-theanine 450-900 mg/day in 46 patients with DSM-5 generalized anxiety disorder found no advantage over placebo on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (p=0.73) or the Insomnia Severity Index (p=0.35) (Sarris 2019), and a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 herb trials found L-theanine did not outperform placebo (MD -0.49, 95% CrI -6.54 to 5.57) (Zhang 2022). Cutting the other way, a systematic review of 9 randomized trials concluded that 200-400 mg/day of supplemental L-theanine may assist in reducing stress and anxiety in people exposed to stressful conditions, though it pooled no effect estimate and called for longer, larger studies (Williams 2020). The strongest population-level signal is cross-sectional: among 42,093 Japanese adults aged 40 and over, drinking 5 or more cups of green tea daily was associated with lower odds of psychological distress (Kessler-6 >=13) than under 1 cup/day (adjusted OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.70-0.91), a design that cannot establish causation (Hozawa 2009).

Doses studied: Matcha 2-2.7 g/day (roughly one teaspoon of powder) for 2-4 weeks in randomized trials; the observational association was seen at 5 or more cups of brewed green tea per day; isolated L-theanine supplement trials used 200-900 mg/day, with the systematic review pointing to 200-400 mg/day.

Why this grade: Human trials of green tea or matcha itself for anxiety are few and small: the key placebo-controlled matcha trial enrolled 39 participants, and the only other matcha RCT retrieved (4 weeks, n not anxiety-assessed) reported no anxiety endpoint whatsoever, finding null results on objective EEG sleep measures with only a non-significant trend on depression scores. Evidence for green tea's putative calming constituent, L-theanine, is mixed and null where tested most rigorously: an 8-week double-blind RCT in generalized anxiety disorder found no benefit over placebo, and a 2022 Bayesian network meta-analysis found L-theanine did not outperform placebo, while a systematic review of 9 RCTs suggests supplemental doses may help healthy people under acute stress. The large supportive dataset is cross-sectional and cannot establish causation. Small, few, preliminary human trials plus null findings in the rigorous clinical tests places this at 'early', graded conservatively; 'moderate' is not available because no meta-analysis supports the food itself.

  1. Stress-Reducing Function of Matcha Green Tea in Animal Experiments and Clinical Trials.
    Randomised trialUnno et al., 2018 · PMID 30308973 · positive

  2. Matcha Does Not Affect Electroencephalography during Sleep but May Enhance Mental Well-Being: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Clinical Trial.
    Randomised trialBaba et al., 2024 · PMID 39275223 · mixed

    Four weeks of 2.7 g/day matcha (50.3 mg theanine, 301.4 mg catechins, 71.5 mg caffeine) in healthy Japanese adults aged 27-64 produced no significant differences versus control in objective EEG sleep measures (total sleep time, sleep latency, wake after sleep onset, sleep efficiency); Beck Depression Inventory-II scores and satisfaction with sleep time only tended to improve (p<0.1), while EEG-measured wake-up time after waking was significantly shortened (p<0.05). No anxiety outcome was assessed in this trial.

  3. L-theanine in the adjunctive treatment of generalized anxiety disorder: A double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial.
    Randomised trialSarris et al., 2019 · PMID 30580081 · null

    In 46 participants with DSM-5 generalized anxiety disorder, 8 weeks of adjunctive L-theanine 450-900 mg/day did not outperform placebo for anxiety reduction on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (p=0.73) or insomnia severity on the Insomnia Severity Index (p=0.35); only self-reported sleep satisfaction (ISI item 4, p=0.015) and a subgroup with non-clinical insomnia symptoms (ISI <=14, p=0.007) separated from placebo, and no significant cognitive effects were found.

  4. Medicinal herbs for the treatment of anxiety: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisZhang et al., 2022 · PMID 35378276 · null

    In a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 randomized trials comparing 12 medicinal herbs for anxiety, L-theanine did not outperform placebo on endpoint Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores in terms of statistical certainty (MD -0.49, 95% CrI -6.54 to 5.57).

  5. The Effects of Green Tea Amino Acid L-Theanine Consumption on the Ability to Manage Stress and Anxiety Levels: a Systematic Review.
    Systematic reviewWilliams et al., 2020 · PMID 31758301 · positive

    Systematic review of 9 peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials of orally supplemented pure L-theanine versus control concluded that 200-400 mg/day may assist in the reduction of stress and anxiety in people exposed to stressful conditions; no pooled effect estimate was calculated, and the authors state that longer-term, larger-cohort studies are needed before L-theanine can be clinically justified for this use.

  6. Acute effects of 150 mg caffeine on subjective, physiological, and behavioral components of anxiety in panic disorder and healthy controls - A randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial.
    Randomised trialHoppe et al., 2025 · PMID 40577029 · null

    Safety evidence on green tea's caffeine content: in a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial in 29 patients with panic disorder and 53 healthy controls, 150 mg caffeine did not increase subjective anxiety or interoceptive attention in either group, though it did increase skin conductance responses and avoidance behavior in both; the authors note panic induction is documented at doses above 400 mg and recommend that caffeine-abstinence advice for panic disorder be based on higher doses.
