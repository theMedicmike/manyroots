Conventional medicine

Trazodone

Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

ImportantIf trazodone was prescribed for you, don't stop it or change the dose because of this grade. It should be started and stopped under a prescriber's direction, not adjusted on your own.

Trazodone is an older antidepressant that doctors very often prescribe at low doses for sleep, even though it has never been approved for insomnia. When researchers pool the trials, it appears to improve how good people rate their sleep and adds some measured sleep time on a sleep study, but it did not increase how much sleep people felt they got, and next-day grogginess was more common than on placebo. The main US sleep-medicine guideline actually recommends against using it for insomnia, so its popularity is running well ahead of what has been shown.

Safety

Not a controlled substance and no dependence liability, which is a large part of its clinical appeal. The consistent harms in trials are next-day drowsiness (roughly 2.5x placebo), dizziness, dry mouth, blurred vision, and appetite change; discontinuation for adverse effects was about 2.3x placebo. Orthostatic hypotension is a meaningful falls risk in older adults. Priapism is rare but a urological emergency requiring immediate care. Trazodone is an antidepressant and carries the class warning about emergent suicidal thinking in young adults. It should be started and stopped under a prescriber's direction, not adjusted independently.

Who should be careful: Concurrent use with, or within 14 days of, an MAOI. Caution with known QT prolongation, recent myocardial infarction, significant cardiac arrhythmia, or a history of priapism. Caution in older adults and in anyone at risk of falls.

Interactions: Additive sedation and impairment with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, gabapentinoids and sedating antihistamines. Serotonin syndrome risk when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans, tramadol, linezolid or St John's wort. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. ketoconazole, ritonavir, clarithromycin) raise trazodone levels; CYP3A4 inducers (e.g. carbamazepine) lower them. Additive QT prolongation with other QT-prolonging drugs; additive blood-pressure lowering with antihypertensives.

What the evidence says

Trazodone is one of the most-prescribed off-label sleep medicines in the US, but the trial base is weaker than the prescribing volume implies. The largest synthesis to date (44 RCTs, 3,935 participants) found no effect on subjective total sleep time (WMD 0.73 min, 95% CI -24.62 to 26.07, p=0.96) while showing improvement in subjective sleep quality (SMD -0.58, 95% CI -0.87 to -0.28), fewer nocturnal awakenings (SMD -0.57), wake after sleep onset -13.47 min, and polysomnographic total sleep time +27.98 min (95% CI 4.02 to 51.95). A polysomnography-only meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (466 participants) reported total sleep time +39.88 min (95% CI 14.44 to 65.32) and latency to persistent sleep -19.30 min, but graded the total-sleep-time evidence very low certainty. Cochrane pooled three trazodone trials (370 participants) and found a modest subjective benefit (SMD -0.34, 95% CI -0.66 to -0.02) with no polysomnographic sleep-efficiency difference (MD 1.38 percentage points, 95% CI -2.87 to 5.63), rating the evidence low quality. Harms are consistent across syntheses: dropouts due to adverse effects 2.3x placebo (RR 2.30, 95% CI 1.45 to 3.64), sleep-related adverse effects RR 4.31, and daytime drowsiness OR 2.53. On this basis the AASM issued a recommendation against trazodone for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia, and the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 170 trials (47,950 participants) grouped it with licensed drugs that can work acutely but carry poor tolerability or unavailable long-term data. Two counterweights belong on the record. The 2023 European Insomnia Guideline permits low-dose sedating antidepressants as a class for short-term insomnia treatment (level B), though it does not name trazodone individually and places CBT-I first. And outside insomnia disorder, a small randomised trial in Alzheimer's disease (30 patients, trazodone 50 mg for two weeks) found 42.5 more minutes of nightly sleep by actigraphy without daytime sleepiness - a positive result, but in a dementia population rather than in adults with insomnia.

Doses studied: Off-label bedtime dosing well below antidepressant dosing. Trials pooled in these syntheses varied in dose, with a minimum treatment duration of 5 days in the largest meta-analysis and at least 1 week in the polysomnographic meta-analysis; neither synthesis established a single standard dose. The best-characterised single dose in a randomised trial is 50 mg nightly for two weeks, but that trial was in Alzheimer's disease, not insomnia disorder. Trazodone has no FDA approval for insomnia at any dose, so no regulator-established insomnia dose exists.

Why this grade: Retained at not_supported after adversarial review. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline explicitly suggests clinicians NOT use trazodone for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia, and the largest synthesis (44 RCTs, 3,935 participants) found no effect on subjective total sleep time (WMD 0.73 min, 95% CI -24.62 to 26.07) alongside 2.3x placebo dropout for adverse effects. Objective sleep-continuity signals do exist (polysomnographic total sleep time +28 to +40 min), Cochrane found a modest subjective benefit, and the 2023 European Insomnia Guideline does permit low-dose sedating antidepressants as a class for short-term use at level B without naming trazodone individually. That countervailing evidence is real and is why this is a close call. It does not overturn the grade: the only guideline that assesses trazodone by name recommends against it, Cochrane rated the trazodone evidence low quality, and the polysomnography meta-analysis graded its own total-sleep-time result very low certainty. Heavy real-world off-label prescribing runs ahead of the evidence, which is exactly the gap this grade is meant to show.

Sources (7)
  1. Effects of Trazodone on Sleep: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisKokkali et al., 2024 · PMID 39123094 · mixed

    44 RCTs, 3,935 participants: trazodone did NOT change subjective total sleep time (WMD 0.73 min, 95% CI -24.62 to 26.07, p=0.96) but improved sleep quality (SMD -0.58, 95% CI -0.87 to -0.28), number of nocturnal awakenings (SMD -0.57), wake after sleep onset (WMD -13.47 min) and polysomnographic total sleep time (WMD +27.98 min, 95% CI 4.02 to 51.95); dropouts due to adverse effects were 2.3x placebo (RR 2.30, 95% CI 1.45 to 3.64) and sleep-related adverse effects RR 4.31 (95% CI 2.29 to 8.13). Authors concluded trazodone 'extends total sleep time but does not affect perceived sleep duration'.

  2. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    GuidelineSateia et al., 2017 · PMID 27998379 · negative

    GRADE-based guideline covering individual insomnia drugs: 'We suggest that clinicians not use trazodone as a treatment for sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults. (WEAK)' - a recommendation against, unlike the recommendations for doxepin and ramelteon in the same document.

  3. Antidepressants for insomnia in adults.
    Meta-analysisEveritt et al., 2018 · PMID 29761479 · mixed

    Cochrane review, 23 RCTs (2,806 participants). Three trazodone trials (370 participants) pooled to a modest subjective improvement (SMD -0.34, 95% CI -0.66 to -0.02); two polysomnographic trials (169 participants) showed little or no sleep-efficiency difference (MD 1.38 percentage points, 95% CI -2.87 to 5.63). Evidence rated low quality, with low-quality evidence of more adverse effects (morning grogginess, dry mouth, thirst).

  4. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisDe Crescenzo et al., 2022 · PMID 35843245 · mixed

    170 trials, 36 interventions, 47,950 participants. Trazodone was named among licensed drugs that 'can be effective in the acute treatment of insomnia but are associated with poor tolerability, or information about long-term effects is not available'; it was not among the agents (benzodiazepines, doxylamine, eszopiclone, lemborexant, seltorexant, zolpidem, zopiclone) shown more efficacious than placebo with high-to-moderate certainty (SMD range 0.36-0.83).

  5. Trazodone changed the polysomnographic sleep architecture in insomnia disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisZheng et al., 2022 · PMID 36002579 · positive

    11 RCTs, 466 participants with insomnia disorder: total sleep time +39.88 min (95% CI 14.44 to 65.32, p=0.002), latency to persistent sleep -19.30 min (p=0.04), fewer awakenings (SMD -0.67); but daytime drowsiness (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.14-5.64) and decreased appetite (OR 2.81, 95% CI 1.14-6.92) were more frequent, and GRADE certainty was very low for total sleep time and low for latency and wake after sleep onset.

  6. The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.
    GuidelineRiemann et al., 2023 · PMID 38016484 · mixed

    Current European guideline. Cognitive-behavioural therapy for insomnia is first-line for chronic insomnia in adults of any age (A); when it is not sufficiently effective, 'low-dose sedating antidepressants (B) can be used for the short-term treatment of insomnia (<= 4 weeks)', with longer-term treatment initiated in some cases (B). Trazodone is not named individually in the summary recommendations, so this class-level permission sits alongside, and partly against, the AASM's drug-specific recommendation not to use trazodone.

  7. Trazodone improves sleep parameters in Alzheimer disease patients: a randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled study.
    Randomised trialCamargos et al., 2014 · PMID 24495406 · positive

    30 patients with probable Alzheimer's disease and sleep disturbance (15 trazodone 50 mg at 10 PM, 15 placebo) for two weeks: trazodone users slept 42.5 more minutes per night and increased nighttime percent sleep by 8.5 percentage points on actigraphy, with no significant daytime sleepiness or napping, no effect on cognition or function, and no difference in adverse events. Population is dementia with sleep disturbance, NOT adults with insomnia disorder.
