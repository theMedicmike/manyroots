Potassium (potassium-enriched salt substitutes and potassium supplements)

Strong evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Swapping regular table salt for a potassium-enriched salt substitute is one of the few things on a supplement shelf that has actually been shown to prevent strokes and deaths, not just to move a number on a blood pressure cuff. It works best if you eat a lot of salt and your blood pressure is already high; in people whose pressure is normal it barely moves at all, and potassium taken as capsules has a much weaker track record than the salt swap. The one real catch is your kidneys - if they do not work well, or you take certain blood pressure or heart medicines, extra potassium can build up dangerously, so ask your doctor before you start.

Safety

In the SSaSS trial, serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalaemia were not significantly more common with the salt substitute (3.35 vs 3.30 events per 1000 person-years; RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.80-1.37), and the Cochrane review found blood potassium rose only slightly (0.12 mmol/L) with little to no difference in hyperkalaemia. That reassurance has a hard boundary: all 26 trials in the Cochrane review specifically excluded people in whom increased potassium is known to be potentially harmful, so the safety of potassium loading has essentially not been tested in the people most at risk from it. A 2020 dose-response analysis also found blood pressure rising again at very high potassium intakes in drug-treated hypertensive participants, so more is not better.

Who should be careful: Do not use potassium supplements or potassium-enriched salt substitutes without medical supervision if you have chronic kidney disease, reduced kidney function, kidney failure or are on dialysis, adrenal insufficiency, or any condition that impairs potassium excretion. Salt substitutes look like ordinary table salt, so household use needs a deliberate check that nobody in the home falls into these groups.

Interactions: Potassium loading is risky alongside medicines that retain potassium: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, triamterene), and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Taking a separate potassium supplement on top of a salt substitute compounds the load, as do potassium-based food additives in people with reduced kidney function. Anyone on these drugs should have potassium checked by a clinician rather than self-supplementing.

What the evidence says

Evidence for potassium in hypertension is unusually deep because it includes event outcomes, not only blood pressure - but that depth belongs to salt substitution rather than to potassium capsules. In the open-label cluster-randomised SSaSS trial (20,995 rural Chinese adults with prior stroke or aged 60 or over with high blood pressure; mean follow-up 4.74 years), a 75% NaCl/25% KCl salt substitute reduced stroke (29.14 vs 33.65 events per 1000 person-years; RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.96), major cardiovascular events (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80-0.94) and all-cause death (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82-0.95). A 2022 Cochrane review of 26 RCTs found low-sodium salt substitutes probably reduce systolic pressure by 4.76 mmHg (95% CI -6.01 to -3.50) and diastolic by 2.43 mmHg (95% CI -3.50 to -1.36), with moderate-certainty reductions in non-fatal acute coronary syndrome and cardiovascular mortality and a small rise in blood potassium of 0.12 mmol/L. Potassium given as a supplement is a weaker story. The Cochrane review restricted to potassium supplements in raised blood pressure pooled 5 trials (425 participants) and found no statistically significant change (systolic -11.2 mmHg, 95% CI -25.2 to 2.7; diastolic -5.0, 95% CI -12.5 to 2.4), shrinking to -3.9 mmHg systolic (95% CI -8.6 to 0.8) once a single trial with very high baseline pressure was removed; a later meta-analysis of 23 placebo-controlled trials reported 4.25/2.53 mmHg. The effect is strongly conditional on starting blood pressure: a 2025 dose-response meta-analysis estimated 5.3/3.62 mmHg per 50 mmol/day increase in urinary potassium in people with hypertension but only 0.5/0.12 mmHg in people without it, and in the Trials of Hypertension Prevention Phase 1 (2182 adults with diastolic pressure 80-89 mmHg) potassium supplementation produced no significant blood pressure change at 6 or 18 months while weight loss and sodium reduction did. A 2020 dose-response meta-analysis of 32 trials found a U-shaped curve, with the benefit weakening above a 30 mmol/day difference in excretion and pressure rising again at differences around 80 mmol/day, particularly in drug-treated hypertensive participants.

Doses studied: Salt-substitute trials replaced 25% of sodium chloride with potassium chloride by mass (SSaSS), with substitution across the 26 Cochrane-reviewed trials ranging from about 3% to 77%. Supplement trials used roughly 30-140 mmol/day of potassium for at least 4 weeks; dose-response modelling suggests benefit concentrates around a 30-50 mmol/day increase in intake and diminishes or reverses at much higher intakes.

Why this grade: The strong grade attaches to one specific practice: replacing sodium chloride with potassium-enriched salt in a high-sodium diet. That practice is the only intervention in this node with hard cardiovascular outcome data from a large randomised trial. SSaSS (20,995 people, 600 villages, mean 4.74 years) found lower rates of stroke, major cardiovascular events and death with a 25% potassium chloride salt substitute, and a 2022 Cochrane review of 26 RCTs (34,961 adults) rated the blood-pressure and cardiovascular-mortality benefits moderate-certainty. The grade does NOT extend to potassium taken as capsules: the Cochrane review dedicated to potassium supplements in primary hypertension pooled 5 trials and found no statistically significant blood-pressure effect, and although a later 23-trial meta-analysis reported about 4 mmHg, a 2020 dose-response analysis found a U-shaped curve in which pressure rises again at high intakes. Two further scope limits: the effect is strongly conditional on already having high blood pressure, being close to zero in people with normal pressure; and every trial excluded people at risk of hyperkalaemia, so the grade says nothing about them. The grade rests on trial evidence rather than guideline adoption - a 2024 review of 32 hypertension and 14 kidney guidelines found recommendations on salt substitutes incomplete and inconsistent.

Sources (8)
  1. Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death.
    Randomised trialNeal et al., 2021 (SSaSS) · PMID 34459569 · positive

    In 20,995 adults over a mean 4.74 years, a 75% NaCl/25% KCl salt substitute reduced stroke (29.14 vs 33.65 per 1000 person-years; RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.96), major cardiovascular events (49.09 vs 56.29 per 1000 person-years; RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80-0.94) and all-cause death (39.28 vs 44.61 per 1000 person-years; RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82-0.95); serious adverse events attributed to hyperkalaemia did not differ significantly (3.35 vs 3.30 per 1000 person-years; RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.80-1.37).

  2. Replacing salt with low-sodium salt substitutes (LSSS) for cardiovascular health in adults, children and pregnant women.
    Systematic reviewBrand et al., 2022 (Cochrane) · PMID 35944931 · positive

    Across 26 RCTs randomising 34,961 adults and 92 children, salt substitutes probably reduced systolic pressure by 4.76 mmHg (95% CI -6.01 to -3.50; 20 RCTs, 21,414 participants) and diastolic by 2.43 mmHg (95% CI -3.50 to -1.36; 19 RCTs, 20,830 participants), and slightly reduced non-fatal acute coronary syndrome and cardiovascular mortality (all moderate certainty), while raising blood potassium by 0.12 mmol/L; hyperkalaemia showed little to no difference, and all 26 trials specifically excluded participants for whom increased potassium is known to be potentially harmful.

  3. Potassium supplementation for the management of primary hypertension in adults.
    Meta-analysisDickinson et al., 2006 (Cochrane) · PMID 16856053 · null

    Six RCTs (n=483, 8-16 weeks) met inclusion criteria; meta-analysis of five (n=425) found no statistically significant blood-pressure reduction from potassium supplements (systolic -11.2 mmHg, 95% CI -25.2 to 2.7; diastolic -5.0 mmHg, 95% CI -12.5 to 2.4), falling to -3.9/-1.5 mmHg once one trial in an African population with very high baseline pressure was excluded, and remaining non-significant in the two high-quality trials (n=138). The review concluded the evidence for potassium supplementation lowering blood pressure is not conclusive.

  4. Oral potassium supplementation for management of essential hypertension: A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisPoorolajal et al., 2017 · PMID 28419159 · positive

    In 23 placebo-controlled trials (1213 participants with primary hypertension, minimum 4 weeks), potassium supplementation reduced systolic pressure by 4.25 mmHg (95% CI -5.96 to -2.53; I2=41%) and diastolic by 2.53 mmHg (95% CI -4.05 to -1.02; I2=65%), which the authors described as modest but significant.

  5. Efficacy of nonpharmacologic interventions in adults with high-normal blood pressure: results from phase 1 of the Trials of Hypertension Prevention. Trials of Hypertension Prevention Collaborative Research Group.
    Randomised trialWhelton et al., 1997 (TOHP Phase 1) · PMID 9022561 · null

    In 2182 adults aged 35-54 with diastolic pressure 80-89 mmHg, potassium supplementation produced no significant blood pressure reduction at either 6 or 18 months, whereas weight loss (-2.9/-2.4 mmHg) and sodium reduction (-2.1/-1.2 mmHg) did.

  6. Potassium Intake and Blood Pressure: A Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Meta-analysisFilippini et al., 2020 · PMID 32500831 · mixed

    Across 32 trials of at least 4 weeks (doses 30-140 mmol/day), the relationship was U-shaped: the blood-pressure-lowering effect weakened above a 30 mmol/day difference in urinary potassium and pressure rose again at differences around 80 mmol/day, an increase seen in drug-treated hypertensive participants but not in their untreated counterparts.

  7. Effect of changes in potassium intake on blood pressure: a dose-response meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (2000-2024).
    Journal articleGranal et al., 2025 · PMID 40612568 · mixed

    Across 10 RCTs, a 50 mmol/day rise in urinary potassium excretion was associated with a 5.3 mmHg systolic and 3.62 mmHg diastolic reduction in people with hypertension but only 0.5 mmHg systolic and 0.12 mmHg diastolic in people without hypertension.

  8. Potassium-Enriched Salt Substitutes: A Review of Recommendations in Clinical Management Guidelines.
    Narrative reviewXu et al., 2024 · PMID 38284271 · mixed

    A systematic search of 32 hypertension and 14 kidney clinical guidelines found recommendations about potassium-enriched salt substitutes to be incomplete and inconsistent, with the products rarely used despite the trial evidence; the authors proposed draft wording recommending 75% NaCl/25% KCl salt for patients with hypertension unless they have advanced kidney disease, are taking a potassium supplement or potassium-sparing diuretic, or have another contraindication.
