Herbs

Ginseng (Panax ginseng and Panax quinquefolius)

Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Ginseng root has been tested in a couple of dozen small, short studies in people with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Pooled together they show a very small dip in fasting blood sugar and no reliable change in the three-month average, and a careful US study found ginseng did nothing at all for insulin production or sensitivity. American ginseng in particular can weaken the blood thinner warfarin, so people on warfarin should not take it without their prescriber knowing, and it is not a replacement for any diabetes medication you have been prescribed.

Safety

Ginseng is not a replacement for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and nothing here is a reason to stop or reduce a prescription. In clinical trials the incidence of adverse events with single-ingredient ginseng is similar to placebo, the most common being headache, sleep disturbance and gastrointestinal upset (Coon and Ernst 2002, PMID 12020172). A dedicated 12-week safety trial of American ginseng 3 g/day in 74 people with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk found no change in kidney, liver or haemostatic measures and no difference in the number or severity of adverse events versus placebo (Mucalo et al. 2014, PMID 24891873). Against that, a meta-analysis of 20 RCTs in prediabetes and type 2 diabetes reported that ginseng increased heart rate and TNF-alpha alongside its glucose and lipid benefits (Naseri et al. 2022, PMID 35745129). Ginsenoside content varies widely between products and species, and one clamp study found ginsenosides undetectable in plasma after dosing, which limits generalising from any single trial.

Who should be careful: People taking warfarin should not start American ginseng without prescriber awareness and INR monitoring, because it reduced warfarin's anticoagulant effect in a randomized trial (Yuan et al. 2004, PMID 15238367). A meta-analysis of 20 trials found ginseng raised heart rate alongside its glucose and lipid effects (Naseri et al. 2022, PMID 35745129); no harm from that increase was reported in those trials, but if you have an arrhythmia, angina, heart failure or blood pressure that is not yet controlled - conditions that commonly cluster with type 2 diabetes (Kuan et al. 2022, PMID 36460578) - raise ginseng with your prescriber before starting, and stop the ginseng and seek advice if you notice palpitations, a racing pulse or new breathlessness. Combination products containing ginseng alongside other ingredients have been associated with serious adverse events and even fatalities in spontaneous reports, though causal attribution is usually not possible (Coon and Ernst 2002, PMID 12020172). Type 1 diabetes: not studied, and not a substitute for insulin.

Interactions: Warfarin: American ginseng lowered peak INR and warfarin exposure in healthy volunteers (Yuan et al. 2004, PMID 15238367); for Asian Panax ginseng the data conflict, with one healthy-volunteer study showing no effect on warfarin pharmacokinetics or INR (Jiang et al. 2004, PMID 15089812), another showing a 14% increase in S-warfarin apparent clearance with no direct pharmacodynamic effect (Jiang et al. 2006, PMID 17050802), and a small ischaemic-stroke trial showing no INR difference (Lee et al. 2008, PMID 18637764). Possible interactions with phenelzine (an MAOI) and alcohol have been reported (Coon and Ernst 2002). Ginseng was ineffective on fasting glucose in patients already on oral hypoglycaemic agents or insulin (Gui et al. 2016, PMID 26871778), so additive hypoglycaemia appears unlikely but has not been formally excluded.

What the evidence says

Every endpoint here is a surrogate, including the blood-pressure and arterial-stiffness results: no ginseng trial has measured cardiovascular events, kidney disease, death or progression from prediabetes to diabetes. Shishtar et al. pooled 16 RCTs of at least 30 days in people with and without diabetes: fasting blood glucose fell by 0.31 mmol/L (95% CI -0.59 to -0.03, P=0.03) but there was no significant effect on fasting insulin, HbA1c or HOMA-IR overall, with a significant HbA1c reduction appearing only in a subgroup analysis of parallel versus crossover trials; 67% of trials ran under 12 weeks and participants were relatively well controlled (median HbA1c 7.1% in the diabetes trials). Gui et al. (8 trials in type 2 diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance) likewise found no significant HbA1c difference (SMD -0.148, 95% CI -0.637 to 0.228, P=0.355), with modest fasting glucose, postprandial insulin and HOMA-IR improvements, and specifically noted that ginseng was ineffective at lowering fasting glucose in patients already treated with oral hypoglycaemic agents or insulin. Individual results are mixed: American ginseng extract 3 g/day for 8 weeks lowered HbA1c by 0.29% (P=0.041) and fasting glucose by 0.71 mmol/L (P=0.008) in a 24-person crossover trial; Korean red ginseng 6 g/day for 12 weeks in 19 well-controlled patients did not change the primary endpoint HbA1c (6.5%) although OGTT glucose indices fell 8-11% and insulin indices 33-38%; an Rg3-enriched Korean red plus American ginseng combination at 2.25 g/day for 12 weeks lowered 24-hour systolic blood pressure by 3.98 mmHg (P=0.04) and HbA1c by 0.35% (P=0.02) in 80 patients with diabetes and hypertension; and a Washington University study in overweight adults with impaired glucose tolerance or new type 2 diabetes found 30 days of 8 g/day ginseng root extract or 250-500 mg/day ginsenoside Re improved neither beta-cell function nor clamp-measured insulin sensitivity, with ginsenosides Re, Rb1 and Rb2 undetectable in plasma after treatment. A 2022 meta-analysis of 20 RCTs in prediabetes and type 2 diabetes reported reductions in fasting glucose, total cholesterol, IL-6 and HOMA-IR but also increases in heart rate and TNF-alpha.

Doses studied: American ginseng extract 3 g/day (1 g with each meal) for 8-12 weeks; Korean red ginseng 6 g/day (2 g before each meal) for 12 weeks; combined Rg3-enriched Korean red plus American ginseng 2.25 g/day for 12 weeks; Panax ginseng root extract 8 g/day or ginsenoside Re 250-500 mg/day for 30 days in the null clamp study.

Why this grade: Two meta-analyses of 8-16 small, short trials find a tiny fasting-glucose reduction (about 0.3 mmol/L) and no significant HbA1c effect; the most rigorous mechanistic study, using a hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp, was flatly null and found ginsenosides undetectable in plasma; and the positive RCTs are single-centre studies of 19-80 well-controlled patients, largely from one research group, with the largest HbA1c effect 0.35 percentage points. Trials are small, few and preliminary, every endpoint is a surrogate, no trial has measured a hard clinical outcome or progression to diabetes, and a 20-RCT meta-analysis reported increases in heart rate and TNF-alpha alongside the glucose benefits. Taken with the null pooled HbA1c result, this is early, not moderate.

For fatigue & low energy

Ginseng has been studied most seriously in people who are worn out during or after cancer treatment, and the results there genuinely conflict: one large trial found American ginseng helped by eight weeks, while a second sizeable trial and several smaller ones found it no better than a dummy capsule — the researchers on that second trial concluded there was no good reason to recommend it. Outside cancer, the two trials in people with long-standing unexplained tiredness did not show a clear benefit on their main measure. So this is an honest maybe, not a yes, and it is worth knowing that ginseng can change how blood-thinning medication works.

Safety

Short-term tolerability in the trials is reassuring. In the largest trial (2,000 mg/day American ginseng for 8 weeks in 364 cancer survivors) self-reported and CTCAE-graded provider toxicities did not differ statistically from placebo; the 127-patient advanced-cancer trial actually recorded fewer any-grade toxicities with ginseng than placebo (28/63 vs 33/64, P = .024); the 6-week Korean red ginseng chronic-fatigue trial (3 g/day) reported no adverse events; and the 4-week gastrointestinal-survivor trial reported no treatment-related adverse events. This is a short-term and modest-sized safety record only: no trial ran beyond 8 weeks, so long-term use has not been studied, and product content varies widely because ginsenoside concentration depends on species, plant part and processing method. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established in any of these trials. Readers should also know that one of the non-oncology trials was co-authored by an employee of a ginseng manufacturer.

Who should be careful: Do not add or stop ginseng alongside warfarin or other anticoagulant therapy without medical supervision and INR monitoring, because it can measurably change how much anticoagulation you are actually getting. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established. Persistent fatigue can be the presenting sign of anemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnea, diabetes, depression, heart failure or cancer; ginseng is not a substitute for a diagnostic workup, and new or worsening fatigue lasting weeks warrants medical assessment first.

Interactions: The best-documented interaction is with warfarin, and it runs in the opposite direction to what is often assumed. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 20 healthy volunteers, American ginseng REDUCED warfarin's anticoagulant effect, lowering peak INR versus placebo (difference -0.19, 95% CI -0.36 to -0.07, P = 0.0012) along with INR area under the curve and plasma warfarin levels — meaning less anticoagulation than prescribed, not more bleeding. A review of warfarin-herb interactions found the ginseng case report likewise described decreased anticoagulation; the intracerebral haemorrhage case in that review involved ginkgo, not ginseng. Separately, a case report describes a sudden rise in INR in two warfarin-treated patients using a multi-ingredient product containing Panax ginseng, so interference can go in either direction and is not predictable. Anyone taking warfarin or another anticoagulant should not start or stop ginseng without their prescriber's involvement and INR monitoring.

What the evidence says

The evidence base is dominated by cancer-related fatigue (CRF) and should not be generalised to other fatigue populations. In the phase III multisite double-blind N07C2 trial, 364 fatigued cancer survivors took American ginseng 2,000 mg/day or placebo; change on the MFSI-SF general fatigue subscale was 14.4 (SD 27.1) vs 8.2 (SD 24.8) at the pre-specified 4-week primary endpoint, which was not significant (P = .07), and 20 (SD 27) vs 10.3 (SD 26.1) at 8 weeks (P = .003), with greater benefit in patients still on active treatment. Independent replication has largely failed: a 127-patient double-blind RCT of Panax ginseng 400 mg twice daily in advanced cancer found mean FACIT-F improvement of 7.5 (SD 12.7) vs 6.5 (SD 9.9) for placebo (P = .67), with the authors concluding there is no justification to recommend it; a phase II RCT in 32 head-and-neck survivors found American ginseng 1,000 mg/day no better than placebo (mean Brief Fatigue Inventory 4.6 vs 3.4, non-significant, with the interference-with-general-activity item trending in favour of placebo, P = 0.06); and a randomized trial of red ginseng powder 2.0 g/day in 40 patients on gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel found no significant between-group difference on any Cancer Fatigue Scale domain. The meta-analytic picture is borderline rather than supportive: a 2022 pooled analysis of 7 RCTs reported a small reduction in CRF whose confidence interval touched zero (SMD -0.21, 95% CI -0.42 to 0.00), while a 2026 meta-analysis of 14 phytotherapy RCTs found the ginseng subgroup non-significant with GRADE certainty rated very low; a small 2026 single-centre RCT in 65 gastrointestinal cancer survivors (250 mg/day for 4 weeks) did report a large benefit (adjusted mean difference -1.38, 95% CI -1.90 to -0.86). Outside oncology, both randomized trials failed on their primary endpoint: Korean red ginseng 3 g/day for 6 weeks in 50 adults with unexplained fatigue lasting more than 6 months produced no significant between-group difference on the fatigue VAS or any secondary measure, and a 90-person trial in idiopathic chronic fatigue found P. ginseng 1 g or 2 g/day did not significantly reduce the total numeric fatigue score versus placebo, with significance confined to mental-fatigue and 2 g VAS scores. No adequate randomized evidence exists for ginseng in ME/CFS, post-viral fatigue, iron-deficiency fatigue or thyroid-related fatigue.

Doses studied: Cancer-related fatigue: American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius) ground root 2,000 mg/day for 8 weeks in the largest trial, and 1,000 mg/day for 8 weeks in a null head-and-neck trial. Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng): 400 mg twice daily for 28 days in the 127-patient null advanced-cancer trial, 250 mg/day for 4 weeks in a small positive trial, and red ginseng powder 2.0 g/day for 56 days during chemotherapy (null). Non-cancer fatigue: Korean red ginseng 3 g/day for 6 weeks (null), and a 20% ethanol P. ginseng extract at 1 g or 2 g/day for 4 weeks (null on its primary total-fatigue score). Ginsenoside content depends heavily on species, plant part and processing, so milligram doses are not directly comparable across these trials.

Why this grade: Downgraded from moderate to early after adversarial re-review. The trial base is genuinely randomized and includes one 364-person phase III study, which is larger than 'early' normally implies, but replication has failed more often than it has succeeded, so in practice the evidence still functions as preliminary rather than established. The largest positive trial missed its own pre-specified 4-week primary endpoint (P = .07) and reached significance only at the 8-week secondary timepoint. The largest independent replication — 127 patients on Panax ginseng 400 mg twice daily — was flatly null (P = .67), and its authors concluded there is no justification to recommend ginseng for cancer-related fatigue. Two further randomized trials, in head-and-neck and pancreatic cancer, were also null; the 2022 meta-analysis's pooled confidence interval touches zero; and the 2026 phytotherapy meta-analysis found the ginseng subgroup non-significant with GRADE certainty rated very low. Not graded moderate, because 'overall positive' no longer describes this literature. Not graded not_supported, because two randomized trials including the largest did show benefit, so the evidence is conflicting rather than uniformly null. Any signal is confined to cancer-related fatigue; both non-oncology randomized trials failed on their primary fatigue endpoint.
