Low-dose amitriptyline and other tricyclic antidepressants

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For gut & digestion

Amitriptyline is an old antidepressant used here at a much smaller dose than for depression, where it works on the nerve signalling between gut and brain rather than on mood. The biggest and best trial found people taking it reported meaningfully less severe IBS after six months, though the average improvement was smaller than the researchers had set as their own bar for a clearly worthwhile difference, and trials in children found no benefit over a dummy pill. It is one of the better-supported options when diet changes and first-line medicines have not helped, but it takes weeks to work, needs a slow dose increase, the drowsy and constipating side effects lead about one in eight people to stop, and long-term use is worth reviewing periodically with a doctor.

Safety

Common effects are drowsiness or morning grogginess, dry mouth, constipation and blurred vision; taking the dose a few hours before bed and titrating slowly reduces these. The constipating effect makes it a better fit for diarrhoea-predominant IBS and a potential problem in constipation-predominant IBS, though ATLANTIS post hoc analyses (PMID 39863398) found side effects were similar across subtypes and no evidence of deleterious effects in any particular subtype. A 2025 safety meta-analysis of 54 trials (PMID 40471839) calculated a number needed to harm of 24 for tricyclics — among the highest absolute discontinuation risks of the IBS drugs studied, alongside alosetron (NNH 14) and tenapanor (NNH 16), and higher at larger doses. Long-term safety deserves a specific conversation: in a propensity-matched cohort of 669,083 US adults with IBS, antidepressant use was associated with increased all-cause mortality (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.26-1.45; 1.6% vs 1.0%), consistent across subclasses (Mehravar 2026, PMID 41951879). That study is observational and cannot separate the drug from the reasons it was prescribed, but it is the largest long-term dataset available and the authors themselves call for cautious prescribing. It is not being prescribed as an antidepressant at these doses, and explaining that up front improves acceptance; it also takes several weeks to show effect.

Who should be careful: Avoid in recent myocardial infarction, known arrhythmia or clinically significant QT prolongation, and within 14 days of a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. Use caution in narrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention, prostatic enlargement and severe hepatic impairment. ATLANTIS excluded people with evidence of suicidal ideation, and that exclusion should carry over into practice: tricyclics carry the class warning about suicidal thoughts in younger adults, and tricyclic overdose is cardiotoxic and can be fatal, which matters when prescribing to anyone at risk.

Interactions: Serotonin syndrome risk when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, triptans, linezolid, lithium or MAOIs. CYP2D6 inhibitors — fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, terbinafine, quinidine — raise amitriptyline levels substantially. Additive sedation with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines and sedating antihistamines. Additive anticholinergic load with antispasmodics, bladder antimuscarinics and sedating antihistamines. Additive QT risk with other QT-prolonging drugs.

What the evidence says

ATLANTIS, the largest tricyclic trial ever conducted in IBS, randomised 463 primary-care patients with Rome IV IBS of any subtype to self-titrated amitriptyline 10-30 mg nightly or placebo for 6 months and found a difference in IBS Severity Scoring System score of -27.0 (95% CI -46.9 to -7.10; p=0.0079), with subjective global relief favouring amitriptyline (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.19-2.66; p=0.005). The trial's own pre-specified minimum clinically important difference was 35 points, so the primary result was statistically significant but below that threshold, and amitriptyline had no effect on somatoform symptom reporting, anxiety, depression or work and social adjustment. Pooled data are consistent in direction: 17 antidepressant RCTs gave a relative risk of symptoms not improving of 0.66 (95% CI 0.57-0.76), and a 2026 network meta-analysis of 68 RCTs (6,694 participants) ranked tricyclics second overall (15 trials, 1,519 patients, RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43-0.85), while explicitly noting possible publication bias and low-to-very-low certainty for most comparisons. The negative evidence is real and spans populations: a 431-woman trial of desipramine was not significantly better than placebo on intention-to-treat (60% vs 47% responders, p=0.16, NNT 8.1) and reached significance only per-protocol; a 51-patient trial found neither imipramine 50 mg nor citalopram improved global IBS endpoints over placebo; a 90-child multicentre RCT of amitriptyline was null (63% vs 57.5% feeling better, p=0.63); and the 2021 Cochrane review of antidepressants for paediatric functional abdominal pain disorders found RR 1.17 (95% CI 0.87-1.56, 2 studies, 205 participants, low certainty), concluding there may be no difference from placebo (PMID 33560523). Tolerability is the main practical constraint: adverse-event withdrawals in ATLANTIS were 12.9% versus 8.7% on placebo, and a 2025 safety meta-analysis put the number needed to harm for tricyclics at 24 — among the highest of all IBS drugs, alongside alosetron (14) and tenapanor (16). A 2026 nationwide propensity-matched cohort of 669,083 US adults with IBS also found antidepressant use associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.26-1.45), an observational finding subject to confounding by indication but large enough to warrant discussion before long-term use.

Doses studied: Amitriptyline 10 mg once daily at night, patient-titrated over 3 weeks up to a maximum of 30 mg once daily, continued for 6 months (ATLANTIS). Imipramine was studied at 50 mg daily. In the paediatric trial, amitriptyline was given at 10 mg/day under 35 kg and 20 mg/day over 35 kg for 4 weeks. These are well below antidepressant doses, and ATLANTIS used a self-titration document to guide patients.

Why this grade: Multiple meta-analyses and the large ATLANTIS trial point in the same direction, which rules out an "early" grade. It stops short of "strong" because the effect size is modest — ATLANTIS's 27-point advantage fell short of its own pre-specified 35-point minimum clinically important difference — earlier tricyclic trials (desipramine, imipramine) were null on intention-to-treat, a multicentre paediatric trial and a Cochrane review in children found no difference from placebo, guidelines position tricyclics as second-line with a conditional recommendation, and the most recent network meta-analysis rates overall certainty as low or very low with possible publication bias.

Sources (8)
  1. Amitriptyline at Low-Dose and Titrated for Irritable Bowel Syndrome as Second-Line Treatment in primary care (ATLANTIS): a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trial.
    Ford AC, Wright-Hughes A, Alderson SL, et al., 2023 (The Lancet) · PMID 37858323 · positive

    463 adults at 55 English general practices were randomised to titrated amitriptyline 10-30 mg nightly (232) or placebo (231) for 6 months. IBS-SSS score at 6 months favoured amitriptyline by -27.0 points (95% CI -46.9 to -7.10; p=0.0079). 46 (20%) discontinued amitriptyline (30, or 13%, due to adverse events) versus 59 (26%) on placebo (20, or 9%, due to adverse events). Five serious adverse reactions occurred (two amitriptyline, three placebo) and five serious adverse events unrelated to trial medication.

  2. Low-dose titrated amitriptyline as second-line treatment for adults with irritable bowel syndrome in primary care: the ATLANTIS RCT.
    Wright-Hughes A, Ford AC, Alderson SL, et al., 2024 (Health Technology Assessment) · PMID 39397570 · mixed

    The full trial report states the pre-specified minimum clinically important difference was 35 points on the IBS-SSS, which the observed -27.0 point difference did not reach. Subjective global relief favoured amitriptyline (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.19-2.66, p=0.005), but amitriptyline had no impact on somatoform symptom reporting, anxiety, depression, or work and social adjustment scores. Adverse-event trial withdrawals were more common with amitriptyline (12.9% vs 8.7% for placebo), though most adverse events were mild.

  3. Efficacy of gut-brain neuromodulators and brain-gut behaviour therapies for irritable bowel syndrome: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Khasawneh M, Thakur ER, Goodoory VC, et al., 2026 (Gut) · PMID 42362221 · mixed

    Across 68 RCTs (6,694 participants), tricyclic antidepressants ranked second of all gut-brain treatments (15 trials, 1,519 patients; RR of global IBS symptoms or abdominal pain not improving 0.60, 95% CI 0.43-0.85; P-score 0.79), behind SNRIs (6 trials, 387 patients, RR 0.49, 0.32-0.75), of which no trial was at low risk of bias. The authors report possible publication bias in some analyses and state that overall certainty in the evidence was low or very low for most comparisons.

  4. Cognitive-behavioral therapy versus education and desipramine versus placebo for moderate to severe functional bowel disorders.
    Drossman DA, Toner BB, Whitehead WE, et al., 2003 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 12851867 · null

    In 431 female patients with moderate-to-severe functional bowel disorders, desipramine did not show significant benefit over placebo on intention-to-treat analysis (60% vs 47% responders, p=0.16, NNT 8.1). It reached significance only in the per-protocol analysis (73% vs 49%, p=0.01, NNT 5.2), and more so when participants with undetectable blood levels were excluded (p=0.002) — meaning the apparent benefit depended on excluding people who did not actually take the drug. Cognitive-behavioural therapy, by contrast, was significantly better than education on intention-to-treat (70% vs 37%, p=0.0001, NNT 3.1). All participants were women, which limits generalisability.

  5. Antidepressant therapy (imipramine and citalopram) for irritable bowel syndrome: a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial.
    Talley NJ, Kellow JE, Boyce P, et al., 2008 (Digestive Diseases and Sciences) · PMID 17503182 · null

    In 51 randomised IBS patients (majority diarrhoea-predominant), adequate relief of IBS symptoms — the primary endpoint — was similar across imipramine 50 mg, citalopram 40 mg and placebo, and improvements in abdominal pain were not greater with imipramine. Imipramine did beat placebo on bowel symptom severity ratings for interference (P=0.05) and distress (P=0.02), with non-significant trends on depression score (P=0.08) and SF-36 Mental Component Score (P=0.07). Citalopram was not superior to placebo on any measure. The authors concluded neither drug significantly improved global IBS endpoints over placebo.

  6. Multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of amitriptyline in children with functional gastrointestinal disorders.
    Saps M, Youssef N, Miranda A, et al., 2009 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 19596010 · null

    In 90 children with irritable bowel syndrome, functional abdominal pain or functional dyspepsia randomised to 4 weeks of amitriptyline (10 mg/day under 35 kg, 20 mg/day over 35 kg) or placebo, 63% on amitriptyline reported feeling better and 5% worse, versus 57.5% better and 2.5% worse on placebo (P=0.63). Pain relief was excellent in 15% and good in 35% on amitriptyline versus excellent in 7% and good in 38% on placebo (P=0.85), and logistic regression showed no difference between drug and placebo (P=0.83). Both arms were associated with an excellent therapeutic response, illustrating how large the placebo effect is in this condition. Amitriptyline did reduce anxiety scores (P<0.0001).

  7. Effect of Antidepressants and Psychological Therapies in Irritable Bowel Syndrome: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Ford AC, Lacy BE, Harris LA, Quigley EMM, Moayyedi P, 2019 (American Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 30177784 · positive

    Across 17 RCTs comparing antidepressants with placebo, the relative risk of IBS symptoms not improving was 0.66 (95% CI 0.57-0.76), with similar effects for tricyclics and SSRIs, although with heterogeneity between the SSRI trials (I2=49%, P=0.07). The authors flagged significant heterogeneity and significant funnel plot asymmetry in the psychological therapy analyses, and design issues including lack of blinding, cautioning that treatment effects may be overestimated as a result.

  8. Association of pharmacotherapy with all-cause mortality among patients with irritable bowel syndrome.
    Mehravar S, Yeo YH, Pimentel M, et al., 2026 (Communications Medicine) · PMID 41951879 · negative

    In a 1:1 propensity score-matched retrospective cohort of 669,083 US adults aged 18-65 with IBS, drawn from a nationwide electronic health record database covering 2005 to 2023, antidepressant use was associated with increased all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.35, 95% CI 1.26-1.45; mortality 1.6% vs 1.0%), an association the authors report was consistent across antidepressant subclasses and demographic subgroups. Antispasmodics were not associated with increased mortality (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.89-1.00), nor were secretagogues, polyethylene glycol, rifaximin or eluxadoline. The authors state the findings raise concerns about the safety of antidepressants in IBS treatment and underscore the need for cautious prescribing and further research. This is observational data subject to confounding by indication — people prescribed antidepressants differ systematically from those who are not — and is not a randomised comparison, but it is the largest long-term safety dataset available in this population.

For migraine & headache

Amitriptyline is an old antidepressant used at much lower doses as a nightly tablet to make migraines less frequent - it does nothing for an attack in progress. Pooled trials show people on it have fewer migraine days than people on a dummy pill, and it can suit you if you also have trouble sleeping. Be honest about the limits, though: the evidence comes mostly from older, smaller studies, and the one review that looked specifically at whether it halves your attacks found no clear advantage over a dummy pill. Dry mouth, drowsiness and weight gain are common, and it is dangerous in overdose.

Safety

Anticholinergic and sedative effects are common and dose-limiting: dry mouth (reported by about a third in head-to-head trials), drowsiness and morning grogginess, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention, weight gain (mean 2.4 kg over 26 weeks) and orthostatic hypotension with dizziness or falls, which matters especially in older adults. Amitriptyline is on the Beers list of drugs to avoid in older people for these reasons. Pooled data show tricyclics are less well tolerated than both placebo and SSRIs/SNRIs. It prolongs the QT interval and can cause arrhythmias; it is notably dangerous in overdose, which is a genuine consideration for anyone at risk of self-harm. Serious mood change occurred in three participants in the amitriptyline arm of the CHAMP trial, and antidepressants carry a boxed warning for suicidal thinking in people under 25. Start low, take at night, titrate slowly, and taper rather than stopping abruptly (abrupt withdrawal causes nausea, headache and malaise). Benefit builds over weeks and, per the pooled data, increases with longer treatment - so it should not be judged after only a few weeks.

Who should be careful: Recent myocardial infarction, significant conduction abnormalities or arrhythmia, congenital long QT syndrome, severe hepatic impairment, and mania. Avoid within 14 days of an MAO inhibitor. Use with great caution in narrow-angle glaucoma, benign prostatic hyperplasia or urinary retention, epilepsy (lowers seizure threshold), and in older adults. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding - a clinician decision. Avoid or use with extra caution where overdose risk is a concern.

Interactions: Contraindicated with MAO inhibitors (hypertensive crisis, serotonin syndrome). Additive QT prolongation with other QT-prolonging drugs, including some antipsychotics, antiarrhythmics, macrolides and ondansetron. Additive sedation with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines and antihistamines; additive anticholinergic load with oxybutynin, antihistamines and antipsychotics. CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion, quinidine) raise amitriptyline levels substantially. Serotonin syndrome risk when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, linezolid or triptans. May reduce the antihypertensive effect of clonidine and guanethidine.

What the evidence says

Amitriptyline is a daily PREVENTIVE tricyclic; it has no acute role. A meta-analysis of 37 randomised trials found tricyclics reduced migraine attack frequency versus placebo (standardised mean difference -0.70, 95% CI -0.93 to -0.48) and were more likely to halve headache intensity (RR 1.80, 1.24-2.62), with the effect increasing over longer treatment; notably, in the same review tricyclics were NOT significantly better than SSRIs on headache frequency (SMD -0.20, -0.60 to 0.19). A dedicated 2017 meta-analysis of 12 tricyclic trials in 1,006 adults confirmed the frequency benefit (SMD -0.75, -1.05 to -0.46, P<0.00001) but found the responder analysis NULL - people on tricyclics were not significantly more likely to achieve a 50%-or-greater reduction in headache burden than on placebo (RR 1.40, 0.89-2.20, P=.14) - and found amitriptyline no different from SSRIs or SNRIs (SMD -0.01, -0.31 to 0.28, P=.94), with tricyclics less well tolerated than both placebo and SSRIs/SNRIs. An earlier meta-analysis of 38 antidepressant trials found treated patients twice as likely to report headache improvement (RR 2.0, 1.6-2.4), NNT 3.2, but could not determine whether the effect is independent of depression. Modern trial evidence is less flattering. In a 178-patient three-arm trial, amitriptyline 25 mg cut migraine days by 2.2 per month against 1.1 for placebo, but the only reported significance tests were melatonin versus placebo (p=0.009) and melatonin versus amitriptyline (p=0.19); melatonin was superior to amitriptyline on 50%-responder rate, amitriptyline was the least well tolerated arm, and it caused significant weight gain. In the placebo-controlled CHAMP trial (children and adolescents, so not directly transferable), 52% on amitriptyline achieved a 50% reduction versus 61% on placebo (P=0.26) and the trial was halted for futility. A 26-week adult noninferiority trial showed amitriptyline and topiramate equivalent on migraine frequency (-2.7 vs -2.6) but included no placebo arm, and topiramate actually beat amitriptyline on functional disability and three quality-of-life domains. For chronic migraine, a 2024 NIHR health technology assessment found no amitriptyline efficacy trials meeting inclusion criteria at all.

Doses studied: Taken daily at night for PREVENTION, not during an attack. Doses in the retrieved adult trials: 25 mg/day fixed (Goncalves 2016) and 25 mg/day titrated to a maximum of 100 mg/day or maximum tolerated dose (Dodick 2009). Clinical practice commonly uses lower doses than those used for depression.

Why this grade: Three separate evidence syntheses show tricyclics reduce migraine attack frequency versus placebo (Jackson 2010 SMD -0.70; Xu 2017 SMD -0.75; and amitriptyline SMD -1.2 in a 2015 network meta-analysis), which is genuine meta-analytic RCT support. Graded down from strong because the pooled evidence rests on older, smaller trials; because the dedicated 2017 tricyclic meta-analysis found NO significant advantage over placebo on the 50%-responder outcome (RR 1.40, 0.89-2.20, P=.14) and no difference from SSRIs/SNRIs; because a modern three-arm adult trial reported a significance test only for melatonin over placebo, not for amitriptyline; because the largest placebo-controlled trial in any age group (paediatric CHAMP) was stopped for futility; because no eligible amitriptyline efficacy trial in chronic migraine exists at all; and because the 2025 ACP guideline rates all preventive recommendations conditional with low-certainty evidence.

Sources (8)
  1. Tricyclic antidepressants and headaches: systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Jackson et al., 2010 (BMJ) · PMID 20961988 · positive

    37 randomised trials of adults taking tricyclics as sole treatment for at least four weeks. Tricyclics reduced migraine attacks versus placebo (SMD -0.70, 95% CI -0.93 to -0.48) and days with tension-type headache (SMD -1.29, -2.18 to -0.39). 50%-or-greater intensity reduction versus placebo: RR 1.80 (1.24-2.62) for migraine. Effect increased with longer treatment duration. Important internal null: tricyclics were NOT significantly better than SSRIs on headache frequency (migraine SMD -0.20, -0.60 to 0.19). Adverse effects were more common than placebo (RR 1.53, 1.11-2.12) - dry mouth, drowsiness, weight gain - but dropout rates did not increase.

  2. Tricyclic antidepressants for preventing migraine in adults.
    Xu et al., 2017 (Medicine, Baltimore) · PMID 28562550 · mixed

    12 trials, 1,006 participants (9 versus placebo, 3 versus SSRIs/SNRIs). Tricyclics beat placebo on migraine frequency/index (SMD -0.75, 95% CI -1.05 to -0.46, P<0.00001). But the responder analysis was NULL: participants on tricyclics were NOT significantly more likely to achieve a 50%-or-greater reduction in headache burden than those on placebo (RR 1.40, 95% CI 0.89-2.20, P=.14). Amitriptyline did not differ from SSRIs or SNRIs (SMD -0.01, -0.31 to 0.28, P=.94). Tricyclics were less well tolerated than placebo (RR 1.73, 1.00-2.99, P=.05) and than SSRIs/SNRIs (RR 2.85, 0.97-8.41, P=.06).

  3. Treatment of chronic headache with antidepressants: a meta-analysis.
    Tomkins, Jackson et al., 2001 (Am J Med) · PMID 11448661 · positive

    38 randomised placebo-controlled trials, 44 study arms; only 19 used tricyclics (18 serotonin antagonists, 7 SSRIs). Patients on antidepressants were twice as likely to report headache improvement (RR 2.0, 95% CI 1.6-2.4); 31% more treated than placebo patients improved, giving an NNT of 3.2. Standardised mean improvement 0.94 (0.65-1.2). No differences by drug class or between migraine and tension headache. Crucially, the authors state assessment of depression across studies was insufficient to determine whether the effect is independent of depression.

  4. Randomised clinical trial comparing melatonin 3 mg, amitriptyline 25 mg and placebo for migraine prevention.
    Goncalves et al., 2016 (J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry) · PMID 27165014 · mixed

    196 randomised, 178 treated and followed 12 weeks. Mean reduction in migraine headache days: 2.7 melatonin, 2.2 amitriptyline 25 mg, 1.1 placebo. The only significance tests reported are melatonin versus placebo (p=0.009, superior) and melatonin versus amitriptyline (p=0.19, no difference) - no amitriptyline-versus-placebo test is reported. Melatonin was superior to amitriptyline on the proportion achieving a >50% reduction, was better tolerated than amitriptyline, and amitriptyline caused significant weight gain.

  5. Trial of Amitriptyline, Topiramate, and Placebo for Pediatric Migraine.
    Powers et al., 2017 (N Engl J Med, CHAMP) · PMID 27788026 · negative

    NOTE: children and adolescents aged 8-17, not adults. 361 randomised, 328 analysed; concluded early for futility at planned interim analysis. 50%-or-greater reduction in headache days: 52% amitriptyline versus 61% placebo (P=0.26). No differences in headache-related disability, headache days, or trial completion. Amitriptyline caused more fatigue (30% vs 14%) and dry mouth (25% vs 12%), and three patients in the amitriptyline group had serious adverse events of altered mood.

  6. Topiramate versus amitriptyline in migraine prevention: a 26-week, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, double-dummy, parallel-group noninferiority trial in adult migraineurs.
    Dodick et al., 2009 (Clin Ther) · PMID 19393844 · mixed

    331 adults with 3-12 migraines/month, titrated from 25 mg to a maximum of 100 mg/day. Change in mean monthly migraine episodes: -2.7 amitriptyline versus -2.6 topiramate, not significantly different (95% CI -0.6 to 0.7), with no significant differences on any prespecified secondary outcome. There was NO placebo arm, so this establishes equivalence to topiramate rather than efficacy. Against amitriptyline: topiramate gave significantly greater improvement in functional disability during attacks (P=0.040) and in three Migraine-Specific Quality of Life domains (P=0.012, 0.014, 0.029). Amitriptyline caused a mean 2.4 kg weight gain (topiramate 2.4 kg loss), dry mouth in 35.5%, fatigue 24.3%, somnolence 17.8%.

  7. Prevention of Episodic Migraine Headache Using Pharmacologic Treatments in Outpatient Settings: A Clinical Guideline From the American College of Physicians.
    Qaseem et al., 2025 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 39899861 · mixed

    Guideline for adults with 1-14 headache days per month. The tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline was one of the eight drug classes ACP evaluated for initiating preventive monotherapy. All three ACP recommendations have conditional strength and low-certainty evidence - so amitriptyline's guideline support, like every other preventive's, is explicitly low-certainty.

  8. Preventive drug treatments for adults with chronic migraine: a systematic review with economic modelling.
    Mistry et al., 2024 (Health Technol Assess) · PMID 39365169 · null

    The authors report finding no trials of amitriptyline (or propranolol) in chronic migraine meeting inclusion criteria for the efficacy review. Amitriptyline appeared only in the adverse-events review of 40 RCTs and 25,891 participants, where people using amitriptyline or topiramate had nervous-system or gastrointestinal adverse events. The review concludes there was not sufficient quality evidence to support the use of oral drugs other than topiramate in chronic migraine.
