Acupuncture

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache Moderate evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For chronic pain

Acupuncture reliably beats doing nothing for long-standing muscle and joint pain, and many people do feel better after a course of it. The harder question is how much of that comes from the needles themselves: when trials compare real acupuncture against a convincing fake, the extra benefit is small and in several conditions it disappears entirely. It is low-risk when done by a trained practitioner with sterile single-use needles, so it can be reasonable to try - with realistic expectations.

Safety

Low risk in trained hands, and the common events are minor. The 2020 Cochrane review found a similar incidence of adverse events immediately after treatment with acupuncture versus sham (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.46 to 1.01; four trials, 465 participants), with the most commonly reported events being insertion-point pain, bruising, haematoma, bleeding, temporary worsening of low back pain, and pain in the leg or shoulder. The fibromyalgia Cochrane review reported no serious adverse events but noted there were too few events to be certain of the risks. A 35-year systematic review of case reports documented rare serious complications - infections (primarily hepatitis, traced to a few practitioners) and organ, tissue and nerve injury - with a decline in serious reports after 1988 attributed to clean-needle technique and stricter practitioner training.

Who should be careful: Use a licensed practitioner using sterile single-use needles; historical serious harms cluster around poor technique and reused equipment. Extra caution with bleeding disorders or anticoagulant therapy, with local skin infection, cellulitis or open wounds at needling sites, and with needling over the chest wall or supraclavicular fossa (pneumothorax risk). Anyone with a pacemaker or implanted cardiac device should clear electroacupuncture with their cardiologist. Tell the practitioner about pregnancy, lymphoedema or a limb at risk after lymph-node surgery.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents increase bruising, haematoma and bleeding at needle sites. Immunosuppression (including from disease-modifying drugs, chemotherapy or high-dose corticosteroids) raises infection risk and makes sterile technique non-negotiable.

What the evidence says

An updated individual-patient-data meta-analysis pooled 39 randomised trials and 20,827 patients with non-specific musculoskeletal pain, osteoarthritis, chronic headache or shoulder pain, and found acupuncture superior to both sham and no-acupuncture control for every pain condition (all P < .001), with differences close to 0.5 SD versus no-acupuncture control and close to 0.2 SD versus sham, and only about a 15% decrease in treatment effect at one year. Against this, the 2020 Cochrane review of 33 trials (8270 participants) in chronic non-specific low back pain found low-certainty evidence of immediate pain relief versus sham (MD -9.22 on a 0-100 VAS, 95% CI -13.82 to -4.61) that did not meet the 15-point clinical threshold, and very low-certainty evidence of no improvement in back-specific function versus sham (SMD -0.16, 95% CI -0.38 to 0.06); acupuncture was clearly better than no treatment (MD -20.32, 95% CI -24.50 to -16.14). The Cochrane review of acupuncture for peripheral joint osteoarthritis (16 trials, 3498 people) found statistically significant but clinically irrelevant sham-controlled short-term improvement in pain (SMD -0.28, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.11) that became smaller and non-significant when restricted to the shams judged most likely to blind participants, and was clinically irrelevant at six months (SMD -0.10, 95% CI -0.21 to 0.01); against a waiting list, benefits were large and clinically relevant (pain SMD -0.96, 95% CI -1.19 to -0.72), though the review authors noted much of that may reflect expectation or placebo effects. The same pattern holds in fibromyalgia: the Cochrane review of 9 trials (395 participants) found moderate-quality evidence that acupuncture did not differ from sham in reducing pain or fatigue or improving sleep or global well-being. A 282-participant Zelen-design trial in adults aged 50 and over with chronic knee pain found neither needle nor laser acupuncture improved pain versus sham at 12 weeks (mean difference -0.4, 95% CI -1.2 to 0.4, against a minimal clinically important difference of 1.8 units).

Doses studied: Trials delivered courses of manual or electro-acupuncture over roughly six to twelve weeks; the sham-controlled knee trial delivered treatments across 12 weeks. The individual-patient-data meta-analysis found no obvious association between trial outcome and the characteristics of the acupuncture treatment itself; variation in effect size was driven predominantly by what the control group received, with smaller effects in trials using a penetrating-needle sham and in trials with high-intensity control-arm treatment.

Why this grade: The largest individual-patient-data meta-analysis (39 trials, 20,827 patients) found acupuncture superior to both sham and no-acupuncture control, but the margin over sham was only about 0.2 SD versus about 0.5 SD over no acupuncture. Sham-controlled benefit falls below clinical-importance thresholds or disappears in three separate Cochrane reviews - chronic non-specific low back pain, peripheral joint osteoarthritis, and fibromyalgia - and a rigorous 282-participant sham-controlled knee trial was flatly null. Guidelines list it as an option rather than a first-line mainstay. Clear signal against no treatment, weak-to-absent signal against sham - graded on the lower reading.

For migraine & headache

Acupuncture has more and better evidence behind it than most complementary therapies for preventing migraine, and people who get it do tend to have fewer migraine days than people who get nothing. The catch is honest and important: in the biggest, most careful trials, fake acupuncture — needles placed in the wrong spots, or that do not penetrate the skin — worked almost as well as the real thing. So the benefit appears to be genuine but not mostly about where the needles go. It has a good safety record.

Safety

Acupuncture has a good safety record when performed by trained practitioners using single-use sterile needles. Minor effects are common: needle-site pain (reported in 1-45% of patients across prospective surveys), tiredness (2-41%), and minor bleeding or bruising (0.03-38%). Feeling faint or fainting is uncommon (0-0.3%). Serious harm is rare — pneumothorax occurred twice in nearly a quarter of a million treatments in pooled prospective surveys. In migraine trials specifically, adverse events and dropouts due to adverse effects were no different from sham acupuncture, and were significantly LOWER than with prophylactic migraine drugs (OR 0.25 for reporting an adverse effect, OR 0.27 for dropping out because of one); a 2025 multicentre migraine trial reported adverse events in 5.1% of those receiving real acupuncture.

Who should be careful: Needling requires caution or avoidance in people with bleeding disorders or on anticoagulants (bruising and bleeding risk), over infected or broken skin, and near implanted devices. Electroacupuncture is generally avoided in people with pacemakers or implanted electronic devices. Verify the practitioner is licensed/registered and uses single-use sterile needles; reused or improperly sterilised needles carry infection risk. A new or changing headache pattern needs a medical diagnosis first.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. In trials it was studied both as an add-on to symptomatic treatment of attacks and as an alternative to drug prophylaxis; it does not replace acute rescue medication.

What the evidence says

PREVENTIVE evidence is the stronger of the two literatures. The Cochrane review of 22 trials (4985 participants) found acupuncture reduced headache frequency versus no acupuncture (SMD -0.56, 95% CI -0.65 to -0.48), with headache frequency at least halved in 41% versus 17% of participants (RR 2.40, 2.08-2.76; NNTB 4), rated moderate quality. Versus sham acupuncture the pooled effect was small but statistically significant (SMD -0.18, -0.28 to -0.08; responders 50% vs 41%, RR 1.23, 1.11-1.36; NNTB 11). Versus prophylactic drug treatment, acupuncture was modestly better after treatment (SMD -0.25) though not maintained at follow-up, with fewer dropouts due to adverse effects (OR 0.27, 0.08-0.86). Three large sham-controlled trials counter the specific-effect claim: the German GERAC trial randomized 960 patients and found reductions of 2.3 days (verum), 1.5 days (sham) and 2.1 days (standard drug therapy), not significantly different across groups (p=0.09), with responder rates of 47%, 39% and 40% (p=0.133); a 302-patient trial found identical 2.2-day reductions in the acupuncture and sham arms (difference 0.0 days, 95% CI -0.7 to 0.7, P=.96) while both beat a waiting list; and a 2025 four-centre trial of 198 patients with migraine without aura, comparing penetrating with non-penetrating needling at the SAME acupoints, missed its primary endpoint (difference in attack-frequency change at 16 weeks -0.6, 95% CI -1.5 to 0.05, p=0.069), though responder rate (risk difference 17.2%, 5.2-29.1, p=0.007) and pain reduction did favour real needling. A 249-patient trial did find true acupuncture superior to sham at 16 weeks (difference 1.1 attacks, 0.4-1.9, P=.002), with sham not significantly different from waiting list. For ACUTE attacks, a separate 2023 meta-analysis (21 RCTs, 1926 participants) found acupuncture may increase headache freedom at 2 hours versus sham (RR 6.03, 1.62-22.41, low certainty) but showed little to no difference versus pharmacological therapy (RR 0.95, 0.59-1.52, low certainty); certainty across acute outcomes was low to very low.

Doses studied: Preventive trials used a minimum of 8 weeks. Typical regimens: 10 sessions over 6 weeks (GERAC), 12 sessions over 8 weeks, or 20 sessions delivered 5 days per week over 4 weeks. Acute-attack trials used a single session, typically 10-30 minutes.

Why this grade: The Cochrane review of 22 trials (4985 participants) provides moderate-quality evidence that acupuncture reduces migraine frequency compared with no acupuncture (NNTB 4) and is at least as effective as prophylactic drugs with fewer adverse effects. The honest problem is the sham comparison: the pooled effect over sham acupuncture is real but small (SMD -0.18; 50% vs 41% responders; NNTB 11), and three separate large sham-controlled trials — GERAC (n=960), Linde 2005 (n=302) and a 2025 four-centre trial (n=198) — all failed to show a significant advantage for real needling on their primary outcome. That pattern means most of the observed benefit is not specific to correct point selection or skin penetration. Acute-attack evidence is separate and rated low to very low certainty. Moderate, not strong, because the specific effect is small and the largest rigorous sham-controlled trials are null on their primary endpoints.

For insomnia

Acupuncture for sleep has been studied a lot, and most studies look encouraging - until you look closely at what fake needling does on its own. Pooling 36 trials, researchers found that sham acupuncture by itself made people rate their sleep meaningfully better, while sleep-lab and wrist-monitor measurements didn't budge. Real needling still beats doing nothing, and one review found it beat sham too, but the best-designed reviews say we can't be confident the needles themselves are doing the work. Where it was tested head-to-head against CBT-I, the standard talk-therapy approach, CBT-I came out slightly ahead.

Safety

Generally well tolerated when performed by a licensed practitioner with single-use sterile needles; reported adverse events across trials are mild and short-lived (bruising/hematoma, needling pain, headache, minor bleeding). Adverse-event reporting has historically been poor, and the 2025 Cochrane review is a useful corrective: it found more adverse events with acupuncture than with sham (RR 2.60, 95% CI 0.98 to 6.90) and than with inactive control (RR 15.49, 95% CI 2.12 to 113.10), both very low certainty. Rare serious harms (pneumothorax, nerve injury, infection) are associated with untrained or unlicensed practice. The practical risk for most people is opportunity cost and expense: the highest-certainty comparison to date found acupuncture slightly less effective than CBT-I, which remains the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, and a course of acupuncture is a repeated, paid, in-person commitment.

Who should be careful: Caution with bleeding disorders or anticoagulant therapy; avoid needling over infected, inflamed or numb skin, or into lymphedematous limbs. Electroacupuncture is generally avoided in people with pacemakers or other implanted electrical devices. Certain points are traditionally avoided in pregnancy - tell the practitioner if you are or may be pregnant. Not a substitute for evaluation of untreated sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, or insomnia driven by an untreated medical or psychiatric condition.

Interactions: No known pharmacological interactions, since nothing is ingested. The relevant interaction is clinical rather than chemical: do not stop or reduce a prescribed sleep or psychiatric medication in order to try acupuncture without your prescriber's involvement, as several trials used acupuncture specifically as a comparator to hypnotics such as estazolam.

What the evidence says

Evidence for acupuncture in insomnia is extensive but of consistently low certainty, and the picture depends heavily on the comparator. Against pharmacotherapy, a 2021 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (n=1,475) reported a pooled PSQI effect of -0.74 (95% CI -1.07 to -0.40) with very high heterogeneity (I-squared = 89%) and no significant effect before week 3. A 2022 network meta-analysis of 57 RCTs (4,678 patients) found multiple acupuncture modalities superior to usual treatment on PSQI, but rated certainty moderate-to-low and stated that differences between modalities were small or insignificant. Against sham, the literature is split. A 2020 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (1,108 patients) reported acupuncture superior to sham/placebo on PSQI, ISI, total sleep time, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset and sleep efficiency, though with high heterogeneity and subgroup effects as large as -7.34 PSQI points (I-squared = 86%) that are difficult to credit. Against that, a 140-participant double-blind sham-controlled trial found no significant difference on its primary outcome (sleep-diary sleep efficiency), with only a short-lived total-sleep-time gain of about 22 minutes at 1 week post-treatment, and the 2012 Cochrane review of 33 trials (2,293 participants) concluded that poor methodological quality, heterogeneity and likely publication bias meant the evidence could neither support nor refute acupuncture for insomnia. A 2025 Cochrane review in people with cancer (5 studies, 402 participants) found acupuncture may have little to no effect on insomnia severity versus sham (ISI mean difference -3.17, 95% CI -10.39 to 4.05) and probably performs slightly worse than CBT-I (ISI +2.60, 95% CI 1.13 to 4.07; moderate-certainty evidence). The most useful interpretive finding is a 2026 meta-analysis of 36 RCTs examining the sham arms alone: sham acupuncture by itself significantly reduced PSQI by 1.38 points (95% CI 0.90 to 1.86) and ISI by 2.58 points (95% CI 1.46 to 3.69), with no significant change on polysomnography or actigraphy - indicating that a substantial share of the subjective benefit reported across this literature reflects the treatment ritual rather than needle placement.

Doses studied: Regimens in the trials cited here: 10 sessions over 3 weeks at bilateral HT 7 and KI 7 (Wang 2021); 4 weeks of semi-individualized traditional acupuncture (Yeung 2021). Across the pharmacotherapy-comparison meta-analysis, subgroup analysis found no significant effect at weeks 1 and 2, with significance emerging only from week 3 onward - so short courses appear inadequate. The 57-trial network meta-analysis pooled many distinct modalities (manual acupuncture, electroacupuncture, auricular acupressure, intradermal needling, acupoint catgut embedding), which are not interchangeable.

Why this grade: Dozens of RCTs and several meta-analyses exist (one network meta-analysis pooled 57 trials / 4,678 patients), and pooled results favor acupuncture over usual treatment and over sleeping pills - so this is not a thin or preliminary evidence base. But GRADE certainty is rated low-to-very-low throughout, the two Cochrane reviews came back inconclusive or null against sham, the highest-certainty head-to-head found acupuncture slightly worse than CBT-I, and a 36-trial meta-analysis of sham arms shows sham needling alone moves subjective sleep scores substantially while moving polysomnography and actigraphy not at all. Sham-controlled trials themselves disagree: one 2020 meta-analysis found acupuncture clearly superior to sham, while a double-blind sham-controlled trial found no difference on its primary outcome. Extensive but low-certainty and internally contradictory is the definition of moderate, not strong.
