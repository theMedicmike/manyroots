Triptans (acute attack treatment)

Strong evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

Triptans are prescription medicines you take when a migraine attack has already started - not every day. They are the best-studied acute migraine drugs there are, and they clearly beat a dummy pill: in pooled trials, for roughly every 5 people who take zolmitriptan 2.5 mg, one extra person is completely pain-free at two hours who would not have been on placebo. Be realistic about the ceiling, though - only about a fifth to a third of people end up pain-free at two hours, about a quarter get temporary tightness or tingling in the chest, neck or jaw, and because triptans narrow blood vessels they are not suitable for people with heart disease or a history of stroke.

Safety

Adverse effects are usually transient and mild but are consistently more frequent than placebo, with a clear dose-response (Cochrane zolmitriptan 1-10 mg); a JAMA review puts transient upper-body flushing, tightness or tingling at about 25% of patients. Typical effects include chest, throat, neck or jaw tightness or pressure, paraesthesia, flushing, dizziness, somnolence and nausea. Because triptans are vasoconstrictors, chest symptoms should never be assumed benign in someone with cardiac risk factors. Frequent use is associated with medication-overuse headache: guidance generally caps acute triptan use at fewer than 10 days per month, although in the AMPP cohort triptans - unlike barbiturates and opioids - were not linked to progression to chronic migraine. Injectable sumatriptan adds injection-site reactions. Cardiovascular outcome data in real-world use come from only four observational studies and are inconclusive, especially for stroke.

Who should be careful: Contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease, coronary artery vasospasm (including Prinzmetal angina), prior myocardial infarction, prior stroke or TIA, peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, and severe hepatic impairment. Product labelling also contraindicates use in hemiplegic and brainstem-aura (basilar-type) migraine. Not established in pregnancy - discuss with a clinician. People with cardiovascular disease were excluded from the efficacy trials, so the evidence base does not cover them, and review guidance is that people with or at high risk of cardiovascular disease should avoid triptans.

Interactions: Do not combine with ergotamine or another triptan within 24 hours (additive vasoconstriction). Sumatriptan, rizatriptan and zolmitriptan are contraindicated with, or within 2 weeks of, MAO-A inhibitors. Rizatriptan requires dose reduction with propranolol. Labelling carries a serotonin syndrome warning for combination with SSRIs and SNRIs, although the practical risk is debated. Eletriptan is affected by strong CYP3A4 inhibitors.

What the evidence says

Triptans are 5-HT1B/1D agonists licensed for ACUTE attacks only; they have no established preventive role and are not taken daily. A 2024 network meta-analysis of 137 double-blind RCTs (89,445 participants) found every licensed oral acute drug superior to placebo for 2-hour pain freedom, with eletriptan highest (OR 5.19, 95% CI 4.25-6.33) and naratriptan lowest (OR 1.73, 1.27-2.34); eletriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan and zolmitriptan had the best head-to-head profiles and also outperformed lasmiditan, rimegepant and ubrogepant. A 2025 ACP-commissioned network meta-analysis (21 head-to-head and 165 placebo-controlled trials) rated the evidence that triptans beat NSAIDs as high certainty, while noting triptan regimens often carried a higher risk of adverse events. Cochrane reviews quantify the benefit and its limits: subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg gave an NNT of 2.1 for 2-hour headache relief but 6.1 for sustained pain freedom through 24 hours, and found no evidence that a second 6 mg dose helps after an inadequate response to the first; oral zolmitriptan 2.5 mg gave NNTs of 5.0 for 2-hour pain freedom and 3.2 for 2-hour headache relief, with adverse events showing a clear dose-response from 1 to 10 mg. The absolute ceiling is modest: a 2021 JAMA review puts complete pain elimination at two hours at 20-30% of patients, with transient upper-body flushing, tightness or tingling in about 25%. Cardiovascular safety evidence is thin rather than reassuring: pooling four observational studies gave an OR of 0.86 (0.52-1.43) for serious ischemic events with intense triptan use versus 2.28 (1.18-4.41) for ergotamines, but the two stroke estimates conflicted (0.90 [0.64-1.26] vs 2.51 [1.10-5.71]) and were not pooled. In the AMPP population cohort of 8,219 people with episodic migraine, baseline triptan use was not associated with progression to transformed migraine (OR 1.25, 0.9-1.7), unlike barbiturates (2.06) or opioids (1.98).

Doses studied: Per-attack dosing. Doses confirmed in the retrieved reviews: subcutaneous sumatriptan 4, 6 or 8 mg (most data at 6 mg); oral sumatriptan 50-100 mg; oral zolmitriptan 2.5, 5 or 10 mg; zolmitriptan 5 mg nasal spray. The 2024 network meta-analysis compared licensed oral monotherapies and confirmed its main findings in a sensitivity analysis restricted to FDA-licensed doses.

Why this grade: Two independent 2024-2025 network meta-analyses (137 double-blind RCTs; and 21 head-to-head plus 165 placebo-controlled trials) alongside multiple Cochrane meta-analyses show consistent, replicated superiority over placebo for acute attacks, and the ACP review rates triptan superiority over NSAIDs as HIGH certainty - the strongest comparative evidence anywhere in migraine drug treatment. Graded strong for the ACUTE indication only; there is no preventive evidence base here, and the absolute benefit is partial - roughly 20-30% of people are pain-free at two hours, and about a quarter get upper-body tightness, tingling or flushing.

Sources (7)
  1. Comparative effects of drug interventions for the acute management of migraine episodes in adults: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Karlsson et al., 2024 (BMJ) · PMID 39293828 · positive

    137 double-blind RCTs, 89,445 participants, 17 active interventions. All active drugs beat placebo for 2-hour pain freedom; eletriptan highest (OR 5.19, 95% CI 4.25-6.33) down to naratriptan (OR 1.73, 1.27-2.34). In head-to-head comparisons eletriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan and zolmitriptan had the best profiles and were more efficacious than lasmiditan, rimegepant and ubrogepant. Findings held in a sensitivity analysis restricted to FDA-licensed doses. Authors flag that careful consideration should be given to patients with a high-risk cardiovascular profile.

  2. Pharmacologic Treatment of Acute Attacks of Episodic Migraine: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis for the American College of Physicians.
    Gartlehner et al., 2025 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 40096693 · positive

    21 head-to-head and 165 placebo-controlled trials. Triptans were more effective than NSAIDs (high certainty of evidence) and acetaminophen (low certainty) for pain outcomes at 2 hours and pain freedom up to 48 hours. Triptan plus NSAID combinations beat triptan monotherapy (moderate certainty). Triptan regimens, however, often had a higher risk for adverse events.

  3. Sumatriptan (subcutaneous route of administration) for acute migraine attacks in adults.
    Derry, Derry & Moore, 2012 (Cochrane) · PMID 22336869 · positive

    35 studies, 9,365 participants. Sumatriptan 6 mg SC vs placebo: NNTs of 2.9 and 2.3 for pain-free at one and two hours, and 2.2 and 2.1 for headache relief at one and two hours, but 6.1 for sustained pain-free at 24 h. Notable null within the review: no evidence of increased relief from a second 6 mg dose after an inadequate response to the first. Adverse events were more common with sumatriptan than placebo.

  4. Zolmitriptan for acute migraine attacks in adults.
    Bird, Derry & Moore, 2014 (Cochrane) · PMID 24848613 · positive

    25 studies, 20,162 participants. Oral zolmitriptan 2.5 mg vs placebo: NNT 5.0 (2-hour pain-free), 3.2 (2-hour headache relief), 7.7 (sustained pain-free over 24 h). 10 mg beat 5 mg for pain-free and headache relief at two hours, but adverse events showed a clear dose-response across 1-10 mg. Zolmitriptan 2.5 mg and 5 mg gave 2-hour headache relief to the same proportion as sumatriptan 50 mg (66%, 67%, 68%), though not necessarily the same individuals.

  5. Diagnosis and Management of Headache: A Review.
    Robbins, 2021 (JAMA) · PMID 33974014 · mixed

    Narrative review. States that triptans eliminate pain in 20% to 30% of patients by two hours - so most people treated are not pain-free - and are accompanied by adverse effects such as transient flushing, tightness or tingling in the upper body in 25% of patients. States that patients with, or at high risk for, cardiovascular disease should avoid triptans because of their vasoconstrictive properties. For comparison, acute gepants (rimegepant, ubrogepant) eliminate headache at two hours in about 20%.

  6. Adverse cardiovascular events associated with triptans and ergotamines for treatment of migraine: systematic review of observational studies.
    Roberto et al., 2015 (Cephalalgia; e-published 2014) · PMID 25246519 · null

    Only 4 eligible observational studies from 3,370 citations. Pooled OR for serious ischemic events with intense use was 0.86 (95% CI 0.52-1.43) for triptans versus 2.28 (1.18-4.41) for ergotamines. The two stroke estimates for recent triptan use conflicted (OR 0.90 [0.64-1.26] and 2.51 [1.10-5.71]) and were too heterogeneous to pool. Authors conclude no firm conclusions can be drawn and residual confounding reduces confidence in the estimates.

  7. Acute migraine medications and evolution from episodic to chronic migraine: a longitudinal population-based study.
    Bigal et al., 2008 (Headache) · PMID 18808500 · null

    8,219 people with episodic migraine followed one year in the AMPP study; 209 (2.5%) developed transformed migraine. Compared with acetaminophen users, baseline triptan use was NOT associated with prospective risk (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.9-1.7), whereas barbiturate-containing medications (OR 2.06, 1.3-3.1) and opioids (OR 1.98, 1.4-2.2) were, with a dose-response for barbiturates.
