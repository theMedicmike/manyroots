Minerals & nutrients

Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA)

Moderate evidence · for anxiety & stress Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure Not supported by evidence · for chronic pain

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Omega-3s are the healthy fats found in oily fish like salmon and in fish-oil capsules. The research genuinely disagrees with itself: reviews focused on people who already have a health condition, using higher doses of around 2 grams a day, find a modest easing of anxiety, while the largest review of all, covering more than 41,000 people at more typical doses, found little or no effect on preventing anxiety symptoms. Omega-3s are a sensible part of a good diet and are generally well tolerated, but they are not a dependable answer for anxiety.

Safety

Well tolerated in the anxiety trials: the 2024 dose-response meta-analysis found no increase in adverse events versus placebo (OR 1.20; 95% CI 0.89-1.61), rated GRADE moderate. The common minor effects reported with fish oil generally are fishy aftertaste, burping, loose stools and stomach upset, which tend to be dose-related and can be reduced by taking capsules with food.

Who should be careful: People with fish or shellfish allergy should choose a product verified free of allergenic protein, or an algae-derived omega-3. Anyone with a bleeding disorder, or scheduled for surgery, should discuss high-dose fish oil with their clinician beforehand. People with atrial fibrillation or at high risk of it should review multi-gram omega-3 use with a clinician, since cardiovascular trials of high-dose prescription omega-3 have raised a question about atrial fibrillation risk. Note that this atrial fibrillation caution is a product-label and cardiovascular-trial concern rather than a finding from any of the anxiety studies cited above.

Interactions: High doses may have additive effects with anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs such as warfarin, aspirin and clopidogrel, potentially increasing bleeding tendency; this is a label-level caution rather than a finding from the anxiety trials cited here, and monitoring is prudent when combining them. In the meta-analysis of omega-3 added to sertraline, a subgroup analysis of depression outcomes led the authors to suggest it may be better not to provide the supplementation in patients with coronary disease, though that subgroup estimate was itself non-significant and of very low certainty.

What the evidence says

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis (Su et al.; 19 studies, 1,203 omega-3-treated and 1,037 control participants, mean dose 1,605.7 mg/day) found omega-3 treatment associated with reduced anxiety symptoms versus controls (Hedges g=0.374; 95% CI 0.081-0.666; P=.01), with significant effects only in subgroups with clinical diagnoses and at doses of at least 2,000 mg/day; it pooled both placebo-controlled and non-placebo-controlled designs. A 2024 dose-response meta-analysis (Bafkar et al.; 23 trials, 2,189 participants) estimated an SMD of -0.70 (95% CI -1.17 to -0.22; GRADE low) per 1 g/day, with the greatest improvement at 2 g/day (SMD -0.93) and no effect below 2 g/day, rating overall certainty very low and finding no increase in adverse events (OR 1.20; 95% CI 0.89-1.61). In a 12-week placebo-controlled RCT of 68 healthy medical students, 2.5 g/day of omega-3 produced a 20% reduction in anxiety symptoms and a 14% decrease in LPS-stimulated IL-6 production versus placebo, with no significant change in depressive symptoms. Cutting the other way, the largest synthesis to date (Deane et al. 2021; 31 long-chain omega-3 trials, 41,470 participants) concluded that increasing long-chain omega-3 probably has little or no effect on anxiety symptoms (SMD 0.15; 95% CI 0.05-0.26; I2=0%; median dose 1.1 g/day over 6 months), rated moderate-quality evidence, and a 2020 meta-analysis of omega-3 added to sertraline found no effect on anxiety (Beck Anxiety Inventory pooled MD 0.03; 95% CI -2.22 to 2.28). The 2022 WFSBP/CANMAT guideline gave omega-3 its strongest recommendation for adjunctive use in unipolar depression but identified no nutraceutical, omega-3 included, with sufficient evidence in any anxiety-related disorder apart from N-acetyl cysteine in OCD-related disorders. The picture for anxiety is therefore mixed: a modest signal at higher doses in clinical populations, and little or no effect for prevention at typical doses.

Doses studied: Roughly 1-2.5 g/day of combined EPA+DHA for 8-12 weeks in most anxiety trials, with meta-analytic signals appearing only at 2 g/day or more (mean dose 1,605.7 mg/day in the 2018 meta-analysis). The large null synthesis pooled trials of at least 24 weeks at a median 1.1 g/day.

Why this grade: Meta-analytic RCT evidence exists and is genuinely split. Two meta-analyses find benefit: Su 2018 (19 studies, n=2,240; Hedges g=0.374, significant only in clinically diagnosed subgroups and at doses of at least 2,000 mg/day, pooling both placebo-controlled and non-placebo-controlled designs) and Bafkar 2024 (23 trials, n=2,189; SMD -0.70 per 1 g/day, GRADE low, overall certainty very low). Against them, the largest synthesis to date, Deane 2021, pooled 31 long-chain omega-3 trials in 41,470 participants and concluded omega-3 probably has little or no effect on anxiety symptoms at a median 1.1 g/day, and that conclusion carries moderate-quality GRADE certainty, higher than either positive meta-analysis. Omega-3 added to sertraline was null for anxiety, and the 2022 WFSBP/CANMAT taskforce endorsed no nutraceutical, omega-3 included, in any anxiety-related disorder. This is meta-analytic evidence that is on average positive but modest and mixed, with the benefit confined to higher doses in clinical populations: moderate, not strong, and sitting close to the boundary with a lower grade.

For depression & low mood

Fish oil is the most-studied supplement for low mood, and the honest picture is mixed. Trials of the EPA form, at roughly 1 to 2 grams a day, show a small average improvement in depression questionnaire scores — but no analysis has shown that more people actually respond or recover, and when researchers correct for the fact that positive studies get published more often, most of that improvement disappears. In a five-year trial of more than 18,000 older adults who were not depressed to begin with, fish oil did not prevent depression, and slightly more people taking it became depressed than those taking placebo.

Safety

Generally well tolerated. The most common complaints are fishy aftertaste or burping, stomach upset, loose stools, and nausea. In the Cochrane review, adverse events were similar between omega-3 and placebo groups (OR 1.27, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.64), and a 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies likewise found no difference in adverse events (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.29). In VITAL-DEP, rates over a median 5.3 years were comparable for gastrointestinal bleeding (2.6% omega-3 vs 2.7% placebo), easy bruising (24.8% vs 25.1%), and stomach upset or pain (35.2% vs 35.1%). The clearest signal of harm is atrial fibrillation: pooled cardiovascular trials in 81,210 patients show a 25% relative increase, rising to roughly 49% in trials testing above 1 g/day. Omega-3 is not a substitute for antidepressant medication or psychotherapy, and nobody should stop a prescribed treatment to try it. In bipolar depression, omega-3 has been studied mainly as an add-on rather than a stand-alone, and any supplement change should be made with the prescribing clinician because of mood-switch risk in that population.

Who should be careful: Avoid or use only under medical supervision if you have atrial fibrillation or a history of it, or a known fish or shellfish allergy (an algal-oil source is an alternative). Stop before planned surgery per your surgeon's instructions. Doses above 1 g/day carry the larger atrial fibrillation signal.

Interactions: May add to the effect of warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, aspirin and other antiplatelet drugs; discuss with the prescriber before starting. Very high doses can raise LDL cholesterol in some people. If you take antiarrhythmic medication, raise omega-3 with your cardiologist first.

What the evidence says

The 2021 Cochrane review of 35 RCTs found a small-to-modest benefit of n-3 PUFA over placebo for depressive symptoms in adults with MDD (SMD -0.40, 95% CI -0.64 to -0.16; 33 studies, 1,848 participants), equivalent to about 2.5 HDRS-17 points against a 3.0-point minimal clinically important difference, at very low certainty, with no difference in remission (OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.74 to 1.72) or response (OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.79), and reviewers judged the estimate possibly biased toward a positive finding. Three further meta-analyses reproduce the same pattern of a small significant effect on symptom scales concentrated in EPA-predominant formulas — SMD -0.28 across 26 studies (2,160 participants), SMD -0.36 for EPA at 60% or more of total EPA+DHA (SMD -0.43 at 1 to under 2 g/day of EPA; SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.48 to 0.07 and non-significant at 2 g/day or more), and SMD -0.26 across 36 studies — but that 36-study analysis found no difference from placebo in response (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.20) or remission (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.48). An earlier meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (731 participants) found no significant benefit at all (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.26) and reported that nearly all apparent efficacy was removed by trim-and-fill adjustment for publication bias (SMD 0.01, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.15), with larger apparent effects in shorter, lower-quality, completer-analysed trials. The largest synthesis, covering 31 long-chain omega-3 trials and 41,470 participants, concluded at moderate-quality evidence that supplementation probably has little or no effect on risk of depression symptoms (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.10). Consistently, a 196-participant two-site RCT found neither EPA-enriched nor DHA-enriched omega-3 at 1,000 mg/day superior to placebo over 8 weeks (response 40-50% in all arms), and in VITAL-DEP (18,353 adults aged 50+, median 5.3 years) 1 g/day marine omega-3 was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in depression risk (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.26).

Doses studied: Meta-analytic benefit is concentrated in EPA-predominant preparations: EPA at 60% or more of total EPA+DHA (SMD -0.36), at EPA doses of 1 to under 2 g/day (SMD -0.43), while EPA at 2 g/day or more was not significant (SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.48 to 0.07). A separate meta-analysis found benefit for pure-EPA and EPA-major formulas only at an EPA dose of 1 g/day or less. A 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies suggested 1,000-1,500 mg/day total omega-3 at an EPA:DHA ratio between 1:1 and 2:1 for over 8 weeks as the most favourable profile, while still finding no benefit on response or remission. Null trials used 1,000 mg/day of EPA-enriched or DHA-enriched oil (Mischoulon 2015) and 1 g/day fish oil providing 465 mg EPA + 375 mg DHA (VITAL-DEP); the 41,470-participant prevention synthesis had a median dose of 0.95 g/day over 12 months.

Why this grade: The trial base is large, but the signal is small, fragile, and does not reach the outcomes that matter clinically. Four independent meta-analyses find a small significant reduction in depression scores favouring EPA-predominant preparations (SMD -0.40, -0.36, -0.28, -0.26), which is why this is not graded lower. Against that: no meta-analysis shows any benefit on response or remission (Cochrane OR 1.20 and OR 1.13, both non-significant; Kong 2025 RR 0.99 and RR 1.17); Cochrane rated certainty very low, judged the pooled effect unlikely to be clinically meaningful, and concluded the estimate may be biased toward a positive finding; an earlier meta-analysis found nearly all apparent efficacy vanished after trim-and-fill adjustment for publication bias; the largest synthesis in the field (31 trials, 41,470 participants) found little or no effect at moderate certainty; and the two largest well-conducted trials were null (Mischoulon 2015) and negative (VITAL-DEP). Heterogeneity runs 86-88% and funnel plots are asymmetric across reviews. Graded down from strong on certainty and on the absence of any response or remission benefit; not graded to early because the volume of trials is substantial rather than preliminary.

For high blood pressure

Fish oil capsules shift blood pressure a little - about 1 to 3 mmHg in most trials, more in people with untreated high blood pressure. Followed over years, the very large trials showed no change in overall deaths or strokes, and only a small and uncertain reduction in heart-disease events. There is also a consistent signal that they raise the risk of an irregular heartbeat called atrial fibrillation, and that risk grows with the dose - including the 2 to 3 grams a day used in the blood pressure studies. If you take them, the honest framing is a small effect on a number, with a small and uncertain effect on events and a real trade-off.

Safety

The Cochrane review found increasing long-chain omega-3 had little or no effect on serious adverse events overall, and little or no effect on arrhythmia as a general category (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92-1.06). The clearest specific harm is atrial fibrillation: pooled across seven long-term cardiovascular outcome trials, supplementation raised the risk by about 25%, and by about 49% at doses above 1 g/day, with risk rising roughly 11% per additional gram per day. This matters directly in hypertension, since high blood pressure is itself a driver of atrial fibrillation. Note also that the blood-pressure doses studied (2-3 g/day) sit in the range where the arrhythmia signal is strongest.

Who should be careful: Anyone with a history of atrial fibrillation, or at elevated risk of it, should discuss omega-3 supplementation with a clinician before starting, given the dose-related increase in atrial fibrillation seen across cardiovascular outcome trials. People with fish or shellfish allergy should avoid marine-sourced products.

Interactions: Higher-dose omega-3 can prolong bleeding time, so combining it with anticoagulants (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants), antiplatelet drugs or regular NSAIDs warrants medical advice, particularly before surgery. Effects on blood pressure may add to those of antihypertensive medication.

What the evidence says

Evidence for omega-3 fatty acids in hypertension is a case where a surrogate moves clearly and the outcomes move only slightly. Pooling 70 RCTs, EPA plus DHA reduced systolic pressure by 1.52 mmHg (95% CI -2.25 to -0.79) and diastolic by 0.99 mmHg (95% CI -1.54 to -0.44) overall, with the largest effects in untreated hypertensive participants (-4.51/-3.05 mmHg) and much smaller effects in normotensive participants (-1.25/-0.62 mmHg). A dose-response meta-analysis of 71 trials (4973 individuals) found a J-shaped curve with the optimum between 2 g/day (-2.61 systolic, -1.64 diastolic) and 3 g/day (-2.61 systolic, -1.80 diastolic), and stronger, more linear relations in hypertensive, hyperlipidaemic and older subgroups. Against this, the Cochrane review of 86 RCTs and 162,796 participants found little or no effect of increasing long-chain omega-3 on all-cause mortality (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.93-1.01; high certainty), cardiovascular events (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92-1.01; high certainty), stroke (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.94-1.12) or arrhythmia (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92-1.06), and reported little or no effect on blood pressure; it did find small low-certainty reductions in coronary heart disease events (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85-0.97, NNTB 167) and coronary heart disease mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.81-1.00, NNTB 334), a moderate-certainty reduction in cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86-0.99), and a high-certainty dose-dependent triglyceride reduction of about 15%. In the Trials of Hypertension Prevention Phase 1, fish oil produced no significant blood pressure change in 2182 adults with high-normal pressure at 6 or 18 months. A meta-analysis of 7 cardiovascular outcome RCTs (81,210 participants, weighted mean follow-up 4.9 years) found marine omega-3 supplementation increased atrial fibrillation risk (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.07-1.46), with a stronger effect above 1 g/day (HR 1.49) than at or below 1 g/day (HR 1.12).

Doses studied: Blood-pressure trials used combined EPA+DHA doses centring on about 2.8 g/day (interquartile range 1.3-3.6 g/day); dose-response modelling puts the optimum for blood pressure at 2-3 g/day, and diastolic reductions in the largest meta-analysis required at least 2 g/day. Cardiovascular outcome trials in the Cochrane review used 0.5 g/day to more than 5 g/day, with 19 trials giving at least 3 g/day. The atrial fibrillation signal was present even at 1 g/day or less (about 12% increased risk) and larger above 1 g/day (about 49% increased risk).

Why this grade: Large meta-analyses of randomised trials consistently show a small blood-pressure reduction - roughly 1.5 mmHg systolic overall, rising to about 2.6 mmHg at 2-3 g/day and around 4.5 mmHg in untreated hypertensive participants. That is a real, replicated signal on the cuff reading. Set against it, the Cochrane review of 86 RCTs and 162,796 participants concluded that increasing long-chain omega-3 has little or no effect on all-cause mortality or cardiovascular events (both high certainty) or stroke, and specifically little or no effect on blood pressure; the same review did find small, low-certainty reductions in coronary heart disease events and mortality and a moderate-certainty reduction in cardiovascular mortality, so the outcome picture is modest rather than flatly null. There is also a recognised harm signal: a meta-analysis of 81,210 participants in cardiovascular outcome trials found supplementation increased atrial fibrillation risk in a dose-dependent way, although the Cochrane review found little or no effect on arrhythmia overall. Moderate rather than strong because the surrogate benefit is small and disputed, the outcome benefits are small and low-certainty, and a dose-related harm sits in the same dose range used for blood pressure.

    Across 86 RCTs (162,796 participants), increasing long-chain omega-3 had little or no effect on all-cause mortality (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.93-1.01; high certainty), cardiovascular events (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92-1.01; high certainty), stroke (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.94-1.12) or arrhythmia (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92-1.06), and little or no effect on blood pressure or serious adverse events; it may slightly reduce coronary heart disease mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.81-1.00, NNTB 334, low certainty) and coronary heart disease events (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85-0.97, NNTB 167, low certainty), probably slightly reduces cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86-0.99, moderate certainty), and reduces triglycerides by about 15% in a dose-dependent way (high certainty).

  4. Effect of Long-Term Marine ɷ-3 Fatty Acids Supplementation on the Risk of Atrial Fibrillation in Randomized Controlled Trials of Cardiovascular Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Meta-analysisGencer et al., 2021 · PMID 34612056 · negative

    Across 7 cardiovascular outcome RCTs (81,210 participants, weighted mean follow-up 4.9 years), marine omega-3 supplementation was associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.07-1.46), with greater risk in trials testing more than 1 g/day (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.04-2.15) than 1 g/day or less (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.03-1.22); meta-regression found the hazard ratio rose by 1.11 (95% CI 1.06-1.15) per additional gram per day.

  5. Efficacy of nonpharmacologic interventions in adults with high-normal blood pressure: results from phase 1 of the Trials of Hypertension Prevention. Trials of Hypertension Prevention Collaborative Research Group.
    Randomised trialWhelton et al., 1997 (TOHP Phase 1) · PMID 9022561 · null

    In 2182 adults with diastolic pressure 80-89 mmHg, fish oil supplementation produced no significant blood pressure reduction at 6 or 18 months; only weight loss (-2.9/-2.4 mmHg) and sodium reduction (-2.1/-1.2 mmHg) lowered pressure significantly.

For chronic pain

Fish oil is one of the most popular supplements for aching joints, and the idea behind it — calming inflammation — is a reasonable one. But when researchers ran the biggest and most careful trials in people with long-standing knee pain, the people taking omega-3 got better at almost exactly the same rate as the people taking a dummy capsule. Smaller earlier studies were more encouraging, so this is not a closed question, but on current evidence you should not expect fish oil to meaningfully reduce chronic joint or back pain.

Safety

Generally well tolerated at supplement doses. In the 2024 krill oil trial, adverse events occurred at similar rates on active treatment and placebo (51% vs 54%), and in the 2023 osteoarthritis meta-analysis overall adverse event rates did not differ from placebo (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.64-1.45). The most common complaints in practice are fishy aftertaste, belching and loose stools. A 2022 meta-analysis of 15 cardiovascular outcome trials (Yan et al.) found an increased incidence of atrial fibrillation with omega-3 supplementation (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.10-1.41), which is the most clinically meaningful signal to weigh against a benefit that has not been demonstrated for pain.

Who should be careful: Avoid in people with fish or shellfish allergy; krill oil is crustacean-derived. Discuss with a clinician before use if you have atrial fibrillation or a history of it, given the signal for increased AF incidence, or if you are scheduled for surgery.

Interactions: Caution with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, aspirin, clopidogrel); the same 2022 cardiovascular meta-analysis found no overall excess of bleeding-related disorders with supplemental omega-3 but flagged a higher bleeding risk specifically with prescription EPA ethyl ester formulations. Tell your clinician you are taking it before any procedure.

What the evidence says

The best available evidence for omega-3 fatty acids in chronic musculoskeletal pain is null. In a multicentre Australian trial (n=262 with clinical knee osteoarthritis, significant knee pain and MRI effusion-synovitis), 2 g/day krill oil for 24 weeks produced a mean VAS pain change of -19.9 versus -20.2 for placebo, a between-group difference of -0.3 (95% CI -6.9 to 6.4), against a stated minimum clinically important improvement of 15 points. In the VITAL randomised trial sub-study, 1,398 US adults with chronic knee pain followed for a mean 5.3 years showed no difference in WOMAC pain between marine omega-3 and placebo (time-by-treatment interaction P=0.77), and no effect on function or stiffness. A 2-year dose-ranging trial (n=202) found high-dose fish oil (4.5 g omega-3) was not superior to low-dose (0.45 g), with the low-dose comparator actually showing greater WOMAC pain and function improvement at 2 years and no difference in cartilage volume loss. Against this, a 2023 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (2,070 patients) reported a small significant pooled pain benefit (SMD -0.29, 95% CI -0.47 to -0.11, I-squared 60%) and improved function (SMD -0.21, 95% CI -0.34 to -0.07), and a 6-month krill oil trial (n=235) found a modest WOMAC pain advantage of -5.18 points on a 0-100 scale (95% CI -10.0 to -0.32). Evidence in chronic low back pain is effectively absent: the only relevant trial combined 149 mg EPA with 594 mg L-serine, so any benefit cannot be attributed to omega-3.

Doses studied: Fish oil providing 0.45 g/day (low dose) to 4.5 g/day (high dose) of omega-3 fatty acids over 2 years; krill oil 2 g/day and 4 g/day (the 4 g/day product delivered 0.60 g EPA, 0.28 g DHA and 0.45 g astaxanthin daily).

Why this grade: Adequate, independently funded trials in the target condition are null. The two largest and most rigorous studies in chronic knee osteoarthritis pain — the VITAL sub-study (1,398 adults, mean 5.3 years) and the 2024 JAMA krill oil trial (262 adults, with a stated minimum clinically important improvement of 15 points on the primary endpoint) — both found no difference from placebo, and a 2-year dose-ranging trial found no advantage of high-dose over low-dose fish oil. A 2023 meta-analysis of 9 mostly smaller RCTs did find a small pooled benefit (SMD -0.29), which is why this is not graded as flatly harmful, but that estimate is a small standardised effect, is heterogeneous (I-squared 60%), and was published before the largest null trial. The evidence base is almost entirely in knee osteoarthritis; there is essentially no direct trial evidence for omega-3 alone in chronic low back pain or chronic widespread pain, and evidence from other joint conditions is not evidence for this node. Graded down per the conservative rule.
