Fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum)

Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Fenugreek seeds, a common curry spice, have been tested in a handful of small studies in people with type 2 diabetes and appear to lower blood sugar modestly, and one 3-year Indian study found people with prediabetes who ate 10 grams a day were less likely to develop diabetes. These studies are small and not very rigorous, so treat the result as promising rather than proven, and as something to add alongside your usual care rather than in place of any medication you have been prescribed. Fenugreek is generally well tolerated, but it is a legume related to peanut and can trigger allergies, and it may increase the effect of blood thinners like warfarin.

Safety

Fenugreek is not a replacement for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and nothing here is a reason to stop or reduce a prescription. Trials report mild gastrointestinal side effects in some participants, with no hepatic or renal toxicity and no severe adverse events (Kim et al. 2023, PMID 37762302). A well-known effect is a maple-syrup odour of urine and sweat, harmless in itself but not always harmless in context - see the pregnancy and breastfeeding warning above, where the same odour in a newborn has triggered investigation for a metabolic disease. The Cochrane review of oral galactagogues lists that odour in mothers and urticaria in infants among the minor adverse effects reported across natural galactagogue trials as a group, of which fenugreek was one of many botanicals studied (Foong et al. 2020, PMID 32421208). Doses of 10 g/day were taken for 3 years without reported adverse effects in the prevention trial, but adverse-event monitoring in that study was not systematic.

Who should be careful: People with peanut or other legume allergy: fenugreek is a legume, cross-sensitization is common (fenugreek was the most frequent legume sensitization, 66.3%, among peanut-allergic children; Muller et al. 2022, PMID 36156816), and it is described as an emerging and often hidden allergen, with IgE-mediated anaphylaxis demonstrated in a mouse model (Vinje et al. 2011, PMID 21645030) and fenugreek listed among non-priority legume allergen sources capable of mild-to-severe reactions in people (Abu Risha et al. 2024, PMID 38990406). Pregnancy and breastfeeding: none of the retrieved trials included pregnant women and no controlled safety data exist, so medicinal doses cannot be called safe. There is also a specific documented consequence for a newborn - fenugreek gives urine and sweat a maple-syrup odour, because it shares the odour compound sotolone with maple syrup and with the urine of people who have maple syrup urine disease (Podebrad et al. 1999, PMID 10234605), and ingestion of fenugreek by mothers during labour has given their newborn infants that odour and led to a false suspicion of maple syrup urine disease, with the investigation that follows (Korman et al. 2001, PMID 11532065; a similar false diagnosis was reported after ingestion of a fenugreek-containing herbal tea, Sewell et al. 1999, PMID 10475807, and a scoping review lists maple-syrup odour in the urine, sweat or skin of infants and mothers among reported effects, Amiri Ardekani et al. 2026, PMID 41625343). If you are pregnant or breastfeeding and have taken fenugreek, tell the paediatric team so the odour is not misread. Type 1 diabetes: not studied.

Interactions: Warfarin: a published case report documented a rise in INR on warfarin while taking boldo and fenugreek, which recurred on re-challenge and was rated probable by the Naranjo algorithm (Lambert and Cormier 2001, PMID 11310527); fenugreek is listed among herbs that may potentiate warfarin in pharmacy and cardiology reviews (Heck et al. 2000, PMID 10902065; Izzo et al. 2005, PMID 15676159). Insulin and sulfonylureas: additive glucose lowering should be assumed, not merely considered. Where pooled trials found an effect - at medium or high doses in people with diabetes - it was 0.96 mmol/L off fasting glucose (95% CI -1.52 to -0.40) and 2.19 mmol/L off 2-hour post-load glucose (95% CI -3.19 to -1.19), a drug-sized change (Neelakantan et al. 2014, PMID 24438170), and the prevention trial used 10 g/day for three years (Gaddam et al. 2015, PMID 26436069). The most direct signal is the 12-week randomized trial in which fenugreek total saponins added on top of sulfonylureas lowered fasting glucose, 2-hour post-prandial glucose and HbA1c significantly more than control in 69 patients, with no difference in hepatic or renal function (Lu et al. 2008, PMID 18219452) - that trial reported no hypoglycaemia rates and was not designed to detect them. The true size is uncertain, since another pooled analysis found no significant fasting-glucose effect at all (Shabil et al. 2023, PMID 36837450), but hypoglycaemia is among the adverse effects reported for fenugreek in humans (Amiri Ardekani et al. 2026, PMID 41625343), so the safe assumption is that it adds to your medication. Monitor your glucose closely when starting or stopping fenugreek and have your doses reviewed by the prescriber - do not adjust them yourself.

What the evidence says

With one partial exception, every endpoint here is a surrogate: fasting glucose, post-load glucose, HbA1c. No fenugreek trial has measured cardiovascular events, kidney disease or death; the single trial reporting incident diabetes uses a diagnosis that is itself defined by glucose thresholds. In type 2 diabetes, Neelakantan et al. pooled 10 trials of at least one week: fasting glucose -0.96 mmol/L (95% CI -1.52 to -0.40, I2 80%), 2-hour post-load glucose -2.19 mmol/L, and HbA1c -0.85% (95% CI -1.49 to -0.22) from only three trials, with significant effects seen only at medium or high seed doses in people with diabetes and most trials of low methodological quality. Khodamoradi et al. (12 articles) found fasting glucose -12.94 mg/dL (I2 85%) and HbA1c -0.58%; Kim et al. (10 RCTs, 706 participants) found reductions in fasting glucose, 2-hour glucose and HbA1c but no significant change in HOMA-IR; and Shabil et al. (14 trials, 894 participants) found no significant change in fasting glucose (MD 3.70 mg/dL, 95% CI -27.02 to 19.62, P=0.76) or postprandial glucose (P=0.72) despite an HbA1c reduction of 0.88, flagging trial quality and heterogeneity as a concern. The better-controlled small trials are less impressive: 1 g/day extract for 2 months in 25 newly diagnosed patients left fasting and 2-hour glucose no different from placebo (though glucose and insulin AUC fell), and 2 g/day seed for 12 weeks with only 9 completers produced a non-significant fasting glucose drop (P=0.63) while fasting insulin rose. The one trial with a progression endpoint is in prediabetes: 5 g seed powder twice daily for 3 years (n=66 versus 74 controls) significantly reduced the cumulative incidence of diabetes, lowered fasting and postprandial glucose and LDL cholesterol and raised serum insulin, with controls 4.2 times more likely to develop diabetes and no adverse effects reported; the abstract does not describe blinding, and the result has not been replicated.

Doses studied: 5-10 g/day whole seed powder before meals (10 g/day for 3 years in the prediabetes prevention trial; 2 g/day in a small type 2 diabetes trial); 1 g/day hydroalcoholic seed extract for 2 months; standardized total-saponin capsules three times daily for 12 weeks as an add-on to sulfonylureas.

Why this grade: Four meta-analyses exist and point the same direction, but they pool small, short, mostly low-quality trials: the HbA1c estimate in the 2014 meta-analysis rests on only three trials, the 2023 meta-analysis found no significant effect on fasting or postprandial glucose despite an HbA1c signal, and the better-controlled small placebo-controlled trials found no between-group difference in fasting glucose. The one trial with any progression endpoint (3-year prevention of diabetes in prediabetes) is encouraging but small (140 people), does not describe blinding, and is unreplicated. Everything else is a surrogate marker and no trial has measured cardiovascular, kidney or mortality outcomes. Small, few and preliminary trials with a favourable trend is the definition of early.

Sources (10)
  1. Role of Fenugreek in the prevention of type 2 diabetes mellitus in prediabetes.
    Gaddam A, Galla C, Thummisetti S, Marikanty RK, Palanisamy UD, Rao PV, 2015 · PMID 26436069 · positive

    3-year randomized controlled parallel study in prediabetes (fenugreek n=66, controls n=74, aged 30-70): fenugreek powder 5 g twice daily before meals significantly reduced the cumulative incidence of diabetes and lowered fasting and postprandial plasma glucose and LDL cholesterol while raising serum insulin; controls had a 4.2 times higher chance of developing diabetes; no adverse effects reported. The abstract does not describe blinding or placebo control.

  2. Effect of fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum L.) intake on glycemia: a meta-analysis of clinical trials.
    Neelakantan N, Narayanan M, de Souza RJ, van Dam RM, 2014 · PMID 24438170 · positive

    10 trials of at least 1 week: fenugreek changed fasting blood glucose by -0.96 mmol/L (95% CI -1.52 to -0.40, I2 80%, 10 trials), 2-hour post-load glucose by -2.19 mmol/L (95% CI -3.19 to -1.19, 7 trials) and HbA1c by -0.85% (95% CI -1.49 to -0.22, I2 0%, only 3 trials); significant glucose effects were found only with medium or high doses in people with diabetes, and most trials were of low methodological quality.

  3. The Effects of Fenugreek on Cardiometabolic Risk Factors in Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Khodamoradi K, Khosropanah MH, Ayati Z, et al., 2020 · PMID 32951700 · positive

    12 articles pooled: fenugreek seed versus placebo reduced fasting blood sugar by 12.94 mg/dL (95% CI -21.39 to -4.49, I2 85%), HbA1c by 0.58% (95% CI -0.99 to -0.17, I2 0%), total cholesterol by 9.13 mg/dL and LDL-C by 11.11 mg/dL, with no significant change in other cardiometabolic parameters; the authors caution that high heterogeneity in glycaemic status means findings must be interpreted with great caution.

  4. The Effect of Fenugreek in Type 2 Diabetes and Prediabetes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Kim J, Noh W, Kim A, Choi Y, Kim YS, 2023 · PMID 37762302 · positive

    10 studies, 706 participants: fenugreek significantly reduced fasting blood glucose, 2-hour OGTT glucose and HbA1c, but did NOT significantly reduce HOMA-IR; total cholesterol, triglycerides and HDL-C improved while LDL-C and BMI did not; no hepatic or renal toxicity and no severe adverse events were observed, despite mild gastrointestinal side effects in some studies.

  5. Effect of Fenugreek on Hyperglycemia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Shabil M, Bushi G, Bodige PK, et al., 2023 · PMID 36837450 · mixed

    14 trials, 894 participants: no significant effect on fasting blood glucose (MD 3.70, 95% CI -27.02 to 19.62, P=0.76) or postprandial blood glucose (MD -10.61, 95% CI -68.48 to 47.26, P=0.72), while HbA1c fell (MD -0.88, 95% CI -1.49 to -0.27); the authors flag the quality and heterogeneity of the included trials as a concern and call for rigorous double-blinded RCTs.

  6. Effect of Trigonella foenum-graecum (fenugreek) seeds on glycaemic control and insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes mellitus: a double blind placebo controlled study.
    Gupta A, Gupta R, Lal B, 2001 · PMID 11868855 · mixed

    25 newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients randomized to 1 g/day hydroalcoholic fenugreek seed extract (n=12) or placebo plus usual care (n=13) for 2 months: fasting glucose (148.3 to 119.9 vs 137.5 to 113.0 mg/dL) and 2-hour post-glucose values were not different between groups, although glucose and insulin AUC were significantly lower (P<0.001), percent insulin sensitivity rose and triglycerides fell with fenugreek.

  7. A randomized controlled clinical trial evaluating the effect of Trigonella foenum-graecum (fenugreek) versus glibenclamide in patients with diabetes.
    Najdi RA, Hagras MM, Kamel FO, Magadmi RM, 2019 · PMID 31148988 · null

    12 patients with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes on metformin randomized to fenugreek 2 g/day or glibenclamide 5 mg/day for 12 weeks, with only 9 completing: fenugreek produced an insignificant drop in fasting blood glucose (P=0.63) while fasting insulin rose significantly (P=0.04); no notable adverse effects on hepatic or renal function.

  8. Relevance of sensitization to legumes in peanut-allergic children.
    Muller T, Luc A, Adam T, et al., 2022 · PMID 36156816 · negative

    Among 195 peanut-allergic children, 122 (63.9%) were sensitized to at least one other legume, with fenugreek the most frequent (N=61, 66.3%); allergy to at least one legume was confirmed in 34 of the 122 sensitized children (27.9%), and half of the allergic reactions to legumes other than peanut were severe.

  9. Pseudo-maple syrup urine disease due to maternal prenatal ingestion of fenugreek.
    Korman SH, Cohen E, Preminger A, 2001 · PMID 11532065 · negative

    Case report: fenugreek, maple syrup and the urine of maple syrup urine disease (MSUD) patients share a characteristic odour originating from a common component, sotolone; ingestion of fenugreek by mothers during labour resulted in a maple syrup-like odour in their newborn infants, leading to a false suspicion of MSUD. Added to substantiate the pregnancy and breastfeeding warning; the audit had attached this claim to PMID 10475807, whose PubMed record describes a false diagnosis after ingestion of herbal tea and does not state maternal ingestion.

  10. Exploring the Adverse Effects of Fenugreek in Humans: A Scoping Review.
    Amiri Ardekani E, Parvizi MM, Zare R, Mosaffa Jahromi M, Rampp T, Pasalar M, 2026 · PMID 41625343 · negative

    Scoping review of 60 articles published January 1990 to September 2024: 14 clinical trials reported adverse effects, 23 reported none and 13 gave no information; 10 case reports or series described allergic or hypersensitivity reactions. The most common adverse effect was mild gastrointestinal discomfort after oral consumption; others included hypoglycaemia, potential hypokalaemia, allergic reactions, a maple syrup odour in the urine, sweat or skin of infants and mothers, and interactions with certain medications. The authors conclude fenugreek is generally safe with mostly mild self-limiting effects and no attributed fatalities.
