Arnica (topical gel and oral/homeopathic)

Traditional use · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Arnica comes in two very different forms, and the difference matters. The gel or cream rubbed on the skin was compared with an ibuprofen gel in people with finger-joint arthritis and did about as well — but it has never been compared with a dummy cream in any long-lasting pain problem, so nobody can say how much of that is the plant and how much is simply rubbing something onto a sore joint. The one knee-arthritis study had no comparison group at all, and when arnica cream was properly tested against a dummy cream after eyelid surgery it made no difference to pain, swelling or bruising. The homeopathic pills and pellets are a separate story: several placebo-controlled studies have looked at them and found no real benefit.

Safety

Topical arnica's main risk is allergic contact dermatitis. Trial adverse-event rates were 4.8% in the hand-osteoarthritis trial and 7.6% (including one allergic reaction) in the knee-osteoarthritis open trial, while Cochrane recorded total adverse events in 13% of arnica-gel users versus 8% on ibuprofen gel. Do not apply to broken skin, open wounds, mucous membranes, or the eyes. Swallowing the undiluted herb or tincture is a different matter and is not safe: Arnica montana contains sesquiterpene lactones, including helenalin, that are toxic by mouth and have caused gastrointestinal irritation and, in larger amounts, serious systemic toxicity; arnica tinctures and gels are for external use only. Highly diluted homeopathic preparations (6C, 12C, 30C) contain vanishingly little plant material and were well tolerated in the trials above, but they also showed no benefit over placebo.

Who should be careful: Known allergy to Arnica or to other Asteraceae/Compositae plants (ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold, daisy, echinacea) — cross-reactivity is common. Do not use topically on broken or ulcerated skin, on the eyes or mucous membranes, or over large areas. Do not ingest the herb, tincture, or non-homeopathic extract. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding; safety has not been established.

Interactions: Ingested arnica has a theoretical additive bleeding risk with warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, and high-dose NSAIDs, and should be stopped well before planned surgery or dental procedures. Topical application over the same skin area as other topical analgesics may increase local irritation. Tell any clinician about arnica use before surgery, since patients commonly take it perioperatively without disclosing it.

What the evidence says

For chronic musculoskeletal pain the topical evidence is one randomised, double-blind non-inferiority trial in 204 patients with radiologically confirmed, symptomatically active interphalangeal hand osteoarthritis comparing arnica tincture gel (50 g tincture/100 g, DER 1:20) with 5% ibuprofen gel over 21 days, which found no between-group difference in pain intensity, hand function, or any secondary endpoint (adverse events 4.8% arnica versus 6.1% ibuprofen) but included no placebo arm. Cochrane's 2013 review graded that single study, analysing 174 people, as moderate evidence that arnica gel probably gives benefit similar to ibuprofen gel (pain mean difference -3.8 points, 95% CI -10.1 to 2.5, against an ibuprofen-group mean of 44.2 on a 100-point scale; hand function MD -0.4, 95% CI -1.75 to 0.95) while recording more total adverse events with arnica (13% versus 8%; RR 1.65, 95% CI 0.72 to 3.76), and concluded that the quantity and quality of research on topical medicinal plants in osteoarthritis is insufficient. The only knee-osteoarthritis data come from an uncontrolled open multicentre trial of Arnica montana fresh-plant gel applied twice daily in 79 patients, in which WOMAC total, pain, stiffness and function scores fell significantly at 3 and 6 weeks (P<.0001) with a 7.6% local adverse-event rate including one allergic reaction — a design that cannot separate the gel from natural history and expectation. Where topical arnica has been placebo-tested, results have been null: in 136 bilateral upper-blepharoplasty patients, arnica ointment 10% did not differ from placebo ointment, or from untreated eyelids, in overall periorbital appearance at 3 days, 7 days or 6 weeks, nor in swelling, ecchymosis, erythema, pain or patient satisfaction; and in a 20-participant double-blind placebo-controlled trial after downhill running, topical arnica had no effect on performance or on muscle-damage and inflammatory markers, with lower reported pain at only one of seven assessment points. The oral/homeopathic literature is separate and negative: Ernst and Pittler identified 8 placebo-controlled trials, mostly in conditions associated with tissue trauma and mostly burdened with severe methodological flaws, which on balance did not favour homeopathic arnica; a 3-arm RCT of arnica 30C and 6C versus placebo in 64 hand-surgery patients found no difference in pain (P=0.79) or bruising (P=0.45); and a 2021 meta-analysis of 18 placebo-controlled surgical comparisons found a non-significant pooled effect (Hedges' g 0.18, 95% CI -0.007 to 0.373, P=0.059). The 'Brazilian arnica' gel sometimes cited for low back pain is a different plant (Solidago chilensis), supported by one 20-participant trial that Cochrane rated very low quality; no trial of Arnica montana in low back pain was identified.

Doses studied: Hand osteoarthritis: arnica tincture gel 50 g tincture/100 g (DER 1:20) applied over 21 days, against 5% ibuprofen gel. Knee osteoarthritis: Arnica montana fresh-plant gel applied twice daily for 6 weeks, uncontrolled. Postoperative: arnica ointment 10% applied topically after upper blepharoplasty, null against placebo. Homeopathic preparations studied were arnica 30C and 6C tablets three times daily for 7 days before and 14 days after surgery, and a range of dilutions across the surgical meta-analysis. No dose has been established for chronic low back pain, where no Arnica montana trial exists.

Why this grade: Topical and homeopathic arnica are two different evidence bases and must not be pooled; neither reaches 'early' for chronic pain. For chronic musculoskeletal pain there is no placebo-controlled trial of topical Arnica montana at all. The hand-osteoarthritis evidence is a single randomised double-blind trial of 204 patients against 5% ibuprofen gel with no placebo arm, so it cannot separate a genuine drug effect from vehicle plus natural history; the knee-osteoarthritis evidence is one uncontrolled open-label trial in 79 patients, a design that cannot establish efficacy; and no trial of Arnica montana in low back pain exists. Where topical arnica has actually been placebo-tested — in postoperative recovery and in exercise-induced muscle soreness — it was null, including a 136-patient randomised placebo-controlled blepharoplasty trial that found no difference from placebo ointment or from no ointment in pain, swelling or bruising. The oral/homeopathic form is separately unsupported: a systematic review of 8 placebo-controlled trials, a subsequent 3-arm RCT, and a 2021 meta-analysis of 18 placebo-controlled surgical comparisons all failed to show benefit over placebo, and none of those trials enrolled people with chronic musculoskeletal pain. Graded 'traditional' because arnica has long documented topical use for bruises and sore joints but no adequate placebo-controlled human trial in any chronic pain condition; the homeopathic form considered alone would grade not_supported.

Sources (9)
  1. Choosing between NSAID and arnica for topical treatment of hand osteoarthritis in a randomised, double-blind study.
    Randomised trialWidrig R, Suter A, Saller R, Melzer J, 2007 · PMID 17318618 · positive

    In 204 patients with radiologically confirmed, symptomatically active interphalangeal hand osteoarthritis, 21 days of arnica tincture gel (50 g/100 g, DER 1:20) produced no difference from 5% ibuprofen gel in pain intensity or hand function, or in any secondary endpoint; adverse events were reported by 5 patients (4.8%) on arnica and 6 (6.1%) on ibuprofen. The trial had no placebo arm, so non-inferiority to an NSAID gel does not by itself establish efficacy.

  2. Arnica montana gel in osteoarthritis of the knee: an open, multicenter clinical trial.
    Clinical trialKnuesel O, Weber M, Suter A, 2002 · PMID 12539881 · positive

    In 79 people (26 men, 53 women) with mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis, Arnica montana fresh-plant gel applied twice daily produced significant decreases in median WOMAC total scores and in the pain, stiffness and function subscales at 3 and 6 weeks (both P<.0001), with a local adverse-event rate of 7.6% including one allergic reaction. This was an open, uncontrolled trial with no placebo or active comparator, so the improvement cannot be separated from natural history, regression to the mean, and expectation.

  3. Topical herbal therapies for treating osteoarthritis.
    Systematic reviewCameron M, Chrubasik S (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2013 · PMID 23728701 · mixed

    Moderate evidence from a single study of 174 people with hand osteoarthritis indicated arnica extract gel probably gives benefit similar to ibuprofen gel: pain mean difference -3.8 points on a 100-point scale (95% CI -10.1 to 2.5) against an ibuprofen-group mean of 44.2, and hand function MD -0.4 (95% CI -1.75 to 0.95). Total adverse events were higher with arnica than ibuprofen (13% versus 8%; RR 1.65, 95% CI 0.72 to 3.76), and the review concluded the quantity and quality of current research on topical medicinal plants for osteoarthritis is insufficient.

  4. Arnica Ointment 10% Does Not Improve Upper Blepharoplasty Outcome: A Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Randomised trialvan Exsel DCE, Pool SMW, van Uchelen JH, Edens MA, van der Lei B, Melenhorst WBWH, 2016 · PMID 27348641 · null

    In 136 bilateral upper-blepharoplasty patients randomised to arnica ointment 10% or placebo ointment, with the contralateral side left untreated as an internal control, there was no significant difference between arnica and placebo in overall periorbital appearance at 3 days, 7 days or 6 weeks, and no difference in swelling, ecchymosis, erythema, pain, or patient satisfaction. Untreated eyelids also did not differ from eyelids treated with either arnica or placebo on any outcome measure.

  5. The effects of topical Arnica on performance, pain and muscle damage after intense eccentric exercise.
    Randomised trialPumpa KL, Fallon KE, Bensoussan A, Papalia S, 2014 (epub 2013) · PMID 23947690 · mixed

    In 20 well-trained males in a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of topical arnica reapplied every 4 waking hours for 96 hours after a downhill-running protocol, arnica had no effect on any performance assessment (peak torque, countermovement and squat jump) or on blood markers of muscle damage and inflammation (IL-1beta, IL-6, TNF-alpha, CRP, myoglobin, creatine kinase). Lower pain on visual analogue scale and muscle tenderness was reported at 72 hours only, one of seven assessment time points.

  6. Efficacy of homeopathic arnica: a systematic review of placebo-controlled clinical trials.
    Systematic reviewErnst E, Pittler MH, 1998 · PMID 9820349 · null

    Eight placebo-controlled trials of homeopathic arnica met inclusion criteria, most relating to conditions associated with tissue trauma and most burdened with severe methodological flaws; on balance they did not suggest homeopathic arnica is more efficacious than placebo. The authors concluded the claim of efficacy beyond a placebo effect is not supported by rigorous clinical trials.

  7. Homeopathic arnica for prevention of pain and bruising: randomized placebo-controlled trial in hand surgery.
    Randomised trialStevinson C, Devaraj VS, Fountain-Barber A, Hawkins S, Ernst E, 2003 · PMID 12562974 · null

    In 64 adults randomised to homeopathic arnica 30C, arnica 6C, or placebo for 7 days before and 14 days after elective carpal tunnel surgery (62 in the intention-to-treat analysis), there were no group differences at day 4 in pain on the short-form McGill Pain Questionnaire (P=0.79) or in bruising by colour separation analysis (P=0.45); swelling and analgesic use also did not differ.

  8. Is Homeopathic Arnica Effective for Postoperative Recovery? A Meta-analysis of Placebo-Controlled and Active Comparator Trials.
    Systematic reviewGaertner K, Baumgartner S, Walach H, 2021 · PMID 34977136 · null

    Across 23 publications reporting 29 comparisons (28 analysable), the meta-analysis of the 18 placebo-controlled comparisons of homeopathic Arnica in surgery yielded a non-significant pooled effect size (Hedges' g 0.18, 95% CI -0.007 to 0.373, P=0.059); the larger effect in active-comparator trials (g=0.26) was driven by non-randomised studies and converged toward zero in the randomised ones. The authors themselves interpreted this as a 'small effect size over and against placebo', a reading the confidence interval does not support; notably, this homeopathy-affiliated research team still did not reach statistical significance.

  9. Herbal medicine for low-back pain.
    Meta-analysisOltean H, Robbins C, van Tulder MW, Berman BM, Bombardier C, Gagnier JJ (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2014 · PMID 25536022 · mixed

    The only 'arnica' evidence in low back pain comes from a single 20-participant trial of a gel containing Solidago chilensis ('Brazilian arnica'), a different plant from Arnica montana, which Cochrane rated very low quality evidence for reduced pain perception and improved flexibility versus placebo gel. No trial of Arnica montana in low back pain was identified among the 14 RCTs (2050 participants) reviewed.
