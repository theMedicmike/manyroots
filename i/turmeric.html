Herbs

Turmeric / curcumin

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Early evidence · for depression & low mood Early evidence · for gut & digestion

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For chronic pain

Turmeric extract has been tested more than most supplements for knee arthritis, and most studies find people's pain drops somewhat. The better-run studies find a smaller change than the enthusiastic ones, one recent independent trial of a turmeric-containing blend found nothing at all, and nobody has shown a benefit that lasts beyond a few months. Almost all of this research is about arthritic joints rather than backs, so it is a real stretch to assume it helps back pain. One thing worth taking seriously: concentrated turmeric capsules (not turmeric in food) have caused serious liver injury in a small number of people, so this is not a 'can't hurt' supplement.

Safety

In randomized trials at studied doses over 8 to 16 weeks, turmeric extracts were well tolerated: adverse-event rates were similar to placebo and roughly 12% lower than with NSAIDs (PMID 33511486), CurKOA reported adverse events in 39% of the turmeric arm versus 53% of placebo (PMID 32926799), and the ATLAS trial found similar adverse-event rates between a curcumin-containing combination and placebo (risk difference -3.4%, 95% CI -24.8 to 17.9), with four serious adverse events judged unrelated to treatment (PMID 42331134). The serious signal is liver injury from concentrated supplements, not from turmeric in food. The US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network adjudicated ten cases of turmeric-associated hepatotoxicity, of which five were hospitalized and one died of acute liver failure, with a latency of one to four months and a strong link to the HLA-B*35:01 allele (PMID 36252717). Cases have been rising since 2017. Anyone who develops jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, unusual fatigue, nausea or right-upper-abdominal pain while taking a turmeric supplement should stop it and seek medical assessment.

Who should be careful: Concentrated turmeric or curcumin extracts should be avoided by anyone with active liver disease, unexplained elevated liver enzymes, or a previous episode of drug- or supplement-induced liver injury. This caution applies to concentrated supplements, not to culinary turmeric used in food, which was not implicated in the DILIN series. Safety at supplement doses has not been established in pregnancy, breastfeeding, or in children, because trials have not enrolled these groups. Tell your surgical team about any turmeric supplement before a planned operation.

Interactions: One small clinical study of a phospholipid curcumin formulation (Meriva) found no change in bleeding time in patients taking aspirin, ticlopidine or clopidogrel, and no change in INR in patients on warfarin or dabigatran, after 10 days of supplementation, with no effect on levothyroxine or metformin control (PMID 30070343). That is reassuring, but the study was small, ran only 10 to 15 days, and tested a single branded formulation, so it does not settle the question for other products or for longer use. Separately, three of seven turmeric products implicated in DILIN liver-injury cases contained added piperine (black pepper extract), which sharply increases curcumin absorption (PMID 36252717); a piperine-enhanced product should be treated as a higher-exposure product, not a like-for-like substitute. Anyone taking anticoagulants, antiplatelets, or a drug with a narrow therapeutic index should clear a concentrated turmeric supplement with a pharmacist first.

What the evidence says

A 2021 systematic review of 16 RCTs (1,810 adults with knee osteoarthritis, trials up to 16 weeks) found turmeric extracts reduced knee pain versus placebo (SMD -0.82, 95% CI -1.17 to -0.47) and improved physical function (SMD -0.75, 95% CI -1.18 to -0.33), with effects comparable to NSAIDs but very high heterogeneity (I-squared 86.23% and 90.05%) and moderate risk of bias. The best-characterised placebo-controlled trial, CurKOA (n=70, 12 weeks, co-funded by the University of Tasmania and Natural Remedies Private Limited), found a smaller effect: VAS pain -9.1 mm (95% CI -17.8 to -0.4, p=0.039), with no change in MRI effusion-synovitis volume (3.2 mL, 95% CI -0.3 to 6.8) or cartilage T2 relaxation time, and its secondary analyses found no between-group difference in hsCRP, IL-6 or TNF-alpha while MMP-3 fell further in the placebo arm (difference 1.03 ng/mL, 95% CI 0.19 to 1.88), leaving the proposed anti-inflammatory mechanism unsupported. The independent placebo-controlled ATLAS trial (n=84, 12 weeks, 2026) tested a combination containing curcumin 500 mg with Boswellia, pine bark, MSM and piperine and found no difference from placebo in VAS pain (0.16 mm, 95% CI -6.81 to 7.12), concluding that the evidence does not support its use. A 2018 meta-analysis of 69 supplement trials placed curcumin among seven supplements with large short-term pain effects but found no supplement produced clinically important benefit at medium- or long-term follow-up, with evidence quality rated very low. Turmeric does not appear among the therapies recommended for hand, hip or knee osteoarthritis in the 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline, although a 2019 GRADE-based evidence review conditionally recommended curcuma for chronic musculoskeletal pain on the judgement that benefits outweighed risks with residual uncertainty. Evidence in this condition node is osteoarthritis-specific: no randomized trial of turmeric alone in chronic low back pain was located, only small trials of fixed turmeric-Boswellia combination products reporting implausibly large effects (98.5% pain reduction over 15 days in one).

Doses studied: Trials pooled in the meta-analyses ran up to 16 weeks; the placebo-controlled CurKOA trial used two capsules per day of a standardized Curcuma longa extract for 12 weeks (the abstract does not state the milligram dose). Doses and formulations varied widely across the underlying trials and are not interchangeable: bioavailability-enhanced products (phospholipid, micellar, or piperine-combined) deliver substantially more absorbed curcumin than plain turmeric powder at the same label weight. No dose has been established for chronic low back pain, where the only randomized trials tested fixed turmeric-Boswellia combination products (300 mg once daily for 90 days, PMID 39700654; 500 mg for 15 days, PMID 39973246) rather than turmeric alone.

Why this grade: Multiple placebo-controlled RCTs and several independent meta-analyses in knee osteoarthritis point the same direction, which rules out an 'early' grade: 16 RCTs in 1,810 adults is not a small or preliminary evidence base. But it falls short of 'strong'. Heterogeneity across pooled trials is extreme (I-squared 86.23% for pain, 90.05% for function); most trials are small, short (16 weeks or less) and at moderate risk of bias. The best-characterised placebo-controlled trial (CurKOA) produced a VAS pain difference of only -9.1 mm with a confidence interval nearly touching zero, null results on every imaging and inflammatory outcome, and was co-funded by a supplement manufacturer. A 69-trial supplement meta-analysis found no clinically important benefit at medium- or long-term follow-up, and an independent 2026 placebo-controlled trial of a curcumin-containing combination was frankly null. Turmeric does not appear among therapies recommended in the 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation osteoarthritis guideline, though a 2019 GRADE-based review conditionally recommended curcuma for chronic musculoskeletal pain. The evidence is also condition-specific: it is knee and hand osteoarthritis, not chronic low back pain, where only small combination-product trials exist. Graded moderate on those grounds.

For depression & low mood

Turmeric's active compound curcumin has been tested in a number of small studies for depression, and several of them found people felt somewhat better than on a placebo. But the effect disappears when researchers re-run the numbers slightly differently, one review of the pooled evidence concluded there is great uncertainty about whether it works at all, and chemists have raised serious questions about whether curcumin is even absorbed well enough to act on the brain. Concentrated turmeric supplements are also not risk-free: they have caused serious liver injury in a small number of people, including one death, which is a very different risk profile from turmeric used as a spice in cooking.

Safety

Turmeric as a cooking spice has a long record of safe use and is not the concern here; concentrated, bioavailability-enhanced supplements are a different product with a different risk profile. The clearest documented harm is liver injury: the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network has adjudicated ten cases of turmeric-associated hepatotoxicity, mostly hepatocellular, with a latency of one to four months, five hospitalisations and one death from acute liver failure. Susceptibility appears strongly linked to the HLA-B*35:01 genotype, carried by seven of the ten cases at roughly seven times the population allele frequency, and products combined with piperine (black pepper extract, which raises curcumin absorption) were over-represented. Cases have been rising since 2017, which the DILIN authors attribute to usage patterns and increased piperine combination. Milder and much more common effects are gastrointestinal: nausea, loose stools, reflux and abdominal discomfort, more likely at higher doses - though pooled trial data found no significant excess of digestive or neurological complaints versus placebo, so the common effects are mild rather than frequent. Anyone taking a concentrated turmeric supplement who develops fatigue, dark urine, right-upper-abdominal pain, itching or yellowing of the skin or eyes should stop and seek medical assessment promptly.

Who should be careful: Do not use concentrated turmeric or curcumin supplements if you have existing liver disease, abnormal liver enzymes, or a history of drug-induced liver injury. Stop before elective surgery (usually two weeks ahead) because of possible effects on platelet function. Avoid in gallstone disease or bile duct obstruction, since turmeric stimulates gallbladder contraction. High-dose supplements are not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding; culinary amounts are considered fine. Not established for bipolar depression. Not a substitute for treatment of moderate-to-severe depression, and given that the pooled effect is fragile on sensitivity analysis and null for depressive symptoms in one meta-analysis, it should not be relied on as a primary approach.

Interactions: Curcumin inhibits platelet aggregation, so combining concentrated supplements with warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, aspirin or other antiplatelet drugs may increase bleeding risk. Curcumin can inhibit CYP3A4, CYP2C9 and P-glycoprotein in laboratory studies, which could in principle raise levels of medications cleared by those routes - relevant for narrow-therapeutic-window drugs such as tacrolimus, some statins and some chemotherapy agents; check with a pharmacist rather than assuming. Piperine-containing formulations amplify absorption of curcumin and of some co-administered drugs, which is both the intended benefit and part of the suspected liver-injury signal. Curcumin may lower blood glucose, so monitor if you take insulin or other glucose-lowering medication. Turmeric is relatively high in oxalate, which is relevant if you are prone to calcium-oxalate kidney stones.

What the evidence says

A 2026 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis of 19 RCTs found curcumin supplementation improved depression (8 studies; SMD -0.76, 95% CI -1.22 to -0.30, p=0.001, I-squared 94.5%) and depressive symptoms (11 studies; SMD -0.53, 95% CI -0.97 to -0.09, p=0.019, I-squared 85.7%), with no evidence of publication bias by Begg's test - but the authors state the observed effect was fragile on sensitivity analysis and should be interpreted with caution. An earlier meta-analysis of six trials in depression (377 patients) found SMD -0.344 (95% CI -0.558 to -0.129, p=0.002). Against these, a meta-analysis of ten trials (594 patients) found an overall reduction (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.50 to -0.13) that split on subgroup analysis - significant for diagnosed depression (SMD -0.35, 95% CI -0.56 to -0.15) but null for depressive symptoms (SMD -0.17, 95% CI -0.61 to 0.26) - and concluded that evidence quality is low, indicating great uncertainty about efficacy and acceptability. The two most-cited primary trials are mixed rather than clean: an 8-week trial (n=56, 500 mg twice daily) found no separation from placebo over weeks 0-4 and a significant group-by-time interaction only over weeks 4-8 (IDS-SR30 total F(1,53)=4.22, p=0.045; mood F(1,53)=6.51, p=0.014), with the anxiety measure only a non-significant trend (p=0.097); a 12-week trial (n=123) found active treatments combined superior to placebo (p=0.031) but no difference between curcumin doses or between curcumin and a curcumin/saffron combination. A 2021 international meta-review judged the curcumin evidence supportive but likely subject to publication bias. The 2017 medicinal chemistry review of curcumin argues the compound's instability, reactivity and poor bioavailability make positive in vitro and in vivo signals unreliable.

Doses studied: Most depression trials used a bioavailability-enhanced standardised curcumin extract at 500 mg twice daily (1,000 mg/day) for 8-12 weeks; one four-arm trial compared 250 mg twice daily against 500 mg twice daily and found no difference between the doses. Formulations differ substantially between studies and are not interchangeable, because plain curcumin is very poorly absorbed - some products add piperine (black pepper extract) specifically to raise absorption, which is relevant to the liver-injury signal below. Culinary turmeric provides far less curcumin than any of these preparations.

Why this grade: Curcumin has a moderate number of RCTs and pooled meta-analyses that favour it, which would ordinarily argue for a higher grade, but five specific problems keep it at early. First, the most recent GRADE-assessed meta-analysis reports that its own significant effect became fragile under sensitivity analysis, with heterogeneity of I-squared 94.5%. Second, an earlier meta-analysis of ten trials found the effect held for diagnosed depression but was null for depressive symptoms, and concluded that evidence quality is low with great uncertainty about efficacy and acceptability. Third, an international meta-review of psychiatric phytoceuticals explicitly flagged the curcumin meta-analyses as likely affected by publication bias. Fourth, the underlying trials are small (n=56 and n=123 in the two best-known ones) and cluster within a small number of research groups. Fifth, a major medicinal-chemistry review classifies curcumin as both a pan-assay interference compound and an invalid metabolic panacea - unstable, reactive and non-bioavailable - and states that no double-blind placebo-controlled trial of curcumin has been successful. Taking the lower grade when torn is the right call here.

For gut & digestion

Turmeric and its active compound curcumin are widely sold for gut problems, but the research in IBS specifically is thin and points in different directions. The one careful placebo-controlled trial of turmeric on its own in people with diagnosed IBS found no benefit at all, and most of the encouraging results come from products that mix curcumin with other ingredients, in studies part-authored by the companies that make them. It is also not risk-free: concentrated turmeric supplements are now a recognised cause of rare but sometimes severe liver injury, so this is not a harmless thing to take indefinitely.

Safety

Turmeric in food is not the issue; concentrated supplements are. Turmeric-associated liver injury is a recognised and increasing problem: the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network documented ten adjudicated cases, all since 2011 and six since 2017, of which five required hospitalisation and one patient died of acute liver failure. Injury was typically hepatocellular with a latency of one to four months, and seven of ten patients carried the HLA-B*35:01 allele at far above population frequency, meaning susceptibility is partly genetic and not predictable in advance. Three of the seven analysed products also contained piperine (black pepper extract), which sharply increases curcumin absorption; high-bioavailability and piperine-boosted formulations are the ones of greatest concern. Stop immediately and seek medical care for jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, itching, right upper abdominal pain, nausea or unexplained fatigue. In the trials themselves curcumin was otherwise well tolerated, with no significant adverse events in the 79-participant placebo-controlled trial and only minor adverse effects reported in nine of the 21 studies in a systematic review; the commonest minor complaints at supplement doses are nausea, loose stools and reflux, which matters if the reason you are taking it is gut symptoms.

Who should be careful: Do not use turmeric or curcumin supplements if you have known liver disease, abnormal liver blood tests, gallstones or bile duct obstruction, or a previous herb- or drug-induced liver injury. Avoid combining with heavy alcohol use or with other supplements associated with liver injury. Stop at least two weeks before surgery because of antiplatelet effects. Not established as safe at supplement doses in pregnancy or breastfeeding. If you have iron deficiency, be aware curcumin can chelate iron. Alarm features — weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, onset after age 50 — need medical assessment before self-treating as IBS.

Interactions: Piperine (black pepper extract), included in many turmeric products to boost absorption, also inhibits drug-metabolising enzymes and can raise blood levels of other medicines; it was present in three of the seven products chemically analysed in the DILIN liver injury series. Use caution with anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs (warfarin, DOACs, aspirin, clopidogrel) because of curcumin's antiplatelet activity, and with drugs that have a narrow therapeutic window. Tell any clinician monitoring your liver function or INR that you take turmeric. Because turmeric supplements are frequently reformulated for higher bioavailability, a product you have tolerated before is not automatically the same product today.

What the evidence says

The most rigorous IBS-specific test was negative: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 106 IBS patients gave Curcuma xanthorrhiza 60 mg daily, Fumaria officinalis 1500 mg daily or placebo for 18 weeks, and IBS-related pain on a 0-50 mm scale INCREASED by 2.0 (SD 9.5) mm in the curcuma group versus a 0.3 (SD 9.9) mm decrease with placebo (P=0.81), with distension falling 1.4 (SD 12.5) versus 2.1 (SD 9.2) mm (P=0.48) and the authors concluding these herbs cannot be recommended for IBS. Every positive result carries a design or sponsorship caveat. An 8-week placebo-controlled trial of a curcumin extract 500 mg daily in 79 adults (77 analysed) found significantly greater reduction in Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale total score and DASS-21 anxiety, with no effect on intestinal microbiota or SIBO breath testing and no significant adverse events, but participants had self-reported digestive complaints rather than Rome-diagnosed IBS and an author was affiliated with the ingredient manufacturer. A 30-day trial in 121 IBS patients of curcumin combined with fennel essential oil reported a mean relative IBS-SSS reduction of 50.05% (SD 28.85) versus 26.12% (SD 30.62) with placebo (P<0.001) and 25.9% versus 6.8% symptom-free (P=0.005), but tested a fixed combination in which fennel oil has independent antispasmodic activity, with two authors from the sponsor company; and a 67-participant trial of curcumin-boswellia phytosome reduced bloating and abdominal pain versus a low-FODMAP diet alone, but had no placebo arm and all its research-and-development authors came from the extract manufacturer. An earlier 207-participant pilot of turmeric extract reported large drops in IBS prevalence and a 22-25% fall in abdominal pain, but was only partially blinded with no placebo arm and found no significant difference between its two doses (P=0.071). Syntheses converge on uncertainty: a 2024 systematic review of 26 studies judged most at high risk of bias, declined to meta-analyse for heterogeneity, and concluded efficacy in digestive disorders remains unclear; a 2021 systematic review found only 4 of 7 IBS studies beneficial; and a 2025 umbrella review found curcumin improved IBS symptom severity but with small effects and low-to-very-low GRADE certainty within a review that overall does not support nutritional interventions for IBS. Note the boundary: curcumin's more developed evidence base is in ulcerative colitis, a different disease with mucosal inflammation, and does not transfer to IBS.

Doses studied: Curcuma xanthorrhiza 60 mg daily for 18 weeks in the negative IBS trial (Brinkhaus 2005); a curcumin extract (Curcugen) 500 mg once daily for 8 weeks in adults with self-reported digestive complaints (Lopresti 2021); one or two standardised turmeric extract tablets daily for 8 weeks in an uncontrolled pilot (Bundy 2004); curcumin plus fennel essential oil, two capsules twice daily for 30 days (Portincasa 2016); curcumin-boswellia phytosome 500 mg twice daily for 30 days alongside a low-FODMAP diet, with no placebo arm (Giacosa 2022).

Why this grade: Human evidence in IBS is small, inconsistent and confounded by design and sponsorship. The one independent, adequately conducted placebo-controlled trial of a turmeric mono-preparation in diagnosed IBS (n=106, 18 weeks) was flatly negative, with pain scores drifting the wrong way, and its authors concluded curcuma cannot be recommended for IBS. Every positive signal carries a caveat: one placebo-controlled trial was in people with self-reported digestive complaints rather than Rome-diagnosed IBS and included an author from the ingredient manufacturer; two more tested fixed combination products from which curcumin's contribution cannot be isolated, both with sponsor-company authors, and one of those had no placebo arm at all; and the largest pilot was only partially blinded with no placebo arm. Syntheses agree the picture is unresolved: a 2024 systematic review of 26 studies found most at high risk of bias and concluded efficacy in digestive disorders remains unclear, a 2021 review found only 4 of 7 IBS studies beneficial, and a 2025 umbrella review found any effect small with low-to-very-low certainty. That combination is 'early', not 'moderate'. It does not meet the definitional bar for 'not_supported' because a placebo-controlled trial of a curcumin mono-extract did show benefit on a validated digestive symptom scale. No IBS trial cited here reported IBS-C versus IBS-D subtype results.

Sources (9)
  1. Herbal medicine with curcuma and fumitory in the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome: a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial.
    Brinkhaus B, Hentschel C, Von Keudell C, Schindler G, Lindner M, Stutzer H, et al., 2005 · PMID 16173134 · negative

    106 IBS patients (mean age 48, 63% female) randomised to Curcuma xanthorrhiza 60 mg daily (n=24), Fumaria officinalis 1500 mg daily (n=24) or placebo (n=58) for 18 weeks. IBS-related pain on a 0-50 mm visual analogue scale INCREASED by 2.0 (SD 9.5) mm in the curcuma group versus a 0.3 (SD 9.9) mm decrease with placebo (P=0.81); distension fell 1.4 (SD 12.5) mm with curcuma versus 2.1 (SD 9.2) mm with placebo (P=0.48). Global assessments of symptom change and IBS-related psychological stress also did not differ. The authors conclude that use of these herbs for IBS cannot be recommended.

  2. Efficacy of a curcumin extract (Curcugen) on gastrointestinal symptoms and intestinal microbiota in adults with self-reported digestive complaints: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study.
    Lopresti AL, Smith SJ, Rea A, Michel S, 2021 · PMID 33478482 · positive

    8-week parallel-group double-blind trial, 79 adults randomised and 77 analysed, curcumin extract 500 mg once daily versus placebo. Curcumin produced a significantly greater reduction in Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale total score and in DASS-21 anxiety score. No significant between-group change on other self-report measures, and no significant effect on intestinal microbiota profile or SIBO breath test. Well tolerated with no significant adverse events. Two limitations bear on weight: participants had self-reported digestive complaints, not Rome-criteria diagnosed IBS, and one author is affiliated with DolCas Biotech, which supplies the tested extract.

  3. Curcumin and Fennel Essential Oil Improve Symptoms and Quality of Life in Patients with Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    Portincasa P, Bonfrate L, Scribano ML, Kohn A, Caporaso N, Festi D, et al., 2016 · PMID 27308645 · positive

    121 patients with mild-to-moderate IBS (IBS-SSS 100-300, pain VAS 30-70) randomised to curcumin plus fennel essential oil or placebo, two capsules twice daily for 30 days. Mean relative IBS-SSS decrease 50.05% (SD 28.85) versus 26.12% (SD 30.62) with placebo (P<0.001); symptom-free patients 25.9% versus 6.8% (P=0.005); all IBS-QoL domains improved. Because this is a fixed curcumin-plus-fennel-oil combination the result cannot be attributed to curcumin alone, fennel essential oil having independent antispasmodic and carminative activity; two authors are from the Department of Clinical Research of Alfa Wassermann SpA.

  4. Beneficial Effects on Abdominal Bloating with an Innovative Food-Grade Formulation of Curcuma longa and Boswellia serrata Extracts in Subjects with Irritable Bowel Syndrome and Small Bowel Dysbiosis.
    Giacosa A, Riva A, Petrangolini G, Allegrini P, Fazia T, Bernardinelli L, Peroni G, Rondanelli M, 2022 · PMID 35276778 · positive

    67 IBS subjects with small bowel dysbiosis randomised for 30 days to Curcumin Boswellia Phytosome 500 mg twice daily plus a low-FODMAP diet (n=33) or the low-FODMAP diet alone (n=34). The intervention group showed significant decreases in bloating, abdominal pain and urinary indican (P<0.0001) and a better global assessment of efficacy (P<0.0001). Two design limits are decisive for weighting: there was NO placebo arm, so diet-plus-capsule is compared with diet alone rather than with a dummy capsule, and the formulation combines curcumin with boswellia so curcumin's contribution cannot be isolated. Four authors, including all three from the R&D department, are affiliated with Indena SpA, which manufactures the tested phytosome.

  5. Turmeric extract may improve irritable bowel syndrome symptomology in otherwise healthy adults: a pilot study.
    Bundy R, Walker AF, Middleton RW, Booth J, 2004 · PMID 15673996 · mixed

    Partially blinded two-dose pilot with NO placebo arm: 500 volunteers screened, 207 randomised to one or two standardised turmeric extract tablets daily for 8 weeks. IBS prevalence fell 53% and 60% from baseline in the one- and two-tablet groups (P<0.001); abdominal pain/discomfort fell 22% and 25%, with the between-dose difference only tending to significance (P=0.071); roughly two-thirds reported improvement. There were no significant differences between the two dose groups, and the authors themselves state placebo-controlled trials are now warranted to confirm the findings.

  6. A Systematic Review of the Clinical Use of Curcumin for the Management of Gastrointestinal Diseases.
    Atefi M, Darand M, Entezari MH, Jamialahmadi T, Bagherniya M, Sahebkar A, 2021 · PMID 34331698 · mixed

    Systematic review of 21 clinical trials comprising 1,478 gastrointestinal patients. For IBS specifically, only 4 of 7 studies showed a beneficial effect of curcumin or turmeric supplementation — that is, three did not — compared with 6 of 7 for ulcerative colitis. Nine studies reported minor adverse effects. The authors call for more randomised controlled trials to confirm the results. Useful because it quantifies how inconsistent the IBS-specific trial record is.

  7. A systematic review of the efficacy and safety of turmeric in the treatment of digestive disorders.
    Thavorn K, Wolfe D, Faust L, Shorr R, Akkawi M, Isaranuwatchai W, et al., 2024 · PMID 38503513 · mixed

    From 1,136 citations screened, 26 eligible studies of turmeric across inflammatory bowel disease, IBS, dyspepsia, GERD and peptic ulcer. Meta-analysis was not possible due to high heterogeneity. Most studies were assessed as high risk of bias with methodological limitations. Descriptive synthesis suggests turmeric is safe with possible efficacy in IBD or IBS, but inconsistent effects for other conditions, and the authors conclude that the efficacy of turmeric in digestive disorders remains unclear.

  8. Nutritional Interventions in Adult Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome: An Umbrella Review of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses of Randomized Clinical Trials.
    Zeraattalab-Motlagh S, Ranjbar M, Mohammadi H, Adibi P, 2025 · PMID 39110917 · mixed

    Umbrella review of 175 trials across 58 meta-analyses covering 11 nutritional interventions in IBS. Curcumin (with vitamin D3) is reported to improve IBS symptom severity, but the authors state these outcomes have mainly shown small effects with low to very low evidence certainty on GRADE. Only probiotics for abdominal pain (RR 4.04, 95% CI 2.36-6.92) and a low-FODMAP diet for IBS symptoms (RR 1.48, 95% CI 1.14-1.93) reached moderate certainty, and the review concludes overall that it does not support nutritional interventions for improving IBS symptoms.

  9. Liver Injury Associated with Turmeric-A Growing Problem: Ten Cases from the Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network [DILIN].
    Halegoua-DeMarzio D, Navarro V, Ahmad J, Avula B, Barnhart H, Barritt AS, et al., 2022 · PMID 36252717 · negative

    Ten adjudicated cases of turmeric-associated liver injury in the US DILIN, all enrolled since 2011 and six since 2017. Eight were women, nine were White, median age 56 (range 35-71); injury was hepatocellular in nine and mixed in one. Five patients were hospitalised and one died of acute liver failure. Latency was 1 to 4 months. Seven of ten carried HLA-B*35:01 (two homozygous), an allele frequency of 0.450 versus 0.056-0.069 in population controls. All seven products chemically analysed contained turmeric and three also contained piperine (black pepper), which increases curcumin absorption.
