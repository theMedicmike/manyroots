Practices

Cognitive behavioural therapy and pain-focused psychological therapy

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Pain-focused talk therapy does not tell you the pain is not real. It teaches practical skills for handling the fear, avoidance and low mood that long-term pain builds up, so pain interferes less with your life. Across many trials the average benefit is genuine but small, and it works best when paired with a structured exercise or physiotherapy programme rather than used alone.

Safety

No serious harms have been established, but the honest position is that harms data are thin rather than reassuring: the Cochrane review found adverse events were inadequately recorded and/or reported across studies and rated the evidence on harms as very low quality for every comparison. The realistic costs are burden and access - weekly sessions over roughly two months plus practice between them, therapist availability and expense. Adherence is a real limit: in the largest back-pain trial, only 53.7% of participants attended 6 or more of the 8 sessions.

Who should be careful: Not a substitute for medical evaluation of a new, changing or red-flag pain problem. People with active suicidal ideation, psychosis, severe untreated depression, or a substance-use crisis need those addressed first or concurrently by a clinician equipped to manage them, not deferred into a pain-coping programme.

Interactions: No drug interactions. The framing matters clinically: presenting CBT as evidence that pain is 'psychological' is both inaccurate and counterproductive - it is skills training for living with real pain, and mis-framing it predicts dropout. It is not a reason to stop or reduce prescribed medication without clinician involvement.

What the evidence says

The 2020 Cochrane review of psychological therapies for chronic non-cancer pain (excluding headache) included 75 studies with 9401 participants at treatment end, most with fibromyalgia, chronic low back pain, rheumatoid arthritis or mixed chronic pain. CBT, with the largest evidence base (59 studies), showed very small benefit over active control for pain (SMD -0.09, 95% CI -0.17 to -0.01; 3235 participants; 23 studies), disability (SMD -0.12, 95% CI -0.20 to -0.04) and distress (SMD -0.09, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.00), and small benefit over treatment-as-usual for pain (SMD -0.22, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.10; 2572 participants; 29 studies), disability (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.19) and distress (SMD -0.34, 95% CI -0.44 to -0.24); effects were largely maintained at follow-up versus usual care but not versus active control. Behavioural therapy (8 studies, 647 participants) and acceptance and commitment therapy (5 studies, 443 participants) showed no evidence of difference from active control, on very low quality evidence. A 2022 network meta-analysis of 97 trials and 13,136 participants with chronic non-specific low back pain found CBT delivered with physiotherapy care produced clinically important improvement in physical function (SMD 1.01, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.44) and pain (SMD 0.92, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.42) immediately post-intervention, while only behavioural therapy plus physiotherapy held a clinically important pain effect to mid-term follow-up. In a 342-participant randomised trial, 57.7% of the CBT group achieved clinically meaningful improvement in function at 26 weeks versus 44.1% with usual care (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.69). Adverse events were inadequately recorded and/or reported across trials.

Doses studied: Formats vary across trials. In the largest chronic low back pain trial, CBT was delivered in eight weekly two-hour groups. In the chronic low back pain network meta-analysis, psychological interventions were most effective when delivered in conjunction with physiotherapy care (mainly structured exercise); behavioural therapy plus physiotherapy was the only combination whose pain effect remained clinically important to mid-term (6-12 month) follow-up, and no trial had investigated the long-term effectiveness of pain education plus physiotherapy care.

Why this grade: A 75-study, 9401-participant Cochrane review confirms real but small benefits: CBT's advantage over active control was SMD -0.09 for pain and over usual care SMD -0.22, with mostly moderate-to-low quality evidence. A 97-trial network meta-analysis concluded psychological interventions are most effective when delivered in conjunction with physiotherapy care, and every clinically important effect it reported came from such a combination. Closely related therapies (behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy) showed no clear benefit over active control on very low quality evidence, and ACP rates the underlying CBT evidence for chronic low back pain as low quality. Genuine but modest and delivery-dependent, so moderate rather than strong.

  1. Psychological therapies for the management of chronic pain (excluding headache) in adults.
    Williams ACdeC, Fisher E, Hearn L, Eccleston C, 2020 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 32794606 · mixed

    75 studies, 9401 participants. CBT gave very small benefit over active control (pain SMD -0.09, 95% CI -0.17 to -0.01; 23 studies) and small benefit over usual care (pain SMD -0.22, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.10; 29 studies); effects vs active control were not maintained at follow-up. Behavioural therapy (8 studies) and ACT (5 studies) showed no evidence of difference from active control on very low quality evidence. Adverse events were inadequately recorded and/or reported across studies.

  2. Psychological interventions for chronic, non-specific low back pain: systematic review with network meta-analysis.
    Ho EK, Chen L, Simic M, Ashton-James CE, Comachio J, Wang DXM, Hayden JA, Ferreira ML, Ferreira PH, 2022 (BMJ) · PMID 35354560 · positive

    97 RCTs, 13,136 participants. CBT delivered with physiotherapy care produced clinically important post-intervention gains in physical function (SMD 1.01, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.44) and pain (SMD 0.92, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.42), but only behavioural therapy plus physiotherapy maintained a clinically important pain effect to mid-term follow-up (SMD 1.01, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.60), and pain education plus physiotherapy gave the most sustainable physical-function effect (SMD 0.63, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.00). Long-term effectiveness remains uncertain.

  3. Effect of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction vs Cognitive Behavioral Therapy or Usual Care on Back Pain and Functional Limitations in Adults With Chronic Low Back Pain: A Randomized Clinical Trial.
    Cherkin DC, Sherman KJ, Balderson BH, Cook AJ, Anderson ML, Hawkes RJ, Hansen KE, Turner JA, 2016 (JAMA) · PMID 27002445 · positive

    342 adults. At 26 weeks, 57.7% of the CBT group had clinically meaningful (>=30%) improvement in functional limitations vs 44.1% with usual care (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.69), and 44.9% vs 26.6% for pain bothersomeness (RR 1.69, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.41). Only 53.7% attended 6 or more of 8 sessions.

  4. Multidisciplinary biopsychosocial rehabilitation for chronic low back pain.
    Kamper SJ, Apeldoorn AT, Chiarotto A, Smeets RJEM, Ostelo RWJG, Guzman J, van Tulder MW, 2014 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 25180773 · mixed

    41 RCTs, 6858 participants. Moderate-to-low quality evidence of modest long-term benefit over usual care for pain (SMD 0.21, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.37) and disability (SMD 0.23, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.40), but moderate-to-low quality evidence of NO difference on work outcomes versus usual care (OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.47). Against physical treatment, long-term disability improved (SMD 0.68, 95% CI 0.16 to 1.19) and work outcomes did too (OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.53), though the long-term pain difference was not significant (SMD 0.51, 95% CI -0.01 to 1.04).

  5. Noninvasive Treatments for Acute, Subacute, and Chronic Low Back Pain: A Clinical Practice Guideline From the American College of Physicians.
    Qaseem A, Wilt TJ, McLean RM, Forciea MA, 2017 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 28192789 · positive

    Cognitive behavioural therapy and operant therapy are included among recommended first-line nonpharmacologic options for chronic low back pain, but explicitly on low-quality evidence (unlike exercise, multidisciplinary rehabilitation, acupuncture and MBSR, which are listed as moderate-quality).
