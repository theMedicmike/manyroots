Herbs

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis)

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Lemon balm is a gentle, long-used calming herb, and one small trial of a special high-absorption extract did improve sleep scores. But the evidence is thin and it pulls in both directions: when researchers tested the ordinary herb against a dummy pill for twelve weeks and measured sleep properly, sleep did not improve, even though mood and anxiety did. Most of the other positive studies mixed lemon balm with another herb or were run by the companies making the extract. Treat this as a promising early signal, not a proven sleep remedy.

Safety

Lemon balm was well tolerated across every trial retrieved - 400 mg/day of concentrated extract for 3 weeks, 700 mg/day for 12 weeks and 3 g/day of dried herb for 8 weeks - with no serious adverse events reported. The 2005 Prescrire assessment explicitly classified melissa as safe, though unproven, for sleep. Longer-term use, and use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children, has not been adequately studied. The frequently repeated caution about lemon balm and thyroid function traces to in vitro work published in 1984 and 1985, in which freeze-dried Melissa officinalis extracts inhibited the binding of thyrotropin to human thyroid membranes and blocked the receptor-binding and biological activity of Graves' immunoglobulins; a fresh search found no human study measuring thyroid outcomes with lemon balm, and none of the clinical trials retrieved here measured them, so this remains a laboratory-derived theoretical concern rather than a demonstrated one.

Who should be careful: Not established as safe in pregnancy or lactation. Caution and clinician input are reasonable in thyroid disease, particularly Graves' disease or while on thyroid replacement, given the laboratory-demonstrated antithyrotropic activity that has never been evaluated in people.

Interactions: Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, sedating antihistamines and other CNS depressants. Theoretical interaction with thyroid medication such as levothyroxine, untested in humans. Note that bioavailability-enhanced Phytosome and phospholipid-carrier formulations deliver substantially more active polyphenol than the plain herb, so doses are not interchangeable between products - and the plain-herb form is the one that failed to improve sleep in the trial that measured it.

What the evidence says

The most direct evidence is a prospective, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study of a highly standardised Melissa officinalis Phytosome designed to improve oral bioavailability of its hydroxycinnamic acids: Insomnia Severity Index averaged 6.8 +/- 4.1 on treatment versus 9.7 +/- 3.7 on placebo (a 2.9-point reduction, p = 0.003), slow-wave sleep duration rose about 15% while REM fell about 10%, and 87% of the treated group versus 30% of placebo reported improved sleep quality (chi-square 21.01, p = 0.0003); the authors themselves note that lemon balm's anti-anxiety and anti-insomnia effects 'have not always been observed in a reproducible manner in humans', and two are affiliated with Velleja Research (Di Pierro 2024). Directly against this, a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial of the plain herb - 700 mg/day hydroalcoholic extract for 12 weeks in 60 randomised patients with type 2 diabetes and depressive symptoms, 44 completing - found significant between-group improvements in depression (p < 0.001) and anxiety (p = 0.04) but no significant difference in sleep quality on the PSQI (Safari 2023). In 80 patients with chronic stable angina, 3 g/day of plain Melissa officinalis for 8 weeks significantly reduced DASS-21 depression, anxiety and stress scores and total PSQI sleep disturbance versus placebo (P < 0.05), though this was a cardiac rather than an insomnia population (Haybar 2018), and a 3-week trial of 400 mg/day of a phospholipid-carrier extract in 100 adults recruited partly for poor sleep (PSQI > 5) reported significant improvements in mood, anxiety, stress, affect, mental wellbeing and quality of life (all p < 0.001) without listing any sleep-specific improvement among its outcomes (Bano 2023). Combination trials are more favourable but cannot isolate the herb: 1000 mg M. officinalis plus 400 mg Nepeta menthoides nightly for 4 weeks in insomniacs produced greater reductions in Insomnia Severity Index (4.97 +/- 4.69 vs 1.60 +/- 3.70, p = 0.002) and total PSQI (4.14 +/- 3.69 vs 1.42 +/- 2.81, p = 0.001) plus increased total sleep time (p < 0.001) versus placebo (Ranjbar 2018); a valerian/lemon balm product significantly reduced PSQI-measured sleep disorder versus placebo in 100 women aged 50-60 (Taavoni 2013); and lemon balm with fennel extract improved all MENQOL domains versus citalopram and placebo in 60 postmenopausal women with sleep disturbance, though no sleep-specific scale was reported (Shirazi 2021). Independent appraisals are markedly cooler: a 2005 Prescrire review of about 20 plants licensed in France for minor sleep disturbance grouped melissa with linden, vervain and orange flower as having no demonstrated efficacy though safe, identifying only valerian as having a modest real effect, and a 2015 meta-analysis of 14 insomnia RCTs found the only herbal monopreparations with usable trial evidence were valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling - lemon balm was not among them.

Doses studied: Plain dried herb: 3 g/day for 8 weeks (Haybar 2018). Plain hydroalcoholic extract: 700 mg/day for 12 weeks, null on sleep (Safari 2023). Bioavailability-enhanced proprietary extracts: 400 mg/day phospholipid-carrier aqueous extract (200 mg twice daily) for 3 weeks (Bano 2023); a standardised Phytosome preparation in Di Pierro 2024. In combination: 1000 mg M. officinalis nightly with 400 mg Nepeta menthoides for 4 weeks (Ranjbar 2018); 500 mg/day lemon balm with fennel for 8 weeks (Shirazi 2021). No consensus dose has been established for insomnia.

Why this grade: Human sleep evidence is limited to a handful of small, short trials, and it is not consistent. The most direct positive result used a proprietary bioavailability-enhanced Phytosome formulation with authors affiliated to the formulator's research company; the only placebo-controlled trial of the plain herb that measured sleep with a validated instrument found no significant difference from placebo on the PSQI; a third trial recruited people partly on the basis of poor sleep but reported no sleep-specific improvement among its outcomes; and the remaining positive trials tested lemon balm inside combination products or in a cardiac population. A 2005 independent drug-bulletin assessment classified melissa as having no demonstrated efficacy for sleep, and a 2015 meta-analysis of herbal monopreparations for insomnia did not find lemon balm among the herbs with usable RCT evidence at all. That is 'early' evidence, not 'moderate' - and it is not 'traditional' only because one adequate placebo-controlled crossover trial with a sleep primary endpoint does exist.

Sources (9)
  1. Effects of Melissa officinalis Phytosome on Sleep Quality: Results of a Prospective, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled, and Cross-Over Study.
    Randomised trialDi Pierro F, Sisti D, Rocchi M, et al., 2024 · PMID 39683592 · positive

    Double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial of a standardised Melissa officinalis Phytosome: Insomnia Severity Index 6.8 +/- 4.1 on treatment versus 9.7 +/- 3.7 on placebo (2.9-point reduction, p = 0.003); slow-wave sleep duration increased about 15% and REM decreased about 10%; 87% versus 30% reported improved sleep quality (chi-square 21.01, p = 0.0003). No significant change in physical activity or anxiety levels. The authors state that prior human results with lemon balm have not been reproducible, which they attribute to poor bioavailability of the plain herb; two authors are affiliated with Velleja Research.

  2. The effects of melissa officinalis on depression and anxiety in type 2 diabetes patients with depression: a randomized double-blinded placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialSafari M, Asadi A, Aryaeian N, et al., 2023 · PMID 37131158 · null

    Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 60 patients with type 2 diabetes and depressive symptoms (30 per arm; 44 completed 12 weeks) comparing 700 mg/day M. officinalis hydroalcoholic extract with toasted flour. Mean changes in depression (BDI-II) and anxiety (BAI) differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001 and p = 0.04), but no significant differences were observed in sleep quality measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, nor in fasting blood sugar, hs-CRP, anthropometric indices or blood pressure. This is the only placebo-controlled trial of the plain herb with a validated sleep instrument retrieved, and it was null on sleep.

  3. The effects of Melissa officinalis supplementation on depression, anxiety, stress, and sleep disorder in patients with chronic stable angina.
    Randomised trialHaybar H, Javid AZ, Haghighizadeh MH, et al., 2018 · PMID 29908682 · positive

    Double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 80 patients with chronic stable angina: 3 g/day Melissa officinalis for 8 weeks significantly reduced DASS-21 depression, anxiety and stress scores and total Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index sleep disturbance versus placebo (P < 0.05). The population was cardiac patients, not people with diagnosed insomnia.

  4. The possible "calming effect" of subchronic supplementation of a standardised phospholipid carrier-based Melissa officinalis L. extract in healthy adults with emotional distress and poor sleep conditions: results from a prospective, randomised, double-blinded, placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Journal articleBano A, Hepsomali P, Rabbani F, et al., 2023 · PMID 37927585 · mixed

    Three-week randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 100 healthy adults with moderate depression, anxiety or stress (DASS-42) or poor sleep (PSQI > 5): 400 mg/day phospholipid-carrier lemon balm extract (Relissa) significantly improved depressive mood, anxiety, stress, positive and negative affect, mental wellbeing and quality of life (all p < 0.001), and was well tolerated with no treatment-emergent effects or serious adverse events. Poor sleep was an entry criterion, but no sleep-specific improvement appears among the outcomes reported as improved.

  5. Efficacy of a Combination of Melissa officinalis L. and Nepeta Menthoides Boiss. & Buhse on Insomnia: A Triple-Blind, Randomized Placebo-Controlled Clinical Trial.
    Journal articleRanjbar M, Salehi A, Rezaeizadeh H, et al., 2018 · PMID 29741926 · positive

    Triple-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial in insomniacs: 1000 mg M. officinalis plus 400 mg N. menthoides nightly for 4 weeks produced greater reductions in Insomnia Severity Index (4.97 +/- 4.69 vs 1.60 +/- 3.70, p = 0.002) and total PSQI (4.14 +/- 3.69 vs 1.42 +/- 2.81, p = 0.001) and increased total sleep time on sleep diaries (p < 0.001) versus placebo. Lemon balm's individual contribution cannot be isolated from the combination.

  6. Valerian/lemon balm use for sleep disorders during menopause.
    Randomised trialTaavoni S, Nazem Ekbatani N, Haghani H, 2013 · PMID 24199972 · positive

    Randomised trial in 100 women aged 50-60 complaining of sleep disorders, allocated to a valerian/lemon balm product or placebo (50 per group) with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index administered before and after. A significant reduction in sleep disorder was observed in the intervention group compared with placebo. The abstract reports no effect sizes or p-values, and lemon balm's separate contribution cannot be isolated from valerian.

  7. The Effectiveness of Melissa Officinalis L. versus Citalopram on Quality of Life of Menopausal Women with Sleep Disorder: A Randomized Double-Blind Clinical Trial.
    Randomised trialShirazi M, Jalalian MN, Abed M, Ghaemi M, 2021 · PMID 33465795 · mixed

    Randomised double-blind trial in 60 postmenopausal women with sleep disturbance given lemon balm combined with fennel fruit extract (500 mg daily), citalopram 30 mg, or placebo for 8 weeks. Mean scores on all Menopause-Specific Quality of Life domains improved significantly more in the lemon balm group (p < 0.001), for example 2.2 +/- 0.84 versus 0.56 +/- 0.58 versus 0.36 +/- 0.55 in the vasomotor domain. Sleep disturbance was an entry criterion but no sleep-specific instrument is reported, and the intervention was a lemon balm-fennel combination.

  8. Management of insomnia: a place for traditional herbal remedies.
    Comparative studyPrescrire International, 2005 · PMID 15984105 · null

    Independent review of about 20 plants approved in France for medications 'traditionally used' for minor sleep disturbance concluded that melissa - grouped with littleleaf linden, vervain and orange flower - has no demonstrated efficacy although it is safe and can therefore be used; only valerian extracts were judged to have a modest but real effect on subjective sleep quality versus placebo.

  9. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisLeach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · negative

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomised controlled trials involving 1602 participants with insomnia. Only four orally administered herbal monopreparations had usable trial evidence - valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling - and lemon balm was not among them. No herbal medicine differed significantly from placebo or active control on any of thirteen measures of clinical efficacy, and the authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to support herbal medicine for insomnia.
