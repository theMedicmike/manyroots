Diabetes Prevention Program-style intensive lifestyle intervention

Strong evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

A structured programme that helps you lose about 7% of your body weight and build up to 150 minutes a week of brisk walking is one of the best-tested things in all of medicine for people whose blood sugar is high but not yet in the diabetes range. Across several large trials it roughly halved the number of people who went on to be diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, and in the US study it worked better than the drug metformin. What it has not shown consistently is fewer heart attacks, strokes or deaths: one long Chinese trial found fewer cardiovascular events and about 1.4 extra years of life, while the American follow-up found no reduction in heart events at all, and everyday real-world versions of the programme produce far less weight loss than the trials did.

Safety

Nothing here is a reason to stop, skip or reduce a prescribed glucose-lowering medicine. The DPP and DPPOS reported no safety signal from the lifestyle arm over two decades. Expected nuisance harms are musculoskeletal aches and injuries when activity is increased quickly, and gallstones become more likely with faster weight loss. Weight regain after the intensive phase is the norm, so benefit depends on continued support.

Who should be careful: The trials enrolled adults with prediabetes who were not taking glucose-lowering drugs. This is a prevention programme, not a treatment for established type 2 diabetes and never a substitute for insulin in type 1 diabetes. People with unstable heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, advanced retinopathy, foot ulcers or recent injury should have the exercise component tailored by a clinician before starting. Deliberate weight loss is not appropriate in pregnancy, active eating disorders, or frailty in older adults without supervision.

Interactions: Anyone already taking insulin or a sulfonylurea who cuts calories and increases activity can become hypoglycaemic; doses usually need to be adjusted by the prescriber as weight falls. Blood-pressure medication may also need reducing as weight drops. These are decisions for the prescriber, not for the person taking the medicine.

What the evidence says

What is being measured matters here. The primary outcome in these trials is a new diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, which is defined by blood-glucose thresholds rather than by any event a person would feel; HbA1c, fasting glucose and weight are surrogates. Hard outcomes such as heart attack, stroke and death have been looked for: two of these trials report them directly - Da Qing over 30 years and DPP/DPPOS over a median 21 years - and Cochrane pooled deaths across 10 trials; only Da Qing found benefit. In the US Diabetes Prevention Program (n=3234, prediabetes by elevated fasting and post-load glucose, mean BMI 34, mean follow-up 2.8 years) diabetes incidence was 4.8 cases per 100 person-years with intensive lifestyle versus 7.8 with metformin and 11.0 with placebo, a 58% (95% CI 48-66%) relative reduction with lifestyle versus 31% (17-43%) with metformin, lifestyle being significantly more effective; 6.9 people had to take part for three years to prevent one case, versus 13.9 for metformin. The Finnish DPS (n=522, impaired glucose tolerance) independently found a 58% risk reduction with 4-year cumulative incidence 11% versus 23%. The 2017 Cochrane review pooled 11 trials (4511 participants) for diet plus physical activity and found 14.8% versus 25.7% developing diabetes (RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.50-0.64), rated moderate-quality; but it rated the mortality evidence very low quality and found no difference (12 deaths in 2049 versus 10 in 2050, RR 1.12, 0.50-2.50), judged none of the 12 included trials at low risk of bias across all domains, and concluded that most trials did not investigate patient-important outcomes. In DPPOS the lifestyle effect attenuated but persisted: a 27% reduction at 15 years (HR 0.73, 0.65-0.83) with cumulative incidence 55% versus 62% for placebo, although the aggregate microvascular outcome (nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy) did not differ between arms (11.3% versus 12.4%). Only the Da Qing 30-year follow-up reports benefit at the outcome level: median diabetes onset delayed 3.96 years, cardiovascular events HR 0.74 (0.59-0.92), cardiovascular death HR 0.67 (0.48-0.94), all-cause death HR 0.74 (0.61-0.89) and life expectancy +1.44 years. Against that, over a median 21 years in DPP/DPPOS neither lifestyle (HR 1.14, 0.87-1.50) nor metformin reduced major cardiovascular events, and the pragmatic UK Let's Prevent cluster trial (n=880) found a non-significant 26% reduction (HR 0.74, 0.48-1.14) with an HbA1c change of only -0.06 percentage points. Real-world delivery is weaker than the trials: a network meta-analysis of 63 effectiveness and translation studies (n=17,272) found diabetes incidence of 9% versus 12% in the controlled subset (RR 0.71, 0.58-0.88) and only 1.5 kg more weight lost (95% CI -2.2 to -0.8) than control, well short of the 4 kg or more achieved in the original trials. All of this is prevention evidence in prediabetes; the analogous trial in established type 2 diabetes (Look AHEAD) is covered under weight loss.

Doses studied: DPP: goal of at least 7% weight loss and at least 150 minutes/week of moderate physical activity, delivered as a 16-session individual core curriculum followed by monthly contact, over a mean 2.8 years. Finnish DPS: individualised counselling on weight, total and saturated fat, fibre and physical activity, mean 3.2 years. Da Qing: clinic-level diet, exercise, or diet-plus-exercise advice for 6 years. Trials pooled by Cochrane ran for two to six years.

Why this grade: Three independent randomised trials in people with impaired glucose tolerance (US DPP n=3234, Finnish DPS n=522, Da Qing n=577) each roughly halved progression to a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, and a Cochrane meta-analysis of 11 trials (4511 participants) puts the pooled effect at RR 0.57 (95% CI 0.50-0.64) and rates that moderate-quality evidence. The 'strong' grade is carried specifically by the Da Qing 30-year follow-up, which is the only randomised trial here that found benefit at the level of hard outcomes, and it is a 577-person cluster-randomised trial whose result the larger DPP/DPPOS trial did not reproduce: fewer cardiovascular events, fewer cardiovascular and all-cause deaths, and 1.44 extra years of life expectancy. It is not carried by the surrogate data alone. Readers must weigh the other side: over a median 21 years the DPP/DPPOS cohort showed no reduction in major cardiovascular events (HR 1.14), the DPPOS aggregate microvascular outcome did not differ between arms, Cochrane found no mortality difference on very low-quality evidence and judged none of the 12 pooled trials at low risk of bias, and real-world versions of the programme deliver a fraction of the trial-level weight loss.

Sources (8)
  1. Reduction in the incidence of type 2 diabetes with lifestyle intervention or metformin.
    Knowler WC et al. (Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group), 2002 · PMID 11832527 · positive

    RCT, n=3234 adults with elevated fasting and post-load glucose (mean age 51, mean BMI 34.0), mean follow-up 2.8 years. Diabetes incidence 11.0, 7.8 and 4.8 cases per 100 person-years in the placebo, metformin and lifestyle groups; lifestyle reduced incidence by 58% (95% CI 48-66%) and metformin by 31% (17-43%) versus placebo, lifestyle significantly more effective than metformin. To prevent one case over three years, 6.9 people would need the lifestyle programme versus 13.9 for metformin. Outcome is a glucose-defined diagnosis, not a cardiovascular event.

  2. Prevention of type 2 diabetes mellitus by changes in lifestyle among subjects with impaired glucose tolerance.
    Tuomilehto J et al. (Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study Group), 2001 · PMID 11333990 · positive

    RCT, n=522 overweight middle-aged adults with impaired glucose tolerance (mean age 55, mean BMI 31), mean follow-up 3.2 years. Weight loss at year 1 was 4.2 kg (intervention) versus 0.8 kg (control). Cumulative diabetes incidence at 4 years was 11% (95% CI 6-15%) versus 23% (17-29%); risk reduced by 58% (P<0.001). No cardiovascular or mortality outcomes reported.

  3. Morbidity and mortality after lifestyle intervention for people with impaired glucose tolerance: 30-year results of the Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Outcome Study.
    Gong Q et al., 2019 · PMID 31036503 · positive

    Cluster-randomised trial, n=577 adults with impaired glucose tolerance, 6-year intervention and 30-year follow-up (540 of 576, 94%, assessed). Combined intervention group had a median delay in diabetes onset of 3.96 years (95% CI 1.25-6.67), fewer cardiovascular events (HR 0.74, 0.59-0.92), lower incidence of microvascular complications (HR 0.65, 0.45-0.95), fewer cardiovascular deaths (HR 0.67, 0.48-0.94), fewer all-cause deaths (HR 0.74, 0.61-0.89) and 1.44 years longer life expectancy (95% CI 0.20-2.68). This is the only trial in the set that found benefit at the level of hard outcomes - not the only one that looked. The larger DPP/DPPOS cohort reported hard cardiovascular outcomes over a median 21 years and found no benefit (HR 1.14, PMID 35603600). The outcome-level record is therefore one positive 577-person cluster-randomised trial against one null 3,234-person trial.

  4. Diet, physical activity or both for prevention or delay of type 2 diabetes mellitus and its associated complications in people at increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Hemmingsen B et al. (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2017 · PMID 29205264 · mixed

    Cochrane review of 12 RCTs randomising 5238 people, interventions lasting 2-6 years. Diet plus physical activity reduced diabetes incidence: 315 of 2122 (14.8%) versus 614 of 2389 (25.7%), RR 0.57 (95% CI 0.50-0.64), 11 trials, moderate-quality evidence. But all-cause mortality showed no difference (12 of 2049 versus 10 of 2050, RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.50-2.50, 10 trials, very low-quality evidence), cardiovascular mortality was rarely reported (very low quality), no trial was judged at low risk of bias across all domains, and the authors concluded that most trials did not investigate patient-important outcomes.

  5. Long-term effects of lifestyle intervention or metformin on diabetes development and microvascular complications over 15-year follow-up: the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study.
    Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group, 2015 · PMID 26377054 · mixed

    DPPOS follow-up of 2776 (88%) of the DPP cohort over a mean 15 years. Diabetes incidence reduced by 27% with lifestyle (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.65-0.83) and 18% with metformin (HR 0.82, 0.72-0.93) versus placebo, with between-group differences declining over time; cumulative incidence at year 15 was 55% (lifestyle), 56% (metformin) and 62% (placebo). Prevalence of the aggregate microvascular outcome did not differ significantly between groups (placebo 12.4%, metformin 13.0%, lifestyle 11.3%).

  6. Effects of Long-term Metformin and Lifestyle Interventions on Cardiovascular Events in the Diabetes Prevention Program and Its Outcome Study.
    Goldberg RB et al., 2022 · PMID 35603600 · null

    DPP/DPPOS, n=3234, median 21-year follow-up. Neither intervention reduced the primary outcome of nonfatal myocardial infarction, stroke or cardiovascular death: lifestyle versus placebo HR 1.14 (95% CI 0.87-1.50, P=0.34), metformin versus placebo HR 1.03 (0.78-1.37, P=0.81). No effect on the extended cardiovascular outcome either, despite long-term prevention of diabetes.

  7. A community based primary prevention programme for type 2 diabetes integrating identification and lifestyle intervention for prevention: the Let's Prevent Diabetes cluster randomised controlled trial.
    Davies MJ et al., 2016 · PMID 26740346 · null

    Pragmatic UK cluster RCT, 44 general practices, n=880 adults with prediabetes (mean age 64), 3-year follow-up. A 6-hour group education programme with annual refresher gave a non-significant 26% lower risk of type 2 diabetes (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.48-1.14, P=0.18), with small but statistically significant improvements in HbA1c (-0.06 percentage points, 95% CI -0.11 to -0.01), LDL cholesterol, sedentary time and step count.

  8. Global Diabetes Prevention Interventions: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis of the Real-World Impact on Incidence, Weight, and Glucose.
    Galaviz KI et al., 2018 · PMID 29934481 · mixed

    Systematic review and network meta-analysis of 63 real-world effectiveness and translation studies (n=17,272, mean age 49.7 years). In the controlled studies only (n=7), diabetes cumulative incidence was 9% with intervention versus 12% with control (absolute risk reduction 3%, RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.58-0.88). Intervention participants lost only 1.5 kg more weight (95% CI -2.2 to -0.8) and achieved a 0.09 mmol/L greater fasting glucose decrease (-0.15 to -0.03) than controls, far less than in the original efficacy trials.
