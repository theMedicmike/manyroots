White and pink noise for sleep

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

A white-noise machine can genuinely help if what is keeping you awake is traffic, a neighbour, or a snoring partner, because masking a disruptive sound is a real and understandable mechanism, and a small laboratory study did show steady sound blunting the effect of traffic noise on sleep. What is much weaker is the claim that continuous noise improves sleep in and of itself: the careful reviews rate that evidence as very low quality, and the studies disagree about whether noise helps or actually disturbs sleep. Nobody has properly tested one of these machines in ordinary adults with insomnia at home. If you do try one, keep the volume low and the speaker across the room rather than beside your head, because these machines can get loud enough to be a hearing concern.

Safety

Volume, not the sound itself, is the documented risk. A 2024 scoping review found commercially available white noise machines can exceed 91 dB at maximum volume, above the NIOSH exposure guideline for a 2-hour work shift in adults, and concluded that existing data support limiting the maximum sound intensity and duration of these devices. Animal models show deleterious effects of continuous moderate-intensity white noise on early development, with human data generally corroborating, although the same review noted low-intensity exposure during sleep may be beneficial. The systematic review of noise as a sleep aid separately warns that continuous noise may negatively affect both sleep and hearing. Practical mitigation: place the device across the room rather than beside the head or beside a crib, and keep the volume well below conversational level — the one controlled overnight pink-noise study used 45 dB, roughly the level of a quiet library. Long-term nightly use in adults has not been studied for hearing outcomes, so absence of reported harm here reflects absence of study, not demonstrated safety.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications for adults. Particular caution with infants and young children given the developmental evidence and the tendency to place devices close to a crib; the review authors called for limits on device intensity and duration for this reason. People with tinnitus or hyperacusis should introduce masking sound cautiously and at low volume, since responses to sound enrichment vary widely. Anyone who needs to hear an alarm, a baby monitor, a medical alert, or a smoke detector overnight should ensure masking noise does not obscure it.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. The relevant practical interaction is with anything else that impairs the ability to be woken by a genuine alarm, including sedative-hypnotics, alcohol, and earplugs used alongside a noise machine.

What the evidence says

The evidence base for continuous white or pink noise as a sleep aid is far thinner than the size of the market for these devices would suggest. The most rigorous synthesis screened all studies of continuous white or similar broadband noise and sleep, included 38 articles, and found heterogeneity in noise characteristics, sleep measurement methodology, adherence and control conditions such that findings ranged from continuous noise improving sleep to continuous noise disrupting it; applying GRADE criteria, the quality of evidence that continuous noise improves sleep was VERY LOW, which the authors noted contradicts its widespread use. A 2025 meta-analysis of 12 RCTs and 1,301 participants did report PSQI reductions with white noise in adults aged 18-64 (MD -3.70, 95% CI -4.90 to -2.50, P<0.001) and adults 65 and over (MD -2.71, 95% CI -4.98 to -0.44, P=0.02), and in both ICU (MD -4.04) and non-ICU (MD -2.61) settings, but the adult PSQI analysis showed substantial heterogeneity and the pooled populations were dominated by hospitalised and critically ill patients rather than community adults with insomnia disorder. The best-known bedroom study enrolled 10 adults in New York City complaining of sleep difficulty from environmental noise using an uncontrolled within-subject ABA design; actigraphic wake after sleep onset (p=0.007) and diary sleep latency (p=0.016) improved, but the change in number of awakenings did not survive Holm's sequential correction (which required p<0.01) and the change in sleep efficiency did not reach significance (p=0.063). The masking mechanism has better-controlled support: in a 12-participant polysomnographic crossover, traffic noise at 45-65 dB produced acute sleep fragmentation and metabolite shifts that were attenuated when continuous 45 dB broadband pink noise was played through the night, though the authors themselves urge caution given the sample size and homogeneity. A distinct and better-controlled literature on closed-loop acoustic stimulation shows that 50 ms pink-noise bursts phase-locked to the up-phase of slow oscillations increase slow-wave activity and slow-wave sleep, including across 14 nights at home in patients with Alzheimer's disease, though with substantial individual variability; that technique requires EEG detection hardware and is not the same intervention as a bedside machine emitting continuous sound.

Doses studied: Continuous white or broadband noise played through the night; a widely cited bedroom study used a commercial white-noise device in the participant's own bedroom for one week. Hospital studies used roughly 50-60 dB delivered via earphones or app; a laboratory crossover used continuous 45 dB broadband pink noise across a full night. Noise characteristics, volume, adherence and speaker placement were reported inconsistently across the literature, which is one reason the evidence cannot be pooled cleanly. Closed-loop research applies 50 ms pink-noise bursts at approximately 40 dB, phase-locked to slow oscillations via an EEG headband, which is a fundamentally different exposure from a continuously running bedside machine.

Why this grade: Human studies exist, but the most rigorous systematic review of continuous broadband noise as a sleep aid rated the quality of evidence that it improves sleep as VERY LOW under GRADE and stated plainly that this contradicts its widespread use, with findings across studies ranging from noise improving sleep to noise disrupting it. The one supportive meta-analysis is heterogeneous and its adult data are dominated by hospitalised and critically ill patients rather than community adults with insomnia disorder; a dedicated search for a trial in community adults with chronic insomnia disorder returned nothing. The best-known bedroom study had 10 participants and no control group, and the only continuous-pink-noise polysomnography trial had 12 healthy participants. Not graded moderate because no adequately powered, controlled trial in the target population was found. The separate closed-loop pink-noise literature is mechanistically real but tests a different intervention entirely and should not be used to prop up this grade.

Sources (6)
  1. Noise as a sleep aid: A systematic review.
    Riedy SM, Smith MG, Rocha S, Basner M, 2020 (Sleep Med Rev) · PMID 33007706 · mixed

    PROSPERO-registered systematic review (CRD42020148736) of all studies of continuous white or similar broadband noise and sleep; 38 articles included from three databases with independent dual screening and risk-of-bias assessment. Found heterogeneity in noise characteristics, sleep measurement methodology, adherence, and control conditions, with resulting variability in findings 'the extremes being that continuous noise improves or disrupts sleep.' Following GRADE criteria, the quality of evidence for continuous noise improving sleep was VERY LOW, which the authors state contradicts its widespread use. They conclude additional research with objective sleep measures and detailed descriptions of noise exposure is needed before promoting continuous noise as a sleep aid, especially since it may also negatively affect sleep and hearing.

  2. Impact of white noise on sleep quality across age groups and in critically ill/non-critically ill patients: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Ding Y, Sun X, Yin J, et al., 2025 (Sleep Med) · PMID 41151421 · positive

    12 RCTs, 1,301 participants (356 infants and young children, 648 adults aged 18-64, 297 adults 65+; 544 critically ill, 401 non-critically ill). White noise significantly reduced PSQI scores in adults (MD -3.70, 95% CI -4.90 to -2.50, P<0.001) and older adults (MD -2.71, 95% CI -4.98 to -0.44, P=0.02), and in both ICU (MD -4.04, 95% CI -6.35 to -1.73) and non-ICU (MD -2.61, 95% CI -3.84 to -1.38) settings. In infants results were mixed: 24-h total sleep time increased (MD 137.51 min, P=0.0001), 12-h sleep efficiency increased (MD 6.62%, P=0.008) and awakenings fell at 24 h (MD -19.42, P=0.02) and 12 h (MD -1.83, P=0.006), but nocturnal 12-h total sleep time did not increase (MD 102.56 min, 95% CI -39.91 to 245.04, P=0.16), 24-h sleep efficiency did not improve (P=0.46) and 24-h WASO was unchanged (P=0.46). Sensitivity analysis showed substantial heterogeneity in adult PSQI scores, though leave-one-out analysis left the pooled direction unchanged; the authors note the study is limited by heterogeneity and methodological quality.

  3. Pink noise reduces impact of traffic noise on sleep and the blood metabolome: a cross-over pilot study.
    Vincens N, Nause A, Basner M, et al., 2026 (Commun Med) · PMID 41513961 · positive

    Crossover pilot trial (NCT05319262) in which 12 healthy individuals slept five consecutive nights in acoustically isolated bedrooms with polysomnography: a familiarisation night, a quiet baseline night, a night with road, rail and air traffic noise at 45-65 dB, a night with continuous 45 dB broadband pink noise, and a night with both. Discrete traffic noise events induced acute elevations of the odds ratio product indicating sleep fragmentation even while total sleep time and overall macrostructure were preserved, and were associated with elevated leucine, lactic acid and acetone. Both the sleep and metabolic disturbances were attenuated when pink noise was played continuously through the night. The authors state results should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and subject homogeneity; participants were healthy, not adults with insomnia disorder.

  4. The effects of white noise on sleep and duration in individuals living in a high noise environment in New York City.
    Ebben MR, Yan P, Krieger AC, 2021 (Sleep Med) · PMID 34049045 · mixed

    10 adults recruited from a New York City sleep clinic with sleep disturbance and high reported environmental noise, studied in a within-subject ABA design with no control group (one-week baseline, one-week white noise device, one-week washout). Actigraphic wake after sleep onset improved, t(9)=3.438, p=0.007, and diary-reported sleep latency improved, t(9)=2.947, p=0.016. The number of awakenings showed only a trend, t(9)=2.622, p=0.028, which did NOT meet the Holm's sequential corrected threshold of p<0.01, and sleep efficiency by actigraphy did not reach significance, t(9)=-2.121, p=0.063.

  5. Continuous white noise exposure during sleep and childhood development: A scoping review.
    De Jong RW, Davis GS, Chelf CJ, Marinelli JP, Erbele ID, Bowe SN, 2024 (Sleep Med) · PMID 38663282 · negative

    PRISMA-ScR scoping review; 20 of 644 retrieved publications met inclusion criteria, covering 83 animal and 9,428 human subjects. White noise machines can exceed 91 dB on maximum volume, which exceeds National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health noise exposure guidelines for a 2-hour work shift in adults. Evidence suggests deleterious effects of continuous moderate-intensity white noise exposure on early development in animal models, generally corroborated by human data, although studies also suggest low-intensity exposure during sleep may be beneficial. The authors conclude that existing data support limiting the maximal sound intensity and duration of commercially available white noise devices.

  6. Acoustic Stimulation to Improve Slow-Wave Sleep in Alzheimer's Disease: A Multiple Night At-Home Intervention.
    Van den Bulcke L, Davidoff H, Heremans E, et al., 2024 (Am J Geriatr Psychiatry) · PMID 39048400 · mixed

    15 older patients with Alzheimer's disease (6 women, mean age 76.27, mean MoCA 16.07) wore a DREEM2 headband for two baseline nights then 14 nights during which the device randomly delivered either 50 ms pink noise bursts (approximately 40 dB) phase-targeted to the slow-wave up-phase or sham. At group level, stimulation significantly enhanced slow-wave activity and slow-wave sleep, sustained throughout the intervention. However, substantial variability existed in individual response, and patients with lower baseline slow-wave sleep received fewer stimulations on average, possibly resulting in less enhancement. This is a phase-locked EEG-triggered stimulus, not continuous bedside noise.
