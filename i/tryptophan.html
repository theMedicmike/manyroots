Minerals & nutrients

L-tryptophan

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Tryptophan is an amino acid from protein foods that your body converts into serotonin and then melatonin, and it has been studied as a sleep aid since the 1970s. Pooled analysis suggests it may modestly shorten the time you spend awake in the middle of the night, mostly at doses of 1 gram or more, but it does not clearly help with anything else about sleep. It is worth knowing that the American Academy of Sleep Medicine reviewed the evidence and recommends against using it for insomnia, and that in real-world practice most people who start on it move to something stronger within a few months — so this is a genuinely mixed picture rather than a settled one.

Safety

The most important safety fact about L-tryptophan is historical. An epidemic of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome — a serious and sometimes fatal illness featuring debilitating myalgia and marked peripheral eosinophilia — reached epidemic proportions in the US in late 1989 and early 1990, with 1,536 cases and 27 deaths reported as of August 1990; death typically followed ascending polyneuropathy with paralysis and respiratory arrest. The aetiology was traced to a contaminant in the bulk manufacturing process at a single Japanese company, and use of L-tryptophan as a dietary supplement was discontinued in 1989. Modern pharmaceutical-grade tryptophan has not been associated with a comparable outbreak, but this episode is why supplement sourcing and purity matter here more than for most nutrients. Reported side effects in trials are mild — daytime drowsiness, nausea, headache and dizziness, more common at higher doses. Doses used in the literature ranged from 1 g to 15 g; safety of chronic use at the upper end of that range is not established, and tryptophan supplements have not been studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Who should be careful: Avoid alongside serotonergic medication without clinician supervision. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding (unstudied). People with a history of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome or scleroderma-like conditions, and people with liver or kidney impairment, should not take tryptophan supplements without medical advice. Do not use before driving or operating machinery until you know how it affects you, and have persistent insomnia evaluated rather than self-treated.

Interactions: The following reflect established pharmacology rather than findings from the trials cited above. Tryptophan raises serotonin and should not be combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAO inhibitors, tricyclic antidepressants, triptans, tramadol, lithium or St John's wort without medical supervision, because of serotonin syndrome risk. Additive sedation is possible with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, antihistamines and other sedating medication. Because tryptophan competes with other large neutral amino acids for transport into the brain, taking it with a high-protein meal may blunt any effect.

What the evidence says

Evidence for L-tryptophan in insomnia and poor sleep in adults is mixed and modest. A 2022 systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression (Sutanto 2022) collected 18 articles and pooled data from 4: tryptophan shortened wake after sleep onset by 81.03 minutes per gram (p = 0.017; standardised mean difference -1.08, 95% CI -1.89 to -0.28), and doses of 1 g or more produced shorter wake after sleep onset than doses under 1 g (28.91 vs 56.55 minutes, p = 0.001) — but it did not affect any other sleep component. A double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in 98 adults genotyped for 5-HTTLPR (van Dalfsen 2019) found 1000 mg/day for one week improved actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset regardless of genotype, with only a marginally significant subjective sleep-quality gain. A randomised placebo-controlled trial in 80 detoxified individuals with drug dependence (Wang 2016; 45 completers) found 1000 mg/day for two weeks reduced Athens Insomnia Scale scores significantly more than placebo (p = 0.017) while producing no benefit on any Symptom Check-List-90 dimension. Against this, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline (Sateia 2017) applied GRADE and suggests clinicians NOT use tryptophan for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults (weak recommendation against), grouping it with melatonin, valerian, diphenhydramine, trazodone and tiagabine. Real-world data point the same way: in a German observational study of graduated drug therapy for moderate-to-severe chronic insomnia (Boer 2025), 60.5% of patients began on l-tryptophan as the least potent first step, but only 12.5% were still taking it at three months, most having switched to antidepressants, hypnotics or combination therapy. An earlier systematic review of complementary treatments (Sarris 2010) likewise classified the evidence for L-tryptophan as mixed, and the older literature (Schneider-Helmert 1986) reported first-night sleep-latency reductions mainly in younger people with situational insomnia, with chronic insomnia requiring repeated low doses and benefit appearing late in treatment or even after discontinuation — a pattern hard to distinguish from regression to the mean.

Doses studied: 1 g/day was the most common dose in modern trials; the older literature used 1 g to 15 g at bedtime. The meta-analysis found doses of 1 g or more gave shorter wake after sleep onset than doses under 1 g

Why this grade: Graded down from 'moderate' and up from 'not_supported'. A 2022 meta-analysis exists and is positive, but it pooled only 4 studies and found an effect on a single endpoint (wake after sleep onset) with no effect on any other sleep component; most primary trials are small, several are decades old, and the populations were often not people with clinically diagnosed insomnia. Against that, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's guideline explicitly suggests clinicians NOT use tryptophan for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults. 'not_supported' was considered because a guideline recommending against literally meets that definition, but was rejected: the AASM recommendation is graded WEAK and reflects low evidence quality rather than a demonstrated absence of effect, and a later meta-analysis is positive on one endpoint. With a narrow positive meta-analytic signal on one side and a guideline recommendation against on the other, plus real-world data showing most patients escalate off it within three months, 'early' is the conservative and honest grade.

  3. Effect of graduated drug therapy for moderate-to-severe chronic insomnia on the severity of disease: an observational study in Germany.
    Boer J, Toncar T, Stange A, Rosenblum L, Fietze I, 2025 · PMID 39172069 · mixed

    Observational study of a graduated therapy scheme in 86 eligible patients with moderate-to-severe chronic insomnia, l-tryptophan being the least potent first choice. 60.5% started on l-tryptophan; by 3 months only 12.5% were still taking it, and by 6 months 22.2% remained on l-tryptophan, melatonin or agomelatine, the remainder having switched to antidepressants, hypnotics, daridorexant or combinations. Bears on durable real-world effectiveness rather than short-term efficacy.

  4. The serotonin transporter gene-linked polymorphic region (5-HTTLPR) and the sleep-promoting effects of tryptophan: A randomized placebo-controlled crossover study.
    van Dalfsen JH, Markus CR, 2019 · PMID 31237183 · positive

    Double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in 98 adults (47 homozygous S-allele, 51 homozygous L-allele), 1000 mg/day tryptophan for one week. Improved actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset irrespective of allelic variation; the subjective sleep-quality improvement was only marginally significant and confined to the S-allele group.

  5. Tryptophan for the sleeping disorder and mental symptom of new-type drug dependence: A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.
    Wang D, Li W, Xiao Y, et al., 2016 · PMID 27428201 · mixed

    80 detoxified individuals with new-type drug dependence randomised to tryptophan 1000 mg/day or placebo for 2 weeks; 45 completed (24 tryptophan, 21 placebo). Reduction in Athens Insomnia Scale score was significantly greater with tryptophan (p = 0.017), but no significant differences appeared on any Symptom Check-List-90 dimension or the overall score (all p > 0.05).

  6. A systematic review of insomnia and complementary medicine.
    Sarris J, Byrne GJ, 2010 · PMID 20965131 · mixed

    Systematic review; 64 complementary-medicine RCTs for chronic insomnia identified, 20 met inclusion criteria. Reported evidentiary support for acupressure, tai chi and yoga, but classified the evidence for L-tryptophan as mixed, and noted many trials were excluded for small sample size or inadequate control conditions.

  7. Evaluation of L-tryptophan for treatment of insomnia: a review.
    Schneider-Helmert D, Spinweber CL, 1986 · PMID 3090582 · positive

    Review of sleep-laboratory and outpatient studies. Reported first-night reductions in sleep latency at doses of 1-15 g in younger people with situational insomnia only; in chronic or severe insomnia, benefit required repeated low doses and appeared late in treatment or even after discontinuation. Reported absence of side effects and no development of tolerance in long-term use.

  8. Tryptophan toxicity: a pharmacoepidemiologic review of eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome.
    Milburn DS, Myers CW, 1991 · PMID 1763543 · negative

    Pharmacoepidemiologic review of the eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome (EMS) epidemic linked to tryptophan supplements: EMS reached epidemic proportions in the US in late 1989 and early 1990, with 1,536 cases and 27 deaths reported as of August 1990. Features included debilitating myalgia and marked peripheral eosinophilia; death typically followed ascending polyneuropathy with paralysis and respiratory arrest. Aetiology traced to a contaminant in the bulk manufacturing process of tryptophan at a single Japanese company.
