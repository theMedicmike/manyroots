Conventional medicine

NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)

Strong evidence · for migraine & headache Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Not supported by evidence · for gut & digestion

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For migraine & headache

Ordinary anti-inflammatory painkillers - ibuprofen, aspirin, diclofenac - genuinely help a migraine attack, and the trial evidence for that is solid. But be realistic about how much: with ibuprofen 400 mg, about a quarter of people are completely pain-free at two hours compared with about one in eight on a dummy pill, and around half get some meaningful relief. They are less effective than triptans, an anti-inflammatory combined with a triptan works better than either alone, and taking them often carries real stomach and heart risks.

Safety

In single-dose migraine trials, adverse events with ibuprofen and diclofenac occurred at the same rate as placebo and were mild and transient; aspirin was slightly above placebo. The meaningful risks come from repeated or high-dose use: dyspepsia, gastric and duodenal ulceration, upper gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation (increased by every NSAID regimen studied), fluid retention, raised blood pressure, worsening of heart failure (risk roughly doubled by all NSAIDs), acute kidney injury, and ischaemic cardiovascular events with diclofenac and, for coronary events, ibuprofen. Naproxen carried the lowest vascular risk of the NSAIDs studied but among the highest gastrointestinal risk. Frequent use also risks medication-overuse headache; guidance generally caps simple analgesic and NSAID use at fewer than 15 days per month, and the AMPP cohort found NSAID use was associated with progression to chronic migraine specifically at high headache-day counts.

Who should be careful: Active or prior peptic ulcer or gastrointestinal bleeding; established ischaemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease or heart failure (diclofenac in particular); severe renal or hepatic impairment; aspirin- or NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease (aspirin-sensitive asthma with nasal polyps); third trimester of pregnancy (premature ductus arteriosus closure), and avoidance is generally advised from 20 weeks. Aspirin should not be given to children or teenagers with viral illness (Reye's syndrome). Caution in older adults, dehydration and chronic kidney disease.

Interactions: Additive bleeding risk with anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs), antiplatelets and SSRIs/SNRIs. Ibuprofen taken close to low-dose aspirin can blunt aspirin's cardioprotective antiplatelet effect. Increased nephrotoxicity and reduced antihypertensive effect when combined with ACE inhibitors or ARBs plus diuretics (the 'triple whammy'). Raise lithium and methotrexate levels. Additive gastrointestinal risk with corticosteroids. Do not combine two NSAIDs.

What the evidence says

NSAIDs are used for ACUTE attacks; they are not daily preventives. Cochrane meta-analyses put the benefit and its ceiling clearly: ibuprofen 400 mg gave 2-hour pain freedom in 26% versus 12% on placebo (NNT 7.2), 2-hour headache relief in 57% versus 25% (NNT 3.2) and 24-hour sustained relief in 45% versus 19% (NNT 4.0) across 9 studies and 4,373 participants, with adverse events occurring at the same rate as placebo. Diclofenac potassium 50 mg gave NNTs of 6.2 for 2-hour pain freedom and 8.9 for 2-hour headache relief across 5 studies and 1,356 participants, with the reviewers noting only a minority of patients achieve pain-free responses. Aspirin 900-1000 mg gave NNTs of 8.1, 4.9 and 6.6 for 2-hour pain freedom, 2-hour relief and 24-hour relief in 13 studies and 4,222 participants, and the reviewers judged it similar to sumatriptan 50 mg or 100 mg overall - though sumatriptan 100 mg did beat aspirin plus metoclopramide for 2-hour pain freedom - with added metoclopramide 10 mg significantly reducing nausea (P<0.00006) and vomiting (P=0.002). The most important negative findings are comparative: the 2025 ACP network meta-analysis rated as HIGH certainty that triptans, and triptan-NSAID combinations, are more effective than NSAIDs alone for pain outcomes at 2 hours and pain freedom up to 48 hours, and a Cochrane review of sumatriptan plus naproxen found the combination superior to either monotherapy. Class harms are established in long-term high-dose trials rather than migraine dosing: an individual-participant meta-analysis of 280 placebo-controlled NSAID trials found diclofenac raised major vascular events by about a third (RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.12-1.78), ibuprofen raised major coronary events (RR 2.22, 1.10-4.48), naproxen did not raise major vascular events (RR 0.93, 0.69-1.27), all NSAIDs roughly doubled heart failure risk, and upper gastrointestinal complications rose for every regimen (ibuprofen RR 3.97, naproxen 4.22). Frequency matters: in the AMPP cohort NSAIDs were overall neutral for progression to chronic migraine (OR 0.85, 0.63-1.17) and protective at low-to-moderate headache frequency, but were associated with increased risk at high monthly headache-day counts.

Doses studied: Single dose per attack. Ibuprofen 200 mg or 400 mg (400 mg better than 200 mg; soluble formulations faster at 1 hour); diclofenac potassium 50 mg; aspirin 900-1000 mg, with or without metoclopramide 10 mg; naproxen 500 mg studied in combination with sumatriptan 50 or 85 mg.

Why this grade: Several independent Cochrane meta-analyses (ibuprofen, diclofenac, aspirin) plus two large network meta-analyses show consistent superiority over placebo for acute attacks. Graded strong for benefit over placebo, but the entry carries two honest qualifiers: the effect is modest - only a minority achieve complete pain freedom - and high-certainty evidence places NSAID monotherapy below triptans and below triptan-NSAID combinations.

For chronic pain

Anti-inflammatory pills like ibuprofen, naproxen and diclofenac do reduce arthritis and back pain more than a dummy pill, and that part is real and consistent. But the average amount of relief is smaller than most people expect: for back pain it is small enough that researchers question whether patients would notice it, and for sciatica the pooled trials showed no clear pain benefit at all. They also carry genuine risks to the stomach, heart and kidneys that grow with dose and duration, so they are generally used at the lowest dose for the shortest time that helps.

Safety

Dose- and duration-dependent risks are the central issue. Pooled individual-participant data from 124,513 randomised participants show coxibs and diclofenac raise major vascular events by roughly a third, all NSAIDs roughly double heart failure risk, and every regimen increases upper gastrointestinal complications (perforation, obstruction, bleeding) — naproxen RR 4.22 and ibuprofen RR 3.97 versus placebo. Renal effects include fluid retention, raised blood pressure and reduced glomerular filtration. In spinal pain trials, gastrointestinal reactions were 2.5 times more frequent even though the median trial lasted 7 days, and Cochrane reviewers noted that short, small trials likely underestimate the true adverse event rate — so real-world long-term rates are probably higher than the trial literature suggests. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest period, and reassess rather than continue indefinitely.

Who should be careful: Should not be used by people with active peptic ulcer or gastrointestinal bleeding, severe heart failure, established ischaemic heart disease or cerebrovascular disease, significant chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis with ascites, or a history of NSAID- or aspirin-triggered asthma, urticaria or angioedema. Avoid in the third trimester of pregnancy and in the peri-operative period after coronary artery bypass grafting. Use great caution in adults over 65 and in anyone who is volume-depleted.

Interactions: Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs), antiplatelets and low-dose aspirin markedly increase bleeding risk. SSRIs and SNRIs, including duloxetine, further raise gastrointestinal bleeding risk when combined with an NSAID. The combination of an NSAID with an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a diuretic substantially increases the risk of acute kidney injury. NSAIDs also raise lithium and methotrexate levels, and combining with corticosteroids compounds ulcer risk. Do not combine two oral NSAIDs, and do not add an oral NSAID on top of a topical NSAID without medical advice.

What the evidence says

Evidence for oral NSAIDs in chronic musculoskeletal pain is extensive but the effect size is modest and varies by pain site. In knee and hip osteoarthritis, a network meta-analysis of 192 trials (102,829 participants) found five oral preparations cleared the minimal clinically important reduction in pain with at least 99% probability — diclofenac 150 mg/day, etoricoxib 60 and 90 mg/day, and rofecoxib 25 and 50 mg/day, the last of which is a withdrawn drug. In chronic low back pain the picture is weaker: a Cochrane review of 13 trials found a mean pain difference of only -3.30 points (95% CI -5.33 to -1.27) on a 0-100 scale versus placebo, rated low-quality, with the difference shrinking when only low-risk-of-bias trials were included. A meta-analysis of 35 placebo-controlled spinal pain trials concluded the effect was statistically real but clinically unimportant, with a number-needed-to-treat of 6 (95% CI 4 to 10). In sciatica, a Cochrane review of 10 trials found NSAIDs did not significantly reduce pain versus placebo (mean difference -4.56, 95% CI -11.11 to 1.99), falling to -0.09 once a single 8-hour trial was removed, while adverse effects rose. Long-term data are thin: across 47 trials of 12 months or longer in knee osteoarthritis, celecoxib showed a pain effect of SMD -0.18 (95% CrI -0.35 to -0.01) that did not survive sensitivity analysis. Harms are well quantified: coxibs and diclofenac increased major vascular events by roughly a third, heart failure risk was roughly doubled by all NSAIDs, and every regimen increased upper gastrointestinal complications.

Doses studied: The osteoarthritis network meta-analysis evaluated 90 active preparations or doses, 68 of them NSAIDs (oral and topical combined), including diclofenac 150 mg/day and etoricoxib 60 and 90 mg/day. Chronic low back pain trials used standard licensed daily doses; median trial duration in the spinal pain meta-analysis was only 7 days, and median follow-up in the Cochrane chronic low back pain review was 56 days.

Why this grade: Many placebo-controlled RCTs and several large meta-analyses show a statistically significant benefit over placebo, and major guidelines (ACR 2019) strongly recommend oral NSAIDs for osteoarthritis. But the size of the benefit is the problem, and it is not uniform across pain sites. In spinal pain the pooled effect falls below the accepted threshold for clinical importance; Cochrane rated the chronic low back pain evidence 'low quality' with effects shrinking further in low-risk-of-bias trials; in sciatica a separate Cochrane review found no significant pain reduction versus placebo at all; and the only network meta-analysis of trials lasting 12 months or more could not establish a durable effect. Combined with dose-dependent cardiovascular, heart-failure and gastrointestinal harms in a 124,513-participant individual-participant meta-analysis, this sits at moderate rather than strong. Graded down from strong because the effect is modest, inconsistent by pain site, and the long-term evidence is weak.

For gut & digestion

ImportantBlack or tarry stools, blood in the stool, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds means a gastrointestinal bleed - an emergency, seek care now. Otherwise, if an NSAID was prescribed for you, don't stop or change it on your own; talk to your prescriber.

If you have indigestion, stomach pain, or you have noticed black stools or blood, look at your painkillers first. Ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and their relatives cause stomach and gut damage - ulcers, bleeding and perforation - and in the best trial evidence available every single one of them raised that risk. The higher the dose and the longer the course, the bigger the risk. This is not a reason to suffer in pain: it is a reason to have the conversation with a pharmacist or doctor about the lowest effective dose, a stomach-protecting medicine alongside it, or a different approach entirely. Never ignore black or bloody stools or vomiting that looks like coffee grounds - that is an emergency.

Safety

🔴 Black or tarry stools, blood in the stool, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds means a gastrointestinal bleed - that is an emergency, not something to monitor. Stop the NSAID and seek care immediately. Risk rises with higher dose, longer use, age, a previous ulcer, Helicobacter pylori infection, and any combination with another drug on the interaction list below. NSAIDs also damage the small bowel (NSAID enteropathy), which upper endoscopy will not show. Beyond the gut, the same pooled trial data found heart failure risk roughly doubled by all NSAIDs and major vascular events raised by about a third with coxibs and diclofenac, and NSAIDs raise blood pressure and blunt blood-pressure medicines - so the cost is not confined to the stomach.

Who should be careful: Active peptic ulcer or gastrointestinal bleeding; a history of NSAID-associated ulcer or bleed; severe heart failure; established ischaemic heart disease, peripheral arterial disease or cerebrovascular disease (for coxibs and diclofenac especially); significant chronic kidney disease; cirrhosis with ascites; the third trimester of pregnancy; aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease and NSAID-triggered asthma; and after coronary artery bypass surgery.

Interactions: Anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban) and antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel) - substantially higher bleeding risk, and low-dose aspirin plus an NSAID is a common and underestimated combination. Corticosteroids - additive ulcer risk. SSRIs and SNRIs - raise gastrointestinal bleeding risk, and this matters because those drugs are graded on the depression and anxiety pages of this site. ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and diuretics - the triple combination with an NSAID risks acute kidney injury, and NSAIDs blunt the blood-pressure-lowering effect. Lithium and methotrexate - NSAIDs raise their blood levels. A proton-pump inhibitor taken alongside reduces but does not eliminate upper gastrointestinal risk and does not protect the small bowel.

What the evidence says

CNT Collaboration 2013 (280 placebo-controlled trials, 124,513 participants, 68,342 person-years, plus 474 head-to-head trials in 229,296 participants) - ALL NSAID regimens increased upper gastrointestinal complications (perforation, obstruction or bleed): coxibs RR 1.81 (95% CI 1.17-2.81, p=0.0070); diclofenac 1.89 (1.16-3.09, p=0.0106); ibuprofen 3.97 (2.22-7.10, p<0.0001); naproxen 4.22 (2.71-6.56, p<0.0001). The same analysis found heart failure risk roughly DOUBLED by all NSAIDs, and major vascular events raised about a third by coxibs (1.37) and diclofenac (1.41) - of 1,000 people on a coxib or diclofenac for a year, three more had a major vascular event, one of them fatal. SOS project 2012 (28 observational studies) gives the per-drug picture: relative risk of upper GI complications versus non-use ranged from 1.43 for aceclofenac, 1.45 for celecoxib (1.17-1.81) and 1.84 for ibuprofen (1.54-2.20), through 3.34 for diclofenac (2.79-3.99), 4.10 for naproxen (3.22-5.23) and 4.14 for indometacin, up to 7.43 for piroxicam (5.19-10.63), 11.50 for ketorolac (5.56-23.78) and 18.45 for azapropazone. High daily doses carried two to three times the risk of low doses. HONEST DISCREPANCY: the randomised and observational data disagree about ibuprofen - the RCT pooling put its GI risk on a par with naproxen (3.97 vs 4.22), while the observational studies put ibuprofen among the LOWEST (1.84) and naproxen high (4.10). Do not read ibuprofen as the safe one on the strength of the observational figure alone. That the damage is real and preventable is confirmed from the other direction: a 2025 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs found rebamipide reduced NSAID-induced gastrointestinal mucosal breaks versus placebo (RR 0.55, 0.31-0.99) and performed comparably to standard proton-pump inhibitors (RR 1.00, 0.51-1.95).

Doses studied: Risk is dose- and duration-dependent: the SOS meta-analysis found high daily doses carried two to three times the upper-gastrointestinal risk of low daily doses, and the CNT trials were largely of high-dose, long-term regimens typical of arthritis treatment. There is no established dose at which the gastrointestinal risk becomes zero. Topical NSAIDs are a separate node on this site precisely because they deliver far less systemic drug.

Why this grade: This is a HARM edge, not a treatment edge. NSAIDs are not a therapy for digestive symptoms - they are a well-established cause of them, and they appear on this page so that anyone with unexplained indigestion, stomach pain or bleeding checks what is in their painkiller drawer. The evidence is as good as evidence gets: the 2013 Coxib and traditional NSAID Trialists (CNT) Collaboration pooled individual participant data from 280 placebo-controlled trials (124,513 participants) and found that EVERY NSAID regimen tested increased upper gastrointestinal complications - defined as perforation, obstruction or bleeding - with rate ratios of 1.81 for coxibs, 1.89 for diclofenac, 3.97 for ibuprofen and 4.22 for naproxen, the last two at p<0.0001. Observational data across 28 studies put the per-drug relative risk anywhere from 1.45 for celecoxib to 11.50 for ketorolac, and found high daily doses carry two to three times the risk of low doses. Adequate evidence showing net harm for the organ system in question, and guidelines that recommend against NSAIDs in anyone with ulcer or bleeding risk, is not_supported by the rule on this site. Nothing here says NSAIDs do not work for pain - they do, and they are graded separately on the chronic pain page. What it says is that the gut is where they take their toll.

  3. Efficacy of Rebamipide in the Prevention of Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug-Induced Gastrointestinal Mucosal Breaks: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Meta-analysisCion RIA, Juyad IGA, Yasay EB; Am J Gastroenterol 2025 · PMID 40366001 · positive

    13 RCTs. Rebamipide reduced the incidence of NSAID-induced gastrointestinal mucosal breaks versus placebo (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31-0.99) and was comparable to standard proton-pump inhibitors (RR 1.00, 0.51-1.95). Adding rebamipide to a PPI showed no clear additional benefit (RR 0.72, 0.43-1.21). Included here as confirmation from the other direction: the mucosal injury is real enough to be measurably prevented.
