Benzodiazepines

Moderate evidence · for anxiety & stress

For anxiety & stress

Benzodiazepines (like diazepam, lorazepam or alprazolam) calm anxiety quickly, and trials do show they beat a placebo over a few weeks — though the quality of much of that older trial evidence is poor. The catch is what happens with ongoing use: the body adapts, stopping becomes hard, and long-term use is linked to memory and thinking problems that can linger even after someone comes off them, plus a higher risk of fractures in older people. That is why doctors generally reserve them for short-term or bridging use rather than as an everyday, long-term anxiety treatment.

Safety

These medicines act within hours, but tolerance and physical dependence develop with sustained daily use, and stopping abruptly after regular use can cause rebound anxiety, insomnia and, in severe cases, seizures — discontinuation should always be a gradual, supervised taper. Notably, the PubMed-retrieved Cochrane review states its included trials never examined dependency or withdrawal at all, so the trial literature underestimates these harms by construction. Meta-analytic evidence links long-term use to deficits in memory, attention, processing speed and visuoconstruction that persist even after successful withdrawal. The dementia question is unresolved: one 2025 meta-analysis found no significant association, another found a small significant one (benzodiazepine RR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04-1.18) with very high heterogeneity. Observational data also associate benzodiazepines with higher suicide mortality in several psychiatric populations (not studied specifically in anxiety disorders, and not causal). Best evidence supports short-term use, or brief bridging while an SSRI/SNRI takes effect, rather than ongoing treatment of chronic anxiety.

Who should be careful: Avoid in people with a history of substance use disorder, in untreated sleep apnoea or severe respiratory disease, and generally in older adults (falls, fractures and confusion — PubMed-retrieved observational evidence links benzodiazepines to hip fracture, OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05-1.66) and in pregnancy. Not appropriate as routine long-term monotherapy for chronic anxiety.

Interactions: Dangerous additive sedation and respiratory depression with opioids (subject of an FDA boxed warning) and with alcohol or other CNS depressants. Effects can be prolonged by drugs that inhibit their liver metabolism. Impairs driving and operation of machinery, especially early in treatment and at higher doses.

What the evidence says

According to PubMed-retrieved evidence, a 2025 network meta-analysis of 56 RCTs (7,556 participants) with GAD found all benzodiazepines significantly better than placebo with no significant differences between individual agents, and tolerability no different from placebo except for diazepam (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.32-1.96) — though certainty ranged from high to very low across comparisons and high heterogeneity and inconsistency were detected in the efficacy network. The Cochrane review of benzodiazepines versus placebo in panic disorder (24 studies, 4,233 participants) found a response advantage (RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.39-1.96; NNTB 4, 95% CI 3-7) but rated the evidence LOW-quality, judged overall methodological quality poor with 20 of 24 studies at high risk of bias in at least one domain, flagged probable publication bias, and noted the trials were short-term only and never examined long-term efficacy or the risks of dependency and withdrawal; more participants dropped out for adverse effects on benzodiazepines (RR 1.58, 1.16-2.15) and more reported at least one adverse effect (RR 1.18, 1.02-1.37). Across classes, a 91-article network meta-analysis put the benzodiazepine effect at SMD -0.40 (95% CrI -0.65 to -0.15) — significant but the smallest of the effective drug classes, against -0.67 for SSRIs — and the 89-trial Lancet network meta-analysis likewise rated benzodiazepines effective but poorly tolerated versus placebo. On harms of sustained use, a meta-analysis of 19 neuropsychological studies found significant deficits in working memory, processing speed, divided attention, visuoconstruction, recent memory and expressive language in long-term users, with deficits persisting after successful withdrawal and abstinence. The dementia question is genuinely unresolved: a 2025 meta-analysis in older adults found a non-significant hazard ratio of 1.17 (95% CI 0.96-1.43) for all-cause dementia and 1.00 (0.87-1.15) for Alzheimer's disease, while a 2025 meta-analysis of GABA-A-acting drugs (19 studies, 2,953,980 patients) found small but significant associations with dementia (RR 1.15, 1.02-1.29) and Alzheimer's disease (RR 1.21, 1.04-1.40), benzodiazepine subgroup RR 1.11 (1.04-1.18), both with very high heterogeneity. Other observational harm signals include hip fracture (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05-1.66 in case-control studies, from 20 studies covering 6,084,083 participants) and higher suicide mortality in most psychiatric diagnostic categories except depression.

Doses studied: Agent-specific anxiolytic doses in short-term randomised trials; most efficacy trials ran a few weeks and, per the Cochrane review, did not examine long-term efficacy or the risks of dependency and withdrawal at all. No adequate RCT evidence supports continuous long-term use. Clinical guidance favours the lowest effective dose for the shortest practical duration, typically as an add-on during the first weeks of antidepressant treatment or as a short-term stand-alone bridge.

Why this grade: Short-term superiority over placebo is replicated: a 2025 network meta-analysis of 56 RCTs (7,556 participants) in GAD found all benzodiazepines significantly better than placebo, and two earlier network meta-analyses agree. The evidence stops short of 'strong' on four counts retrieved from PubMed. (1) Quality: the Cochrane review of benzodiazepines versus placebo in panic disorder (24 studies, 4,233 participants) rated its own findings LOW-quality, judged overall methodological quality poor with 20 of 24 studies at high risk of bias in at least one domain, and flagged probable publication bias. (2) Size: the class effect was the smallest of the effective medication classes in one NMA (SMD -0.40 versus -0.67 for SSRIs). (3) Tolerability: the 89-trial Lancet NMA rated benzodiazepines poorly tolerated versus placebo, and Cochrane found more dropouts due to adverse effects (RR 1.58) and more participants with at least one adverse effect (RR 1.18). (4) Duration and harms: no adequate trial evidence establishes long-term efficacy, trials never examined dependency or withdrawal, long-term use is linked to cognitive deficits that persist after withdrawal, and guidelines position benzodiazepines as a short-term add-on or bridge rather than routine long-term therapy. Note also that the 2025 NMA's certainty ratings ranged from high to very low (40 comparisons very low, 7 low) and one of its co-authors is employed by a pharmaceutical company (BIAL). Torn between strong and moderate, the lower grade applies.

Sources (9)
  1. Comparative Efficacy and Safety of Benzodiazepines in the Treatment of Patients with Generalized Anxiety Disorder: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis.
    Fernandes et al., 2025 (Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics) · PMID 40544830 · positive

    Across 56 RCTs (7,556 participants) with GAD, all benzodiazepines were significantly better than placebo with no significant differences between individual agents; tolerability did not differ from placebo except diazepam (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.32-1.96). CINeMA certainty varied from high to very low (40 comparisons very low, 7 low), and high heterogeneity and inconsistency were detected in the efficacy analysis. The review notes guidelines recommend benzodiazepines as an add-on to antidepressants during the first weeks of GAD treatment or as short-term stand-alone therapy. One co-author is employed by a pharmaceutical company (BIAL - Portela & Ca, S.A.).

  2. Benzodiazepines versus placebo for panic disorder in adults.
    Breilmann et al., 2019 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews) · PMID 30921478 · mixed

    Across 24 studies (4,233 participants), benzodiazepines showed a possible response advantage over placebo (RR 1.65, 95% CI 1.39-1.96; NNTB 4, 95% CI 3-7) and remission advantage (RR 1.61, 1.38-1.88), but the authors rated this LOW-quality evidence, judged overall methodological quality poor with 20 of 24 studies at high risk of bias in at least one domain, and flagged probable publication bias and possible unmasking. More participants dropped out due to adverse effects on benzodiazepines (RR 1.58, 1.16-2.15) and more had at least one adverse effect (RR 1.18, 1.02-1.37). The included trials were short-term only and did not examine long-term efficacy or the risks of dependency and withdrawal; the authors conclude the results 'provide only limited guidance for clinical practice.'

  3. Pharmacological and psychological interventions for generalized anxiety disorder in adults: A network meta-analysis.
    Chen et al., 2019 (Journal of Psychiatric Research) · PMID 31494377 · mixed

    In 91 articles (14,812 participants), benzodiazepines beat placebo for GAD but with the smallest effect among the effective medication classes (SMD -0.40, 95% CrI -0.65 to -0.15, against -0.67 for SSRIs and -0.54 for SNRIs).

  4. Pharmacological treatments for generalised anxiety disorder: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Slee et al., 2019 (Lancet) · PMID 30712879 · mixed

    In this 89-trial, 25,441-patient network meta-analysis of GAD pharmacotherapy, benzodiazepines were effective on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale but poorly tolerated compared with placebo.

  5. The Residual Medium and Long-term Cognitive Effects of Benzodiazepine Use: An Updated Meta-analysis.
    Crowe & Stranks, 2018 (Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology) · PMID 29244060 · negative

    Meta-analysis of 19 studies found statistically significant negative effects of long-term benzodiazepine use in current users across working memory, processing speed, divided attention, visuoconstruction, recent memory and expressive language. In those who had withdrawn and successfully abstained, deficits persisted across recent memory, processing speed, visuoconstruction, divided attention, working memory and sustained attention.

  6. Chronic Use of Benzodiazepine in Older Adults and Its Relationship with Dementia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Rivas et al., 2025 (Harvard Review of Psychiatry) · PMID 39761441 · null

    Pooling five studies of chronic benzodiazepine use in older adults, the association with all-cause dementia was non-significant (HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.96-1.43), and across three studies there was no association with Alzheimer's disease (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.87-1.15). The authors conclude their findings suggest no strong link and that further research is needed.

  7. Use of Drugs Affecting GABA Receptors and the Risk of Developing Alzheimer's Disease and Dementia: a Meta-Analysis and Literature Review.
    Vakili et al., 2025 (Molecular Neurobiology) · PMID 40108057 · negative

    Across 19 observational studies (10 case-control, 9 cohort) covering 2,953,980 patients, use of GABA-A-acting drugs was significantly associated with dementia (RR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.29, I-squared 87.6%) and Alzheimer's disease (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.04-1.40, I-squared 97.6%). In the drug subgroups, benzodiazepines carried RR 1.11 (95% CI 1.04-1.18, I-squared 87.2%) and zolpidem RR 1.28 (1.08-1.52). Heterogeneity was very high and follow-up duration (5-11 years) was significantly associated with it. This directly contradicts the null result above, so the dementia question should be treated as open rather than settled.

  8. Sedative-hypnotics and osteoporotic fractures: A systematic review of observational studies with over six million individuals.
    Xu et al., 2023 (Sleep Medicine Reviews) · PMID 37926010 · negative

    Across 20 observational studies (6,084,083 participants), benzodiazepines were associated with hip fracture in case-control studies (OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05-1.66). Associations with any fracture were not statistically significant (case-control OR 1.33, 95% CI 0.98-1.80; case-crossover OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.95-1.41), while Z-drugs were associated with any fracture in case-crossover studies (OR 1.41, 1.08-1.85). The authors describe the overall association as slight and call for more research.

  9. Effect of psychotropic medications on suicide-related outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.
    Kozhevnikova et al., 2026 (EClinicalMedicine) · PMID 41768277 · negative

    Across 48 observational studies from 13 countries covering more than 6 million people with schizophrenia-spectrum, bipolar, depressive or personality disorders, benzodiazepines were associated with higher risk of suicide mortality in most diagnostic categories, except depression (no pooled benzodiazepine effect estimate is given in the abstract). The authors state the findings are not causal and are limited by residual confounding. Note this evidence comes from those diagnostic populations, not from anxiety-disorder cohorts, so it is a signal to disclose rather than an established risk in anxiety treatment.
