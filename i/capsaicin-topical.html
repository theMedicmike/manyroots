Topical capsaicin

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Capsaicin is the compound that makes chili peppers hot, and rubbed on the skin as a cream or patch it can take the edge off arthritis pain and long-term low back pain for some people. The benefit is real but small — in the best summary of the muscle-and-joint studies, about eight people had to use it for one extra person to get meaningful relief. It also comes with a catch: it stings and burns where you put it, often for the first several days, and somewhere between a third and two thirds of people in trials get that reaction. Because the burning makes it obvious who got the real cream, researchers think some of the measured benefit is expectation rather than the cream itself.

Safety

Burning, stinging and redness at the application site are the expected effect, not a rare side effect: the osteoarthritis meta-analysis found a number needed to harm of 3 for burning, Cochrane recorded adverse events in 80% of Capsicum extract gel users versus 20% on placebo, and Cochrane's overview of topical analgesics put local adverse events at 63% versus placebo with 15% of users withdrawing because of them. Burning is usually worst in the first days and often lessens with continued daily use, but it is the main reason people stop. Wash hands thoroughly after applying (or use gloves), and never touch eyes, nose, mouth, genitals, or contact lenses afterwards. Do not apply to broken, irritated or infected skin, do not bandage tightly or cover with an occlusive dressing unless told to, and avoid heating pads, hot baths, saunas or vigorous exercise right after applying — heat markedly intensifies the burn. Blistering, swelling, or burning that is severe or worsening rather than settling is not the expected reaction: stop and seek medical care. High-concentration 8% capsaicin patches are a prescription clinical procedure applied under supervision, usually with local anaesthetic, and are not a self-care product.

Who should be careful: Known allergy to capsaicin or chili peppers. Do not use on damaged, broken, or inflamed skin, near the eyes or mucous membranes, or on the face. Use caution in people with reduced skin sensation — including diabetic peripheral neuropathy — who may not feel a developing burn. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding; keep away from children and never apply to a child without clinical advice.

Interactions: Few systemic drug interactions, since absorption from low-concentration topical products is minimal. Avoid applying other topical products (NSAID gels, menthol or salicylate rubs, occlusive moisturisers) to the same area at the same time, as this can increase irritation and absorption. Apply in a well-ventilated space and avoid inhaling vapour or powder, which can trigger coughing and airway irritation — relevant for anyone with asthma. If you use anticoagulants and also apply topical NSAIDs, do not stack products on the same skin area without asking a pharmacist.

What the evidence says

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 double-blind RCTs (498 patients, 5 at low and 3 at high overall risk of bias) of topical capsaicin at 0.0125%-5% in osteoarthritis found a significant reduction in visual analogue pain severity versus placebo (SMD -0.84, 95% CI -1.48 to -0.19, P=0.01) at follow-up to 3 months, alongside a marked increase in application-site burning (RR 5.56, 95% CI 1.75 to 17.69, P=0.004; number needed to harm 3), with overall low-to-very-low certainty. The longer-standing BMJ review of topical capsaicin in chronic pain pooled 3 double-blind placebo-controlled musculoskeletal trials (368 patients) and found a relative benefit of 1.5 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.0) with a number needed to treat of 8.1 (95% CI 4.6 to 34), concluding efficacy was moderate to poor and that around one third of patients experienced local adverse events attributable to capsaicin. For chronic low back pain the evidence is separate and stronger: Cochrane found Capsicum frutescens cream or plaster probably outperforms placebo across 3 trials and 755 participants (moderate quality), anchored by a 320-patient double-blind trial in which the compound pain subscore fell 42% with capsicum plaster versus 31% with placebo and responder rates were 67% versus 49% (P=0.002), with local adverse drug reactions in 7.5% versus 3.1% and no systemic side effects. Two countervailing results matter as much as the positive ones: Cochrane's review of topical herbal therapies found moderate-quality evidence from 99 people with knee osteoarthritis that Capsicum extract gel probably does not improve pain (MD -1, 95% CI -6.8 to 4.8, against a placebo mean of 46/100) or knee function (MD -2.6, 95% CI -9.5 to 4.2) while producing adverse events in 80% versus 20% on placebo (rate ratio 4.12, number needed to harm 2); and Cochrane's 2017 overview of 13 reviews (206 studies, about 30,700 participants) rated the efficacy evidence for low-concentration capsaicin as very low quality and typically limited to single studies or sparse data, while rating as high quality the finding that local adverse events reached 63% versus topical placebo in chronic pain (NNH 2.6). A series of n-of-1 trials in 22 people with painful knee osteoarthritis found capsaicin 0.025% cream and 5% ibuprofen gel statistically indistinguishable overall (mean pain reduction 1.6, 95% CI 0.9 to 2.4, versus 1.2, 95% CI 0.5 to 1.8; P=0.221), though 41% of individuals responded better to capsaicin and 18% better to ibuprofen. The 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline gives topical capsaicin a conditional recommendation for knee osteoarthritis only, and the high-concentration 8% patch literature sits mainly in postherpetic neuralgia rather than osteoarthritis or back pain, so it should not be read across.

Doses studied: Trials in the osteoarthritis meta-analysis used topical capsaicin at concentrations from 0.0125% to 5%, with follow-up to 3 months. The preparation closest to a self-care product is 0.025% cream applied to the affected joint for 4 weeks, the regimen used in the n-of-1 knee osteoarthritis trials. Chronic low back pain trials used a Capsicum frutescens plaster applied over the painful area, with the 320-patient trial assessing outcomes after 3 weeks of treatment. High-concentration 8% capsaicin patches are a clinician-applied prescription procedure studied mainly in postherpetic neuralgia, not a self-care product, and their results do not transfer to osteoarthritis or back pain.

Why this grade: Multiple randomised placebo-controlled trials and several independent syntheses point the same direction, but the effect is modest and the certainty is repeatedly downgraded. A 2024 meta-analysis of 8 double-blind RCTs in osteoarthritis found a statistically significant pain reduction (SMD -0.84) but rated the evidence low-to-very-low certainty for short follow-up, small samples and high heterogeneity; the 2004 BMJ review of the musculoskeletal literature found only a relative benefit of 1.5 and NNT 8.1 with an upper confidence limit of 34, which the authors called moderate to poor efficacy; and Cochrane's 2017 overview of topical analgesics rated the efficacy evidence for low-concentration capsaicin — the over-the-counter product most people actually use — as very low quality and limited to single studies or sparse data. Cochrane rated capsicum cream or plaster for chronic low back pain moderate quality across 3 trials and 755 participants, the strongest single result in the file. Against that, Cochrane found moderate-quality evidence that Capsicum extract gel probably does not improve pain or function in knee osteoarthritis while causing adverse events in 80% of users, and blinding is structurally compromised in every trial because the burning sensation tells participants which arm they are in (burning RR 5.56, number needed to harm 3), which inflates apparent effects. Guideline support exists but is conditional and site-specific — the 2019 ACR guideline conditionally recommends topical capsaicin for knee osteoarthritis only — so 'strong' is not warranted, while 'early' would misdescribe a literature that contains multiple meta-analyses and several hundred randomised patients.

Sources (8)
  1. Efficacy and safety of topical capsaicin in the treatment of osteoarthritis pain: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisTshering G, Posadzki P, Kongkaew C, 2024 · PMID 38761115 · positive

    Across 8 double-blind RCTs in 498 osteoarthritis patients (5 at low overall risk of bias, 3 at high), topical capsaicin 0.0125%-5% reduced pain severity versus placebo (SMD -0.84, 95% CI -1.48 to -0.19, P=0.01) at follow-up up to 3 months, but increased application-site burning (RR 5.56, 95% CI 1.75 to 17.69, P=0.004; number needed to harm 3, from 5 studies). The authors rated certainty low-to-very-low because of short duration, small sample sizes and high heterogeneity, and framed capsaicin as an option for short-term management in patients intolerant of NSAIDs.

  2. Systematic review of topical capsaicin for the treatment of chronic pain.
    Meta-analysisMason L, Moore RA, Derry S, Edwards JE, McQuay HJ, 2004 · PMID 15033881 · mixed

    Pooling 3 double-blind placebo-controlled trials in musculoskeletal conditions (368 patients), topical capsaicin 0.025% or plaster gave a relative benefit over placebo of only 1.5 (95% CI 1.1 to 2.0) and a number needed to treat of 8.1 (95% CI 4.6 to 34) for about 50% pain reduction at four weeks; around one third of patients experienced local adverse events attributable to capsaicin. The authors concluded topical capsaicin has moderate to poor efficacy in chronic musculoskeletal or neuropathic pain and may be useful mainly as an adjunct or sole therapy for a small number of patients unresponsive to or intolerant of other treatments.

  3. Topical treatment of chronic low back pain with a capsicum plaster.
    Randomised trialFrerick H, Keitel W, Kuhn U, Schmidt S, Bredehorst A, Kuhlmann M, 2003 · PMID 14581111 · positive

    In 320 patients with non-specific low back pain randomised to capsicum plaster or placebo plaster for 3 weeks, the compound pain subscore of the Arhus low back rating scale fell 42% with capsicum versus 31% with placebo, and responder rates (a reduction of at least 30% from baseline) were 67% versus 49% (P=0.002). Local adverse drug reactions occurred in 12 patients (7.5%) on capsicum versus 5 (3.1%) on placebo; no systemic side effects were observed.

  4. Herbal medicine for low-back pain.
    Meta-analysisOltean H, Robbins C, van Tulder MW, Berman BM, Bombardier C, Gagnier JJ (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2014 · PMID 25536022 · positive

    Capsicum frutescens cream or plaster probably produces more favourable results than placebo in people with chronic low back pain (3 trials, 755 participants, moderate quality evidence). For acute low back pain the picture is unclear (1 trial, 40 participants, low quality evidence), one trial found relative equivalence to a homeopathic ointment (161 participants, very low quality), and the completeness of reporting across the herbal low back pain literature was rated poor.

  5. Topical herbal therapies for treating osteoarthritis.
    Systematic reviewCameron M, Chrubasik S (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2013 · PMID 23728701 · negative

    Moderate-quality evidence from a single trial of 99 people with knee osteoarthritis indicated Capsicum extract gel probably does not improve pain (MD -1, 95% CI -6.8 to 4.8, versus a placebo mean of 46 on a 100-point scale at 4 weeks) or knee function (MD -2.6, 95% CI -9.5 to 4.2, versus a placebo mean of 34.8 on a 96-point scale). Adverse events occurred in 80% of the Capsicum group versus 20% on placebo (rate ratio 4.12, 95% CI 3.30 to 5.17; number needed to treat to result in adverse events 2).

  6. Topical analgesics for acute and chronic pain in adults - an overview of Cochrane Reviews.
    Systematic reviewDerry S, Wiffen PJ, Kalso EA, Bell RF, Aldington D, Phillips T, Gaskell H, Moore RA (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2017 · PMID 28497473 · negative

    Across 13 Cochrane reviews (206 studies, about 30,700 participants), evidence for low-concentration capsaicin was rated very low quality and typically limited to single studies or comparisons with sparse data, in contrast to the moderate-to-high quality evidence supporting topical diclofenac and ketoprofen in chronic musculoskeletal pain. Harms were rated more confidently: local adverse events with low-concentration topical capsaicin in chronic pain reached 63% versus topical placebo (5 studies, 557 participants, number needed to harm 2.6, high quality evidence), and adverse-event withdrawals were 15% versus 3% on placebo (4 studies, 477 participants, NNH 8).

  7. Individual responses to topical ibuprofen gel or capsaicin cream for painful knee osteoarthritis: a series of n-of-1 trials.
    Randomised trialPersson MSM, Stocks J, Sarmanova A, Fernandes G, Walsh DA, Doherty M, Zhang W, 2021 · PMID 33197270 · mixed

    Across 104 aggregated treatment periods in 22 people with painful radiographic knee osteoarthritis, mean pain reduction was 1.6 (95% CI 0.9 to 2.4) on 0.025% capsaicin cream versus 1.2 (95% CI 0.5 to 1.8) on 5% ibuprofen gel, a non-significant difference (P=0.221). Individually, 41% responded better to capsaicin, 18% better to ibuprofen, 18% similarly, and 23% were undetermined, suggesting response is highly individual rather than uniform.

  8. 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Osteoarthritis of the Hand, Hip, and Knee.
    GuidelineKolasinski SL, Neogi T, Hochberg MC, et al., 2020 · PMID 31908163 · mixed

    Topical capsaicin received a conditional, not strong, recommendation and only for knee osteoarthritis; it does not appear among the guideline's recommendations for hand or hip osteoarthritis. Strong recommendations in the same guideline went to exercise, weight loss in overweight or obese patients, self-efficacy and self-management programmes, tai chi, cane use, hand orthoses for first CMC OA, tibiofemoral bracing, topical NSAIDs for knee OA, oral NSAIDs, and intra-articular glucocorticoid injections for knee OA — placing capsaicin as an adjunct rather than first-line therapy.
