Home blood pressure monitoring and self-management

Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Checking your own blood pressure at home helps — but only if the numbers actually lead to something. On its own, monitoring changes nothing measurable; paired with a plan to adjust medication, it lowers the top number by roughly 4 to 9 points. Two other honest findings: the benefit fades once the supporting programme ends, so this works best as an ongoing arrangement with your clinician, and in one trial the extra responsibility of self-care nudged people's depression scores in the wrong direction — worth knowing if measuring makes you anxious.

Safety

Taking your own blood pressure is physically harmless, and the titration trials found adverse events similar to usual care. The documented harms are informational and psychological. Wrist and finger devices and wrong-size cuffs give unreliable numbers, so use a validated upper-arm monitor with a correctly sized cuff. A single high reading means very little. More than that, one randomised trial found that adding automated self-care support to home telemonitoring significantly worsened depression scores on the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale over a year (4.1 to 5.2, P=0.014) and concluded that promoting patient self-care may have negative psychological effects — so take readings on a defined schedule rather than reactively, and treat rising distress as a reason to scale back. Note also that self-monitoring on its own produced no measurable blood pressure benefit in the pooled data: the monitoring only matters if it feeds into a plan.

Who should be careful: Self-monitoring is a poor guide in atrial fibrillation and other irregular rhythms, where oscillometric home devices are unreliable, and in pregnancy, where readings should be interpreted alongside clinical assessment for pre-eclampsia. People whose anxiety or low mood is worsened by checking should agree a limited measurement schedule with their clinician, or stop, rather than monitoring continuously.

Interactions: No drug interactions, but a direct medication-safety issue: in the trials where patients adjusted their own medication, this was done under a pre-agreed algorithm with clinician oversight. Adjusting, skipping or stopping antihypertensives on the basis of home readings without such a plan risks both under-treatment and over-treatment, with hypotension, dizziness and falls as the consequences of the latter. Bring your readings to your clinician; do not act on them alone.

What the evidence says

The definitive analysis is an individual-patient-data meta-analysis in which 25 randomised trials contributed data and the 12-month primary outcome came from 15 of 19 eligible studies (7,138 of 8,292 randomised participants): self-monitoring lowered clinic systolic BP by 3.2 mmHg overall (95% CI -4.9 to -1.6), but the effect depended entirely on what accompanied it — self-monitoring alone gave -1.0 mmHg (95% CI -3.3 to +1.2, non-significant), while monitoring combined with intensive support gave -6.1 mmHg (-9.0 to -3.2). In the four trials measuring ambulatory blood pressure with little or no co-intervention (1,478 patients) there was no benefit at all (clinic -0.2 mmHg; ambulatory +1.1 mmHg, 95% CI -0.3 to +2.5). When monitoring drives medication titration the effect is substantial: TASMINH4 (1,182 patients across 142 UK practices) found -3.5 mmHg with self-monitoring and -4.7 mmHg with telemonitoring versus usual care at 12 months with adverse events similar across groups, and TASMIN-SR (552 patients at high cardiovascular risk using a pre-agreed self-titration algorithm) found a 9.2 mmHg systolic and 3.4 mmHg diastolic advantage at 12 months. Technology on its own does not substitute for that: a 12-week randomised trial of home telemonitoring in 108 primary-care patients with diabetes and hypertension found no significant difference from usual care in systolic BP. Durability is the other weakness: a home telemonitoring plus pharmacist-management trial that produced -10.7, -9.7 and -6.6 mmHg differences at 6, 12 and 18 months had attenuated to a non-significant -2.5 mmHg (95% CI -6.3 to +1.2, P=0.18) at 54 months, 42 months after the intervention ended. A second individual-patient-data analysis (16 trials, 6,522 participants) confirmed a -3.12 mmHg clinic systolic effect regardless of how many hypertension-related comorbidities patients had. No trial in this literature was powered for heart attacks, strokes or deaths.

Doses studied: Trials paired home readings with clinician, pharmacist or patient-led medication titration against defined targets — TASMIN-SR used an office target of 130/80 mmHg and a home target of 120/75 mmHg with an individualised self-titration algorithm. Follow-up was 12 months in TASMINH4, TASMIN-SR and both individual-patient-data meta-analyses; one telemonitoring-plus-pharmacist programme ran 12 months and followed patients to 54 months; the null telemonitoring trial ran 12 weeks.

Why this grade: Large randomised trials and two individual-patient-data meta-analyses show a real but conditional effect: self-monitoring on its own produced no significant blood pressure reduction (-1.0 mmHg, non-significant) and no ambulatory BP benefit, while monitoring linked to medication titration or intensive support produced 4 to 9 mmHg reductions. A separate randomised trial reached the same conclusion independently and found that promoting self-care worsened depression scores. The benefit also does not persist once the supporting intervention stops, an independent 12-week telemonitoring trial was null, and no trial was powered for cardiovascular events. Positive but modest and dependent on co-intervention; graded moderate rather than strong.

Sources (7)
  1. Self-monitoring of blood pressure in hypertension: A systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis
    Tucker KL et al., 2017 · PMID 28926573 · mixed

    Individual patient data provided by 25 randomised trials; 12-month primary outcome data from 15 of 19 eligible studies, covering 7,138 of 8,292 (86%) randomised participants. Self-monitoring reduced clinic systolic BP 3.2 mmHg (95% CI -4.9 to -1.6), but effect varied by co-intervention intensity: self-monitoring alone gave -1.0 mmHg (95% CI -3.3 to +1.2, non-significant), monitoring with intensive support gave -6.1 mmHg (-9.0 to -3.2). In four trials measuring ambulatory BP with little or no co-intervention (1,478 patients), there was no association with lower clinic (-0.2 mmHg, 95% CI -2.2 to 1.8) or ambulatory (+1.1 mmHg, 95% CI -0.3 to 2.5) systolic BP.

  2. Efficacy of self-monitored blood pressure, with or without telemonitoring, for titration of antihypertensive medication (TASMINH4): an unmasked randomised controlled trial
    McManus RJ et al., 2018 · PMID 29499873 · positive

    1,182 hypertensive patients across 142 UK general practices randomised to self-monitoring (n=395), self-monitoring with telemonitoring (n=393), or usual care (n=394); 1,003 (85%) in the primary analysis. At 12 months, adjusted mean systolic BP differences versus usual care were -3.5 mmHg (95% CI -5.8 to -1.2) for self-monitoring and -4.7 mmHg (-7.0 to -2.4) for telemonitoring, with no difference between the two intervention arms (-1.2 mmHg, 95% CI -3.5 to +1.2). Adverse events were similar across all three groups.

  3. Effect of self-monitoring and medication self-titration on systolic blood pressure in hypertensive patients at high risk of cardiovascular disease: the TASMIN-SR randomized clinical trial
    McManus RJ et al., 2014 · PMID 25157723 · positive

    552 patients aged 35 or over with stroke, coronary heart disease, diabetes or chronic kidney disease and baseline BP of at least 130/80 mmHg, across 59 UK practices; primary outcome data from 450 (81%). At 12 months mean BP was 128.2/73.8 mmHg with self-monitoring plus an individualised self-titration algorithm versus 137.8/76.3 mmHg with usual care, a difference of 9.2 mmHg systolic (95% CI 5.7-12.7) and 3.4 mmHg diastolic (1.8-5.0) after baseline correction, comparable across subgroups and without excessive adverse events.

  4. Effect of home telemonitoring on glycemic and blood pressure control in primary care clinic patients with diabetes
    Wakefield BJ et al., 2014 · PMID 24404819 · null

    Single-centre randomised controlled trial comparing 12 weeks of in-home telemonitoring (n=55) with usual care (n=53) in primary-care patients with type 2 diabetes and hypertension and out-of-range HbA1c and/or blood pressure. There were no significant differences between intervention and control groups on either HbA1c or systolic blood pressure. Authors concluded that the addition of technology alone is unlikely to lead to improvements in outcomes.

  5. Long-term Outcomes of the Effects of Home Blood Pressure Telemonitoring and Pharmacist Management on Blood Pressure Among Adults With Uncontrolled Hypertension: Follow-up of a Cluster Randomized Clinical Trial
    Margolis KL et al., 2018 · PMID 30646139 · mixed

    450 patients in 16 primary care clinics; the intervention ran 12 months. Systolic BP differences favouring intervention were -10.7, -9.7 and -6.6 mmHg at 6, 12 and 18 months, but by 54 months the differential reduction had attenuated to a non-significant -2.5 mmHg (95% CI -6.3 to +1.2, P=.18), with diastolic -1.0 mmHg (-3.2 to +1.2, P=.37). Authors concluded long-term control is likely to require continued monitoring and resumption of the intervention.

  6. Effect of home blood pressure telemonitoring with self-care support on uncontrolled systolic hypertension in diabetics
    Logan AG et al., 2012 · PMID 22615116 · mixed

    110 diabetic patients with uncontrolled systolic hypertension randomised to telemonitoring with automated smartphone self-care messages or control; 105 (95.5%) completed the 1-year outcome visit. Daytime ambulatory systolic BP fell 9.1+/-15.6 mmHg in the intervention group with a between-group difference of 7.1+/-2.3 mmHg (P<0.005), achieved without more or different medications. The authors state that home blood pressure monitoring alone had no effect on blood pressure, and that providing self-care support worsened depression on the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (baseline 4.1+/-3.76, exit 5.2+/-4.30, P=0.014), concluding that promoting patient self-care may have negative psychological effects.

  7. Self-monitoring of Blood Pressure in Patients With Hypertension-Related Multi-morbidity: Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Meta-analysis
    Sheppard JP et al., 2020 · PMID 31730171 · mixed

    22 eligible trials, 16 providing individual patient data, 6,522 participants with follow-up. Self-monitoring reduced clinic systolic BP 3.12 mmHg at 12 months (95% CI -4.78 to -1.46) regardless of the number of hypertension-related comorbidities (interaction P=0.260). High-intensity co-interventions outperformed low-intensity ones in patients with obesity (P<0.001) and possibly stroke, but not in coronary heart disease, diabetes or chronic kidney disease.
