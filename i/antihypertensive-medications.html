Blood-pressure lowering: whether to treat, and how low to aim

Strong evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

ImportantNever start, change or stop a blood-pressure medicine because of a website. These are prescription drugs and the decision to treat, and the target to aim for, belong with a prescriber who knows your overall risk.

Lowering high blood pressure is one of the best-tested things in medicine, and the trials counted strokes, heart attacks and deaths rather than the number on the cuff: across hundreds of thousands of people, every 5-point drop in the top number went with roughly 10 percent fewer major events. Two honest limits come with that. The benefit is clearest when your pressure and your overall risk are high — with only mildly raised pressure and lower risk, treatment has not been shown to cut deaths or heart attacks; it may cut strokes, while more people may stop treatment because of side effects. Whether to treat, and how low to aim, is a conversation about your whole risk, not a number to chase.

Safety

These are prescription drugs and no one should start, change or stop them from a website. Harms are quantified in the same trials that show benefit: in SPRINT, intensive treatment raised serious adverse events of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormality and acute kidney injury (PMID 26551272); in ACCORD-BP, serious adverse events attributed to treatment rose from 1.3% to 3.3% (PMID 20228401); and in mild hypertension without cardiovascular disease, withdrawals for side effects may rise (RR 4.80, from one trial, low-certainty) (PMID 41925078). Falls are the harm most commonly assumed, and the randomised evidence does not support it: SPRINT found no increase in injurious falls, and a systematic review in adults 60 and older found lower targets did not raise falls or cognitive impairment, though hypotension, syncope and pill burden did rise (PMID 28114673). Kidney function and electrolytes are normally rechecked after starting or increasing a blood-pressure drug. Seek urgent care for fainting, confusion, severe weakness, a swollen tongue, lips or throat, or a pulse or pressure that drops very low.

Who should be careful: This entry is about the decision to treat and the target to aim for; the contraindications that matter are class-specific and are graded with each class on this page - ACE inhibitors and ARBs in pregnancy, for instance. On targets rather than drugs: an intensive target has not been shown to benefit people with type 2 diabetes, and in mild hypertension without cardiovascular disease, treatment has not been shown to reduce death or total cardiovascular events, though stroke may fall. Frailty, symptomatic orthostatic hypotension and a history of falls are reasons to be cautious about a lower target even though the trial evidence did not find more injurious falls.

Interactions: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) blunt the blood-pressure effect of most classes and, combined with an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a diuretic, raise the risk of acute kidney injury. Interactions specific to one drug class are listed with that class on this page. Alcohol, alpha-blockers, nitrates, PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) and any additional blood-pressure-lowering supplement add to the hypotensive effect. Licorice root, decongestants such as pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine, oral corticosteroids and stimulant medications can raise blood pressure and work against treatment. Disclose every supplement to the prescriber; several interventions discussed elsewhere on this site are themselves mildly blood-pressure-lowering and can stack with medication.

What the evidence says

SURROGATE vs OUTCOME: the millimetres are the surrogate, the events are what these trials counted. Dose-response, class-collective: across 48 randomised trials of different drug classes and 344,716 participants, each 5 mm Hg systolic reduction cut major cardiovascular events by about 10%, with or without prior cardiovascular disease; the analysis reports nothing by drug class (PMID 33933205). Which class, tested head to head: ALLHAT randomised 33,357 higher-risk adults 55 and older to chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg (n=15,255), amlodipine 2.5-10 mg (n=9,048) or lisinopril 10-40 mg (n=9,054) and found no difference on the primary combined coronary outcome: versus chlorthalidone (6-year rate 11.5%), RR 0.98 (95% CI 0.90-1.07) for amlodipine and 0.99 (0.91-1.08) for lisinopril, with all-cause mortality also no different, while heart failure was higher on amlodipine (RR 1.38) and lisinopril (RR 1.19) and stroke on lisinopril (RR 1.15) - class differences graded in the per-class entries on this page (PMID 12479763). How low - the positive side: SPRINT randomised 9,361 people at high cardiovascular risk without diabetes to a systolic target below 120 versus below 140 mm Hg and was stopped early at a median 3.26 years for benefit on the primary composite and on all-cause death (PMID 26551272). How low - the nulls, and they are substantial: in adults 60 and older a systematic review found no significant effect of targets of 140/85 mm Hg or lower on cardiac events, stroke or death; they did NOT raise falls or cognitive impairment, though hypotension, syncope and pill burden rose (PMID 28114673); ACCORD-BP randomised 4,733 people with type 2 diabetes to the same two targets and found no benefit on the primary composite over a mean 4.7 years, with serious treatment-attributed adverse events rising from 1.3% to 3.3% (PMID 20228401); HOPE-3 treated 12,705 intermediate-risk adults averaging 138.1/81.9 mm Hg with candesartan plus hydrochlorothiazide and found no benefit over a median 5.6 years, with benefit confined to those starting above about 143.5 mm Hg (PMID 27041480). Whether to treat at all: the current Cochrane review of untreated mild hypertension (140-159 and/or 90-99 mm Hg) without cardiovascular disease, on individual data from 5 trials and 9,124 participants, found there may be little to no reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 0.64-1.14), total cardiovascular events (RR 0.93, 0.69-1.24) or coronary heart disease (RR 1.12, 0.80-1.57), a possible fall in stroke (RR 0.41, 0.20-0.84; 4 trials, 7,292) and a possible rise in withdrawals for adverse effects (RR 4.80, from one trial of 17,354 on aggregate data), all low-certainty (PMID 41925078). Read together: the case for lowering pressure is strongest where risk is high and the pressure is well above threshold, and it thins out as both fall - in mild hypertension without cardiovascular disease, to no shown effect on death or total cardiovascular events, with stroke possibly falling.

Doses studied: These trials randomised a TARGET or a decision to treat, not a dose. SPRINT and ACCORD-BP both compared a systolic aim below 120 mm Hg with below 140, using whatever combination reached it — SPRINT averaged about three drugs in the intensive arm. HOPE-3 used fixed candesartan 16 mg plus hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg daily without titration. One measurement caveat matters for reading SPRINT: its pressures came from automated office measurement, which typically reads lower than a routine clinic check, so a SPRINT target of 120 is not the same number as 120 measured at an ordinary appointment.

Why this grade: Scoped to the two questions that are about TREATMENT rather than about a drug class: is it worth lowering blood pressure at all, and how low should the target be. Per-class evidence is graded separately on this page. Strong rests on hard clinical events, not on the cuff reading. An individual participant-level meta-analysis of 48 randomised trials (344,716 participants) found each 5 mm Hg fall in systolic pressure cut major cardiovascular events by about 10%, with or without prior cardiovascular disease; it is a class-collective result, pooled across trials of different drug classes, and reports nothing by class. Which class does the lowering was tested head to head in ALLHAT, a three-arm trial in higher-risk adults: neither an ACE inhibitor nor a calcium channel blocker beat a thiazide on the primary coronary outcome or on death, though heart failure was higher on both than on the thiazide, and stroke on the ACE inhibitor - class differences the per-class entries on this page carry. On how low: SPRINT randomised a target rather than a drug and found a systolic aim below 120 mm Hg beat below 140 on major cardiovascular events and on death; in adults 60 and older a systematic review found treating people starting at 160 mm Hg or more to below 150/90 mm Hg reduced cardiac events, stroke and, on moderate-strength evidence, death, while targets of 140/85 mm Hg or lower showed no significant effect on any of the three (PMID 28114673). The grade is held at strong despite substantial honest nulls, because those nulls define WHERE benefit is small or absent rather than overturning it: ACCORD-BP found no primary-outcome benefit from an intensive target in type 2 diabetes, HOPE-3 found no benefit in 12,705 intermediate-risk adults averaging 138/82 mm Hg, and the current Cochrane review of untreated mild hypertension without cardiovascular disease (5 trials, 9,124 participants) found treatment may not reduce death, total cardiovascular events or coronary disease, while stroke may fall (RR 0.41) and withdrawals for side effects may rise (RR 4.80, from one trial), all on low-certainty evidence. That last one is the most important honest null on this topic and it is not a footnote: in mild hypertension without cardiovascular disease, drug treatment has not been shown to reduce death or total cardiovascular events, and only stroke may fall. Two caveats sit inside the grade: SPRINT was stopped early at a median 3.26 years, which tends to exaggerate an effect, and it measured pressure in a way most clinics do not.

Sources (9)
  1. Pharmacological blood pressure lowering for primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease across different levels of blood pressure: an individual participant-level data meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisBlood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration, Lancet 2021 · PMID 33933205 · positive

    Across 48 randomised trials and 344,716 participants (median 4.15 years' follow-up), a 5 mm Hg systolic reduction lowered major cardiovascular events by about 10%: HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.89-0.94) in people without prior cardiovascular disease and HR 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it, with no reliable heterogeneity across baseline systolic categories from <120 to >=170 mm Hg. Primary-prevention event rates fell from 31.9 to 25.9 per 1000 person-years; baseline pressures were 157/89 mm Hg without and 146/84 mm Hg with prior cardiovascular disease. The analysis tests heterogeneity only by prior cardiovascular disease and baseline systolic category and reports nothing by drug class, so this is a class-collective result about the millimetres removed. The Lancet's published correction (PMID 34022983) updates only the collaboration's group list; no estimate changed.

  2. First-line drugs for hypertension.
    Meta-analysisWright JM, Musini VM, Gill R, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2018 · PMID 29667175 · positive

    Twenty-four trials (28 active treatment arms, 58,040 participants, mean age 56, follow-up three to five years) compared a first-line drug with placebo or no treatment across the four drug classes for which trials were found - thiazides, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers and ACE inhibitors; none were found for ARBs or alpha-blockers, and the abstract gives no trial count or total for any single class. Results are mostly applicable to moderate to severe primary hypertension. The collective point: first-line treatment reduced clinical events, not only pressure - low-dose thiazides reduced all four outcomes on high-quality evidence, and the authors judge ACE inhibitors and calcium channel blockers possibly similarly effective on lower-quality evidence.

  3. A Randomized Trial of Intensive versus Standard Blood-Pressure Control.
    Randomised trialSPRINT Research Group (Wright JT, Williamson JD, et al.), N Engl J Med 2015 · PMID 26551272 · positive

    In 9,361 higher-risk adults without diabetes, targeting systolic <120 mm Hg versus <140 mm Hg achieved 121.4 vs 136.2 mm Hg and reduced the primary composite of myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, heart failure or cardiovascular death from 2.19% to 1.65% per year (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64-0.89) and all-cause mortality (HR 0.73, 0.60-0.90). The trial was STOPPED EARLY after a median follow-up of only 3.26 years, which tends to overstate effect size. Serious adverse events of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney injury were higher with intensive treatment; injurious falls were not.

  4. Blood-Pressure Lowering in Intermediate-Risk Persons without Cardiovascular Disease.
    Randomised trialLonn EM, et al. (HOPE-3 Investigators), N Engl J Med 2016 · PMID 27041480 · null

    In 12,705 intermediate-risk adults without cardiovascular disease (mean baseline 138.1/81.9 mm Hg), candesartan 16 mg plus hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg daily versus placebo for a median 5.6 years lowered blood pressure by a further 6.0/3.0 mm Hg but did NOT reduce events: the first co-primary outcome occurred in 260 participants (4.1%) versus 279 (4.4%) (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.79-1.10; p=0.40) and the second in 312 (4.9%) versus 328 (5.2%) (HR 0.95, 0.81-1.11; p=0.51). Benefit appeared only in the prespecified subgroup in the upper third of baseline systolic pressure (>143.5 mm Hg); effects were neutral in the middle and lower thirds.

  5. Effects of intensive blood-pressure control in type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Randomised trialACCORD Study Group (Cushman WC, Evans GW, et al.), N Engl J Med 2010 · PMID 20228401 · null

    In 4,733 adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, an intensive target achieving 119.3 vs 133.5 mm Hg did NOT reduce the primary composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke or cardiovascular death (1.87 vs 2.09% per year; HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.73-1.06; p=0.20) and did not reduce death from any cause (1.28 vs 1.19% per year; HR 1.07, 0.85-1.35; p=0.55). Stroke, a prespecified secondary outcome, was lower (0.32 vs 0.53% per year; HR 0.59, 0.39-0.89; p=0.01), while serious adverse events attributed to antihypertensive treatment rose from 1.3% to 3.3% (p<0.001).

  6. Major outcomes in high-risk hypertensive patients randomized to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or calcium channel blocker vs diuretic: The Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT).
    Randomised trialALLHAT Collaborative Research Group, JAMA 2002 · PMID 12479763 · mixed

    33,357 participants aged 55 or older with hypertension and at least one other coronary risk factor, from 623 North American centres, randomly assigned to chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg/d (n=15,255), amlodipine 2.5-10 mg/d (n=9,048) or lisinopril 10-40 mg/d (n=9,054), with planned follow-up of 4 to 8 years. The primary outcome was combined fatal coronary heart disease or non-fatal myocardial infarction, with no difference between treatments: versus chlorthalidone (6-year rate 11.5%), RR 0.98 (95% CI 0.90-1.07) for amlodipine (11.3%) and 0.99 (0.91-1.08) for lisinopril (11.4%). All-cause mortality did not differ between groups. The 33,357 is the three-arm total; the amlodipine and lisinopril contrasts with chlorthalidone are 24,303 and 24,309 people. Heart failure was higher on amlodipine (10.2% versus 7.7%; RR 1.38, 1.25-1.52) and on lisinopril (8.7% versus 7.7%; RR 1.19, 1.07-1.31), and stroke on lisinopril (6.3% versus 5.6%; RR 1.15, 1.02-1.30). Five-year systolic pressure was significantly higher on amlodipine (by 0.8 mm Hg, P=.03) and on lisinopril (by 2 mm Hg, P<.001) than on chlorthalidone. JAMA published two corrections to this article (2003;289:178 and 2004;291:2196); neither has its own PubMed record, and the figures above are as published in the original report.

  7. Beta-blockers for hypertension.
    Meta-analysisWiysonge CS, Bradley HA, Volmink J, Mayosi BM, Opie LH, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2017 · PMID 28107561 · mixed

    Across 13 randomised trials (40,245 participants taking beta-blockers, three-quarters atenolol), beta-blockers as first-line therapy showed no all-cause mortality benefit versus placebo (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.88-1.11) and no reduction in coronary heart disease (RR 0.93, 0.81-1.07), though total cardiovascular disease fell (RR 0.88, 0.79-0.97) via stroke reduction (RR 0.80, 0.66-0.96). Compared with calcium channel blockers, mortality was higher on an interval reaching unity (RR 1.07, 1.00-1.14) and stroke was clearly higher (RR 1.24, 1.11-1.40), as was stroke versus renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RR 1.30, 1.11-1.53). Most included trials had a high risk of bias.

  8. Benefits and Harms of Intensive Blood Pressure Treatment in Adults Aged 60 Years or Older: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisWeiss J, Freeman M, Low A, et al., Ann Intern Med 2017 · PMID 28114673 · mixed

    The review covered 21 randomised trials of blood-pressure targets or treatment intensity plus 3 observational studies of harms. The figures here are as corrected in April 2018 (PMID 29610912), after an error in how the review abstracted data from the HOT (Hypertension Optimal Treatment) trial had made the summary estimates more favourable to intensive treatment. Nine trials in people with baseline systolic pressure of 160 mm Hg or more gave high-strength evidence that treating to below 150/90 mm Hg reduces stroke (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64-0.91) and cardiac events (RR 0.83, 0.71-0.96), and moderate-strength evidence that it reduces mortality (RR 0.93, 0.85-1.00). Six treat-to-target trials of lower targets (140/85 mm Hg or lower) showed no significant effect on mortality (RR 0.93, 0.75-1.14), stroke (RR 0.86, 0.64-1.07) or cardiac events (RR 0.91, 0.77-1.04), on low-strength evidence from heterogeneous trials, though SPRINT suggests some high-risk patients benefit from lower targets. Lower targets did NOT increase falls or cognitive impairment, but were associated with hypotension, syncope and greater medication burden. An earlier correction (Ann Intern Med 2018;168:159, PMID 29335724) carries no text on PubMed; the figures above follow the April 2018 correction.

  9. Pharmacotherapy for mild hypertension.
    Meta-analysisWang D, Wright JM, Adams SP, Cundiff DK, Gueyffier F, Grenet G, Ambasta A, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2026 · PMID 41925078 · null

    Individual patient data from five randomised trials (9,124 participants; 4,593 on antihypertensives, 4,531 on placebo or no treatment) of starting monotherapy or step-up therapy in adults with UNTREATED MILD hypertension - systolic 140-159 or diastolic 90-99 mm Hg, or both - and no pre-existing cardiovascular disease. There may be little to no reduction in all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.64-1.14; 5 trials, 9,124), total cardiovascular events (RR 0.93, 0.69-1.24; 4 trials, 7,292) or coronary heart disease (RR 1.12, 0.80-1.57; 3 trials, 7,080). There may be a decreased risk of stroke (RR 0.41, 0.20-0.84; 4 trials, 7,292) and an increase in withdrawals due to adverse effects (RR 4.80, 4.14-5.57; 1 trial, 17,354 participants, aggregate trial-level data). Every outcome is low-certainty, downgraded for imprecision, indirectness and risk of bias. The abstract does not name the drug classes used, so this is class-collective evidence; it is the current version of the review, which supersedes the 2012 one.
