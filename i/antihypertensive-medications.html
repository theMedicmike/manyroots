Conventional medicine

Antihypertensive medications (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers, thiazide diuretics)

Strong evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Blood pressure medicines are the most heavily tested part of high blood pressure care, and the trials measured what actually matters — strokes, heart attacks and death — not just the number on the cuff. Across hundreds of thousands of people, every 5-point drop in the top number went with roughly 10 percent fewer major heart and stroke events, but the benefit is clearest when your pressure and your overall risk are high: in a 12,705-person trial of people with only slightly raised pressure and middling risk, treatment made no measurable difference over five and a half years. Side effects are real enough that in low-risk trials 9 percent more people stopped treatment than stopped placebo, so whether to start, switch or stop one of these is a conversation with a doctor, never a decision to make from a website.

Safety

These are prescription drugs; class choice, dose and monitoring belong with a prescriber, and no one should start, change or stop them based on a website. Harms are real and quantified in the same trials that show benefit: in SPRINT, intensive treatment raised serious adverse events of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney injury (PMID 26551272); in ACCORD-BP, serious adverse events attributed to antihypertensive treatment rose from 1.3% to 3.3% (PMID 20228401); and in low-risk stage 1 hypertension, 9% more people stopped treatment because of side effects than with placebo (RR 4.80) (PMID 22895954). Falls are the commonly assumed harm, and the randomised evidence does not support it: SPRINT found no increase in injurious falls, and a systematic review of trials in adults 60 and older found lower targets did not increase falls or cognitive impairment, though they did bring hypotension, syncope and a greater pill burden (PMID 28114673). Common class-specific effects include dizziness and orthostatic hypotension, ACE-inhibitor dry cough, ankle swelling and flushing with dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and increased urination, low potassium, low sodium and raised uric acid or gout with thiazides — chlorthalidone caused more hypokalemia than hydrochlorothiazide (6.0% vs 4.4%) in a 13,523-patient randomised comparison (PMID 36516076). Kidney function and electrolytes are normally checked after starting or increasing an ACE inhibitor, ARB or diuretic. Beta-blockers should not be stopped abruptly. Seek urgent care for fainting, confusion, severe weakness, a swollen tongue, lips or throat, or a heart rate or blood pressure that drops very low.

Who should be careful: ACE inhibitors and ARBs must not be used in pregnancy or by anyone trying to conceive (fetal kidney injury and death), and are contraindicated after any episode of angioedema on these drugs and in known bilateral renal artery stenosis; they are also avoided together (dual renin-angiotensin blockade) because of kidney injury and hyperkalaemia. Thiazides are avoided in significant hyponatraemia, used cautiously in gout, and are not effective at low kidney function. Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) are avoided in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and in significant bradycardia or high-grade heart block. Beta-blockers are avoided in severe asthma, marked bradycardia and untreated heart block, and were inferior to other classes as a first choice for uncomplicated hypertension, with more stroke than calcium channel blockers (RR 1.24, 1.11-1.40) and no mortality advantage over placebo (PMID 28107561). Anyone with resistant, secondary or pulmonary hypertension, or who is pregnant, needs an individualised assessment this page does not cover.

Interactions: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) blunt the blood-pressure effect of most classes and, combined with an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a diuretic, raise the risk of acute kidney injury. Potassium supplements, salt substitutes containing potassium chloride, and potassium-sparing diuretics can cause dangerous hyperkalaemia with ACE inhibitors and ARBs. Thiazides and ACE inhibitors raise lithium levels. Grapefruit juice raises levels of some calcium channel blockers. Alcohol, alpha-blockers, nitrates, PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) and any additional blood-pressure-lowering supplement add to the hypotensive effect. Licorice root, decongestants such as pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine, oral corticosteroids and stimulant medications can raise blood pressure and work against treatment. Disclose every supplement to the prescriber; several interventions discussed elsewhere on this site are themselves mildly blood-pressure-lowering and can stack with medication.

What the evidence says

In an individual participant-level meta-analysis of 48 randomised trials (344,716 participants, median 4.15 years' follow-up, mean baseline 157/89 mm Hg without prior cardiovascular disease and 146/84 mm Hg with it), each 5 mm Hg reduction in systolic blood pressure reduced major cardiovascular events by about 10%: HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.89-0.94) without prior cardiovascular disease and HR 0.89 (0.86-0.92) with it, with primary-prevention event rates falling from 31.9 to 25.9 per 1000 person-years (PMID 33933205). A Cochrane review of 24 placebo-controlled trials (58,040 participants, mean age 56, 3-5 years' follow-up) found first-line low-dose thiazides reduced all-cause mortality from 11.0% to 9.8% (RR 0.89, 0.82-0.97), stroke from 6.2% to 4.2% (RR 0.68, 0.60-0.77) and coronary heart disease from 3.9% to 2.8% (RR 0.72, 0.61-0.84), and ACE inhibitors reduced mortality (RR 0.83, 0.72-0.95), while calcium channel blockers reduced stroke (RR 0.58, 0.41-0.84) without a significant mortality effect (RR 0.86, 0.68-1.09) — and no placebo-controlled first-line trials of ARBs existed to include at all (PMID 29667175). Benefit is not universal, and the largest modern primary-prevention test was null: HOPE-3 randomised 12,705 intermediate-risk adults without cardiovascular disease and a mean baseline of 138.1/81.9 mm Hg to candesartan plus hydrochlorothiazide or placebo for a median 5.6 years, and the first co-primary outcome occurred in 4.1% versus 4.4% (HR 0.93, 0.79-1.10; p=0.40), with benefit confined to the upper third of baseline systolic pressure above 143.5 mm Hg (PMID 27041480). Likewise ACCORD-BP found an intensive target achieving 119.3 versus 133.5 mm Hg in 4,733 adults with type 2 diabetes did not reduce the primary composite (1.87 vs 2.09% per year; HR 0.88, 0.73-1.06; p=0.20) while treatment-attributed serious adverse events rose from 1.3% to 3.3% (PMID 20228401), and in low-risk stage 1 hypertension a Cochrane review of 4 trials (8,912 participants, 4-5 years) found no reduction in mortality (RR 0.85, 0.63-1.15), coronary heart disease (RR 1.12, 0.80-1.57), stroke (RR 0.51, 0.24-1.08) or total cardiovascular events (RR 0.97, 0.72-1.32), with a 9% absolute excess of withdrawal for adverse effects (RR 4.80, 4.14-5.57) (PMID 22895954). Which drug is chosen matters less than whether pressure falls: ALLHAT randomised 33,357 higher-risk adults aged 55 or older to chlorthalidone, amlodipine or lisinopril with no difference in the primary coronary outcome (6-year rates 11.5%, 11.3%, 11.4%) or all-cause mortality, though chlorthalidone beat amlodipine on heart failure (7.7% vs 10.2%; RR 1.38, 1.25-1.52) and lisinopril on stroke (5.6% vs 6.3%; RR 1.15, 1.02-1.30) (PMID 12479763), a 13,523-patient pragmatic trial found chlorthalidone no better than hydrochlorothiazide (10.4% vs 10.0%; HR 1.04, 0.94-1.16; p=0.45) with more hypokalemia (PMID 36516076), and beta-blockers — three-quarters of them atenolol — showed no mortality benefit over placebo (RR 0.99, 0.88-1.11) and more stroke than calcium channel blockers (RR 1.24, 1.11-1.40), with mortality higher on a confidence interval that reaches unity (RR 1.07, 1.00-1.14) (PMID 28107561). At the high-benefit end, SPRINT's intensive target in 9,361 higher-risk adults without diabetes (achieved 121.4 vs 136.2 mm Hg) reduced the primary composite from 2.19% to 1.65% per year (HR 0.75, 0.64-0.89) and all-cause death (HR 0.73, 0.60-0.90), but was stopped early at a median 3.26 years — a design that tends to overstate effect size — and raised hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney injury, though not injurious falls (PMID 26551272).

Doses studied: Standard licensed doses of the first-line classes, titrated to a blood-pressure target rather than given at a fixed dose. ALLHAT used chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg/day, lisinopril 10-40 mg/day and amlodipine 2.5-10 mg/day (PMID 12479763); the Diuretic Comparison Project compared chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg/day with hydrochlorothiazide 25-50 mg/day (PMID 36516076); HOPE-3 used candesartan 16 mg/day plus hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg/day (PMID 27041480). Trials compared drug versus placebo, class versus class, or a standard systolic target of <140 mm Hg versus an intensive target of <120 mm Hg (SPRINT, ACCORD-BP).

Why this grade: An individual participant-level meta-analysis of 48 randomised trials (344,716 participants) and a Cochrane review of 24 placebo-controlled trials (58,040 participants) show reductions in death, stroke and coronary heart disease proportional to the mmHg lowered, and these classes are guideline first-line therapy for established hypertension. The endpoints are hard clinical events rather than the blood-pressure number alone. Graded strong despite substantial honest nulls — HOPE-3 found no benefit in 12,705 intermediate-risk adults averaging 138/82 mm Hg, ACCORD-BP found no primary-outcome benefit from an intensive target in type 2 diabetes, and a Cochrane review found no benefit in low-risk stage 1 hypertension — because these define where benefit is small or absent rather than overturning the overall outcome evidence. Two further caveats sit inside the grade: the strongest placebo-controlled first-line evidence contains no ARB trials at all, and the largest positive target trial, SPRINT, was stopped early.

Sources (10)
  1. Pharmacological blood pressure lowering for primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease across different levels of blood pressure: an individual participant-level data meta-analysis
    Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists' Collaboration, Lancet 2021 · PMID 33933205 · positive

    Across 48 randomised trials and 344,716 participants (median 4.15 years' follow-up), a 5 mm Hg systolic reduction lowered major cardiovascular events by about 10%: HR 0.91 (95% CI 0.89-0.94) in people without prior cardiovascular disease and HR 0.89 (0.86-0.92) in those with it, with no reliable heterogeneity across baseline systolic categories from <120 to >=170 mm Hg. Primary-prevention event rates fell from 31.9 to 25.9 per 1000 person-years; baseline pressures were 157/89 mm Hg without and 146/84 mm Hg with prior cardiovascular disease.

  2. First-line drugs for hypertension
    Wright JM, Musini VM, Gill R, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2018 · PMID 29667175 · positive

    In 24 placebo-controlled trials (58,040 participants, mean age 56, follow-up 3-5 years), first-line low-dose thiazides reduced all-cause mortality 11.0% to 9.8% (RR 0.89, 0.82-0.97), stroke 6.2% to 4.2% (RR 0.68, 0.60-0.77), coronary heart disease 3.9% to 2.8% (RR 0.72, 0.61-0.84) and total cardiovascular events 12.9% to 9.0% (RR 0.70, 0.64-0.76); ACE inhibitors reduced mortality (RR 0.83, 0.72-0.95). Calcium channel blockers reduced stroke (RR 0.58, 0.41-0.84) but not mortality (RR 0.86, 0.68-1.09), withdrawals for adverse effects rose from 5.0% to 11.3% with low-dose thiazides (RR 2.38, 2.06-2.75), and the review found NO randomised trials of ARBs or alpha-blockers as first-line therapy.

  3. A Randomized Trial of Intensive versus Standard Blood-Pressure Control
    SPRINT Research Group (Wright JT, Williamson JD, et al.), N Engl J Med 2015 · PMID 26551272 · positive

    In 9,361 higher-risk adults without diabetes, targeting systolic <120 mm Hg versus <140 mm Hg achieved 121.4 vs 136.2 mm Hg and reduced the primary composite of myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, heart failure or cardiovascular death from 2.19% to 1.65% per year (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64-0.89) and all-cause mortality (HR 0.73, 0.60-0.90). The trial was STOPPED EARLY after a median follow-up of only 3.26 years, which tends to overstate effect size. Serious adverse events of hypotension, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities and acute kidney injury were higher with intensive treatment; injurious falls were not.

  4. Blood-Pressure Lowering in Intermediate-Risk Persons without Cardiovascular Disease
    Lonn EM, et al. (HOPE-3 Investigators), N Engl J Med 2016 · PMID 27041480 · null

    In 12,705 intermediate-risk adults without cardiovascular disease (mean baseline 138.1/81.9 mm Hg), candesartan 16 mg plus hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg daily versus placebo for a median 5.6 years lowered blood pressure by a further 6.0/3.0 mm Hg but did NOT reduce events: the first co-primary outcome occurred in 260 participants (4.1%) versus 279 (4.4%) (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.79-1.10; p=0.40) and the second in 312 (4.9%) versus 328 (5.2%) (HR 0.95, 0.81-1.11; p=0.51). Benefit appeared only in the prespecified subgroup in the upper third of baseline systolic pressure (>143.5 mm Hg); effects were neutral in the middle and lower thirds.

  5. Effects of intensive blood-pressure control in type 2 diabetes mellitus
    ACCORD Study Group (Cushman WC, Evans GW, et al.), N Engl J Med 2010 · PMID 20228401 · null

    In 4,733 adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, an intensive target achieving 119.3 vs 133.5 mm Hg did NOT reduce the primary composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke or cardiovascular death (1.87 vs 2.09% per year; HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.73-1.06; p=0.20) and did not reduce death from any cause (1.28 vs 1.19% per year; HR 1.07, 0.85-1.35; p=0.55). Stroke, a prespecified secondary outcome, was lower (0.32 vs 0.53% per year; HR 0.59, 0.39-0.89; p=0.01), while serious adverse events attributed to antihypertensive treatment rose from 1.3% to 3.3% (p<0.001).

  6. Pharmacotherapy for mild hypertension
    Diao D, Wright JM, Cundiff DK, Gueyffier F, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2012 · PMID 22895954 · null

    In 4 randomised trials (8,912 primary-prevention adults with systolic 140-159 and/or diastolic 90-99 mm Hg and no cardiovascular disease), 4-5 years of antihypertensive drug therapy versus placebo did not reduce total mortality (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.63-1.15) and, in 7,080 participants, did not reduce coronary heart disease (RR 1.12, 0.80-1.57), stroke (RR 0.51, 0.24-1.08) or total cardiovascular events (RR 0.97, 0.72-1.32). Withdrawals due to adverse effects were markedly increased (RR 4.80, 4.14-5.57; absolute risk increase 9%).

  7. Chlorthalidone vs. Hydrochlorothiazide for Hypertension-Cardiovascular Events
    Ishani A, Cushman WC, Leatherman SM, et al., N Engl J Med 2022 · PMID 36516076 · null

    In a pragmatic randomised trial of 13,523 Veterans Affairs patients aged 65 or older (mean age 72, mean baseline systolic 139 mm Hg) already taking hydrochlorothiazide 25-50 mg/day, switching to chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg/day produced NO reduction in the primary composite of nonfatal myocardial infarction, stroke, heart-failure hospitalisation, urgent revascularisation for unstable angina or non-cancer death over a median 2.4 years: 702 patients (10.4%) versus 675 (10.0%) (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.94-1.16; p=0.45), with no difference in any component. Hypokalemia was more common with chlorthalidone (6.0% vs 4.4%, p<0.001).

  8. Major outcomes in high-risk hypertensive patients randomized to angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or calcium channel blocker vs diuretic: The Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT)
    ALLHAT Collaborative Research Group, JAMA 2002 · PMID 12479763 · mixed

    In 33,357 hypertensive adults aged 55 or older with at least one other coronary risk factor, randomised to chlorthalidone, amlodipine or lisinopril and followed a mean 4.9 years, there was NO difference in the primary outcome of fatal coronary heart disease or nonfatal myocardial infarction (6-year rates 11.5%, 11.3%, 11.4%; RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.90-1.07 for amlodipine and 0.99, 0.91-1.08 for lisinopril) and no difference in all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes favoured chlorthalidone: heart failure was higher with amlodipine (10.2% vs 7.7%; RR 1.38, 1.25-1.52), and combined cardiovascular disease (33.3% vs 30.9%; RR 1.10, 1.05-1.16), stroke (6.3% vs 5.6%; RR 1.15, 1.02-1.30) and heart failure (8.7% vs 7.7%; RR 1.19, 1.07-1.31) were higher with lisinopril.

  9. Beta-blockers for hypertension
    Wiysonge CS, Bradley HA, Volmink J, Mayosi BM, Opie LH, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2017 · PMID 28107561 · mixed

    Across 13 randomised trials (40,245 participants taking beta-blockers, three-quarters atenolol), beta-blockers as first-line therapy showed no all-cause mortality benefit versus placebo (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.88-1.11) and no reduction in coronary heart disease (RR 0.93, 0.81-1.07), though total cardiovascular disease fell (RR 0.88, 0.79-0.97) via stroke reduction (RR 0.80, 0.66-0.96). Compared with calcium channel blockers, mortality was higher on an interval reaching unity (RR 1.07, 1.00-1.14) and stroke was clearly higher (RR 1.24, 1.11-1.40), as was stroke versus renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (RR 1.30, 1.11-1.53). Most included trials had a high risk of bias.

  10. Benefits and Harms of Intensive Blood Pressure Treatment in Adults Aged 60 Years or Older: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
    Weiss J, Freeman M, Low A, et al., Ann Intern Med 2017 · PMID 28114673 · mixed

    Across 21 randomised trials plus 3 observational studies of harms, nine trials gave high-strength evidence that control to <150/90 mm Hg reduces mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.83-0.98), cardiac events (RR 0.77, 0.68-0.89) and stroke (RR 0.74, 0.65-0.84), while six trials gave only low- to moderate-strength evidence for lower targets (<=140/85 mm Hg), with marginal effects on cardiac events (RR 0.82, 0.64-1.00) and stroke (RR 0.79, 0.59-0.99) and no significant mortality benefit (RR 0.86, 0.69-1.06). Lower targets did NOT increase falls or cognitive impairment, but were associated with hypotension, syncope and greater medication burden.
