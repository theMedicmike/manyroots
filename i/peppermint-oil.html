Herbs

Enteric-coated peppermint oil

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Peppermint oil in a capsule designed to open further down the gut is the best-studied herb for IBS, and when many small trials are pooled it looks helpful for cramping pain and overall symptoms. But the three largest and most carefully run modern trials — two in adults and one in 228 children — all found it no better than a dummy capsule, so any real effect is probably smaller than the older studies suggest. It also causes more side effects than placebo, most often heartburn.

Safety

Peppermint oil is generally well tolerated but is not side-effect free: in the 2022 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs any adverse event was significantly more common than with placebo (RR 1.57, 95% CI 1.04-2.37), and in the PERSUADE trial adverse events were mild but significantly more frequent in both peppermint arms (P<.005). Heartburn is the specific harm that recurs across this literature — the 2014 meta-analysis names it as the most commonly reported adverse event, and the 2022 meta-analysis assessed gastro-oesophageal reflux as a separate safety outcome. Peppermint oil relaxes smooth muscle, including the lower oesophageal sphincter, so anyone with reflux disease or a hiatus hernia should treat worsening heartburn as an expected effect and stop if it is troublesome. The enteric or delayed-release coating is the point of the product, so capsules must be swallowed whole and never chewed, crushed or opened, which can cause mouth, throat and oesophageal burning. Perianal or rectal burning is also reported. In the 228-child trial adverse events were mild and infrequent, but safety at supplement doses in pregnancy or breastfeeding has not been established. Undiluted peppermint essential oil is not the same product as an enteric-coated IBS capsule and should not be swallowed.

Who should be careful: Avoid, or use only with clinician oversight, if you have significant gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, a hiatus hernia, or achalasia, since the effect on the lower oesophageal sphincter can make reflux worse. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding. In children, the one large paediatric trial found no benefit over placebo, so there is no efficacy case for using it in that group. New or alarm features — weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, symptom onset after age 50, family history of bowel cancer or coeliac disease — need medical assessment before treating symptoms as IBS.

Interactions: The IBS trials were not designed to detect drug interactions and none has been characterised in them. Because the capsules are pH-dependent enteric-coated, acid-suppressing drugs (proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers) and antacids can in principle cause the coating to dissolve early in the stomach and provoke reflux; separate the doses and ask a pharmacist. Anyone taking prescription medicines for reflux, or with liver or gallbladder disease, should check before starting.

What the evidence says

The 2022 update by Ingrosso and colleagues pooled 10 RCTs (1,030 patients) and found peppermint oil superior to placebo for global IBS symptoms (relative risk of not improving 0.65, 95% CI 0.43-0.98; NNT 4, 95% CI 2.5-71) and abdominal pain (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.62-0.93; NNT 7, 95% CI 4-24), while adverse events were significantly more common (RR 1.57, 95% CI 1.04-2.37) and overall evidence quality was rated very low. Other meta-analyses report larger effects: 12 RCTs/835 patients (global RR 2.39, 95% CI 1.93-2.97; pain RR 1.78, 95% CI 1.43-2.20), 9 studies/726 patients (global RR 2.23, 95% CI 1.78-2.81; pain RR 2.14, 95% CI 1.64-2.79), and a 2026 review of 60 studies/6,329 patients (peppermint RR 1.63, 95% CI 1.33-2.00). Against this, the three largest rigorous modern trials were all null: the Dutch PERSUADE trial (189 Rome IV patients analysed) missed both regulatory primary endpoints, with abdominal pain response 46.8% versus 34.4% placebo (P=.170) and overall relief 9.7% versus 4.7% (P=.317), though secondary pain, discomfort and severity measures improved (P=.016, .020, .020). A 6-week US trial of 180 mg enteric-coated peppermint oil three times daily found IBS-SSS improvement of 90.8 points versus 100.3 with placebo, numerically favouring placebo (d=-0.11, P=0.97), with no difference on any secondary endpoint. A 2026 trial in 228 children across 13 Dutch hospitals found treatment success in 44.0% on peppermint oil, 38.7% on peppermint sweets and 37.3% on placebo (OR 1.33, 97.5% CI 0.58-3.09, P=.44), with adequate relief likewise comparable (53.3% versus 49.3%, P=.68). A 2025 umbrella review of 175 trials across 58 meta-analyses concluded peppermint oil's effects were mainly small with low to very low GRADE certainty, in contrast to probiotics for abdominal pain (RR 4.04) and the low-FODMAP diet (RR 1.48), which reached moderate certainty; none of the trials cited here reported subtype-specific results.

Doses studied: Enteric-coated capsules, 180 mg three times daily for 6 weeks (Nee 2021). Small-intestinal-release capsules, 182 mg over 8 weeks (Weerts 2020; the published abstract does not state the dosing frequency). In children aged 8-18, 182 mg capsules twice daily (ages 8-11) or three times daily (ages 12-18) for 8 weeks (Vermeijden 2026). Pooled meta-analyses cover trials mostly in the 180-225 mg three-times-daily range over 2-12 weeks.

Why this grade: Four meta-analyses of placebo-controlled trials find a consistent pooled benefit for global IBS symptoms and abdominal pain (Ingrosso 2022 NNT 4; Alammar 2019 NNT 3; Khanna 2014; Meyer 2026 RR 1.63), and peppermint oil is the only plant-based therapy consistently carried in adult and paediatric IBS guidelines, which rules out both 'early' and 'not_supported'. But it falls well short of 'strong': the three largest and most methodologically rigorous modern trials were all null — Weerts 2020 (n=189 adults) and Nee 2021 both missed their pre-specified primary endpoints, and Vermeijden 2026 (n=228 children) found neither peppermint oil nor peppermint sweets superior to placebo. Ingrosso graded the pooled evidence very low quality with a confidence interval touching 0.98, a 2025 umbrella review rated certainty low to very low, and adverse events were significantly more frequent than placebo. The pooled positive signal therefore rests largely on older, smaller trials while every large modern trial has been negative. Taking the lower of the two candidate grades gives moderate. None of the trials cited here reported separate results for IBS-C, IBS-D or mixed subtypes, so no subtype claim is supportable.

Sources (8)
  1. Systematic review and meta-analysis: efficacy of peppermint oil in irritable bowel syndrome.
    Ingrosso MR, Ianiro G, Nee J, Lembo AJ, Moayyedi P, Black CJ, Ford AC, 2022 · PMID 35942669 · positive

    10 RCTs, 1,030 patients: RR of global IBS symptoms not improving 0.65 (95% CI 0.43-0.98), NNT 4 (95% CI 2.5-71); RR of abdominal pain not improving 0.76 (95% CI 0.62-0.93), NNT 7 (95% CI 4-24). Adverse events significantly more frequent with peppermint oil (RR of any adverse event 1.57, 95% CI 1.04-2.37). The authors state the quality of evidence was very low and that adequately powered RCTs of peppermint oil as first-line treatment are still needed.

  2. Peppermint Oil and Sweets in Pediatric Irritable Bowel Syndrome and Functional Abdominal Pain: A Randomized Trial.
    Vermeijden NK, van der Feen C, Groeneweg M, et al., 2026 · PMID 41610933 · null

    Largest randomised peppermint trial to date: 228 children aged 8-18 with IBS or functional abdominal pain across 13 Dutch hospitals, randomised 1:1:1 to blinded 182 mg peppermint oil capsules, open-label peppermint sweets, or placebo for 8 weeks. Treatment success (at least 30% reduction in abdominal pain intensity) was 44.0% with peppermint oil versus 37.3% with placebo (OR 1.33; 97.5% CI 0.58-3.09; P=.44), and 38.7% with peppermint sweets versus placebo (OR 1.05; 97.5% CI 0.45-2.44; P=.91). Adequate relief was also comparable: 53.3% versus 49.3% (OR 1.15; 97.5% CI 0.54-2.47; P=.68). Adverse events were mild and infrequent. The authors conclude neither preparation was superior to placebo and that proven gut-brain-axis interventions should be prioritised in this population.

  3. Efficacy and Safety of Peppermint Oil in a Randomized, Double-Blind Trial of Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    Weerts ZZRM, Masclee AAM, Witteman BJM, et al. (PERSUADE), 2020 · PMID 31470006 · null

    190 Rome IV patients enrolled at 4 Dutch hospitals, 189 analysed in the intention-to-treat set, 8 weeks. Abdominal pain response (FDA endpoint): 46.8% small-intestinal-release peppermint oil (P=.170) and 41.3% ileocolonic-release (P=.385) versus 34.4% placebo. Overall relief (EMA endpoint): 9.7% (P=.317) and 1.6% (P=.351) versus 4.7% placebo. Neither primary endpoint was met; secondary abdominal pain, discomfort and IBS severity did improve with the small-intestinal-release formulation (P=.016, .020, .020), and adverse events, though mild, were more common in both peppermint arms (P<.005).

  4. Peppermint Oil Treatment for Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Nee J, Ballou S, Kelley JM, Kaptchuk TJ, et al., 2021 · PMID 34319275 · null

    6-week US single-centre trial of enteric-coated peppermint oil 180 mg three times daily versus placebo (1:2 ratio) in Rome IV IBS with IBS-SSS at least 175. Mean IBS-SSS improvement was 90.8 (SD 75.3) with peppermint oil versus 100.3 (SD 99.6) with placebo, numerically favouring placebo; effect size d=-0.11, P=0.97. No significant difference on any secondary endpoint, and multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses produced the same result.

  5. The impact of peppermint oil on the irritable bowel syndrome: a meta-analysis of the pooled clinical data.
    Alammar N, Wang L, Saberi B, Nanavati J, Holtmann G, Shinohara RT, Mullin GE, 2019 · PMID 30654773 · positive

    12 randomised trials, 835 patients. Global symptom improvement RR 2.39 (95% CI 1.93-2.97) from 7 RCTs (253 versus 254 patients), I-squared 0%; abdominal pain RR 1.78 (95% CI 1.43-2.20) from 6 RCTs (278 versus 278). Number needed to treat 3 for global symptoms and 4 for abdominal pain. Adverse events 9.3% (32/344) versus 6.1% (20/327) with placebo across 8 RCTs, RR 1.40 (95% CI 0.87-2.26), not statistically significant.

  6. Peppermint oil for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Khanna R, MacDonald JK, Levesque BG, 2014 · PMID 24100754 · positive

    9 studies, 726 patients. Peppermint oil superior to placebo for global improvement of IBS symptoms (5 studies, 392 patients, RR 2.23; 95% CI 1.78-2.81) and for improvement in abdominal pain (5 studies, 357 patients, RR 2.14; 95% CI 1.64-2.79). Peppermint oil patients were significantly more likely to experience an adverse event, though events were mild and transient, and the authors state the most commonly reported adverse event was heartburn.

  7. Nutritional Interventions in Adult Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome: An Umbrella Review of Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses of Randomized Clinical Trials.
    Zeraattalab-Motlagh S, Ranjbar M, Mohammadi H, Adibi P, 2025 · PMID 39110917 · mixed

    Umbrella review of 175 trials across 58 meta-analyses covering 11 nutritional interventions. Peppermint oil, soluble fibre and aloe vera improved IBS symptoms, but effects were mainly small with low to very low GRADE certainty. Only probiotics for abdominal pain (RR 4.04, 95% CI 2.36-6.92) and a low-FODMAP diet for IBS symptoms (RR 1.48, 95% CI 1.14-1.93) reached moderate certainty. The authors conclude the review does not, overall, support nutritional interventions for improving IBS symptoms.

  8. Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis: Plant-Based Therapy for Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    Meyer A, Auer J, Cascant-Ortolano L, et al., 2026 · PMID 42572206 · positive

    60 eligible studies, 6,329 patients, literature searched to May 2024. Peppermint oil (7 studies) RR 1.63 (95% CI 1.33-2.00) versus placebo with a good safety profile; Chinese herbal medicine (7 studies) RR 1.56 (95% CI 1.27-1.92); dietary fibre (8 studies) RR 1.22 (95% CI 1.00-1.49), approaching but not reaching significance; Aloe vera (3 studies) RR 1.25 (95% CI 0.76-2.06), null. The authors note the quality of many included studies was limited and that further well-powered RCTs are needed.
