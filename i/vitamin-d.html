Vitamin D

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy Early evidence · for gut & digestion Not supported by evidence · for chronic pain

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Vitamin D has been tested against whether people with prediabetes go on to develop diabetes — but so have diet, exercise, metformin and tirzepatide, several of them with much larger effects, and that diagnosis is itself a blood-test threshold rather than something a person feels. Three large vitamin D trials each fell just short on their own, but when their individual data were pooled the risk of crossing into diabetes was about 15% lower, roughly three fewer cases per 100 people over three years. In people who already have type 2 diabetes the effect on blood sugar is small and inconsistent, there is no evidence of benefit for people whose vitamin D level is already good, and no trial has shown it prevents heart attacks, kidney disease or death — it is not a substitute for any medication you have been prescribed.

Safety

Vitamin D is positioned as an inexpensive adjunct to lifestyle intervention and metformin in prediabetes, not a replacement for either (PMID 41707752), and it is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication; nothing on this page is a reason to stop, reduce or delay a prescribed drug. At 4,000 IU/day for 3 years in 2,423 overweight adults with prediabetes, total adverse events were slightly lower than placebo (IRR 0.94, 95% CI 0.90-0.98) with no difference in serious adverse events (PMID 35140313); monthly 100,000 IU for a median 3.3 years did not increase kidney stone events (HR 0.90, 0.66-1.23) or hypercalcaemia in 5,110 adults (PMID 31005969). Two honest cautions from D2d secondary analyses: eGFR fell by 1.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 more than placebo (PMID 34362787) and major adverse cardiovascular events were numerically higher (21 vs 12, HR 1.81, 95% CI 0.89-3.69, not significant; PMID 35753926, in which the change in ASCVD risk score nonetheless favoured vitamin D); neither is proven harm, both are worth disclosing. A caution about the level-chasing this evidence invites: the 25(OH)D figure of 125 nmol/L or above comes from comparing people by what their blood level happened to reach after randomisation, not from any dose anyone was assigned. It is a hypothesis, not a target to chase, and chasing it is how people get into trouble — the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline found no clear evidence defining an optimal target 25(OH)D level for disease prevention and suggests against routine 25(OH)D testing in the populations it considered (PMID 38828931). The largest prevention trial used 4,000 IU/day, which is the tolerable upper intake level for adults set by the National Academy of Medicine (PMID 35140313); the second cholecalciferol trial used 20,000 IU a week, about 2,900 IU a day, and the third used a prescription analogue rather than vitamin D3 at all. Do not go above 4,000 IU/day to hit a number without a clinician monitoring your blood calcium and kidney function — and do not go there at all if you take a thiazide diuretic or a calcium supplement, for the reason described above.

Who should be careful: The prevention trials excluded people with hyperparathyroidism, kidney stones or hypercalcaemia (PMID 25205139), so those are the sensible exclusions for high-dose use. Granulomatous disease is the other standard exclusion for high-dose use: in sarcoidosis, tuberculosis and some lymphomas, immune cells make their own active vitamin D through an enzyme whose output rises with how much substrate you supply, so supplementing can raise blood or urine calcium in a way it does not in other people — hypercalciuria occurred in one of thirteen and two of sixteen patients in two small sarcoidosis studies (PMID 33659946), and the same macrophage mechanism is documented in tuberculosis and other granulomatoses (PMID 11909970) and in calcitriol-mediated hypercalcaemia from lymphoma (PMID 27124160). Anyone with one of these conditions should only take vitamin D with specialist supervision and calcium monitoring. Eldecalcitol is a prescription active vitamin D analogue tested in Japan, not an over-the-counter supplement, and its results do not transfer to ordinary vitamin D3 products.

Interactions: The D2d safety analysis found no increase in hypercalcaemia, hypercalciuria, kidney stones or low eGFR at 4,000 IU/day (PMID 35140313), but the pooled prevention trials were not powered for safety and showed a non-significant hypercalcaemia rate ratio of 2.34 (0.83-6.66) (PMID 36745886). The real hazard is stacking calcium-raising agents: a woman taking calcium carbonate 1 g three times daily plus calcitriol plus cholecalciferol 10,000 IU/day while on chlorthalidone presented with a serum calcium of 23 mg/dL, acute kidney injury and metabolic alkalosis (calcium-alkali-thiazide syndrome, PMID 33178509), and thiazide diuretics are a recognized drug cause of hypercalcaemia (PMID 28806048) — so anyone on a thiazide, or already taking calcium, should not add high-dose vitamin D without medical supervision. Individual 25(OH)D responses to the same dose vary by roughly 60 nmol/L depending on genetics, body mass and starting level (PMID 25702786), so checking a blood level is reasonable at high doses.

What the evidence says

Prevention in prediabetes has been tested against diabetes onset. D2d (n=2,423, 4,000 IU/day, median 2.5 years) found new diabetes at 9.39 vs 10.66 per 100 person-years, HR 0.88 (95% CI 0.75-1.04, P=0.12); the Tromso trial (n=511, 20,000 IU/week, 5 years, mean baseline 25(OH)D 60 nmol/L) found HR 0.90 (0.69-1.18); and DPVD (n=1,256, eldecalcitol 0.75 ug/day, median 2.9 years) found HR 0.87 (0.67-1.17, P=0.39) on its primary endpoint, reaching significance only after multivariable adjustment (HR 0.69, 0.51-0.95, P=0.020) (PMID 35613725) — each individually non-significant as randomized. The individual-participant meta-analysis of these three trials (n=4,190) found an adjusted HR 0.85 (0.75-0.96), a 3-year absolute risk reduction of 3.3% (0.6-6.0%), regression to normal glucose regulation RR 1.30 (1.16-1.46), and HR 0.24 among cholecalciferol recipients who maintained 25(OH)D >=125 nmol/L versus 50-74 nmol/L (a post-randomization comparison that is hypothesis-generating, not a treatment effect); an aggregate meta-analysis of 8 trials (n=4,896) similarly found RR 0.89 (0.80-0.99) with benefit confined to non-obese participants (PMID 33534730), and a 2026 review notes the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline now recommends vitamin D for adults with prediabetes without routine level testing (PMID 41707752). Glycaemic surrogates in established type 2 diabetes are modest and quality-dependent: Chen 2024 (39 RCTs, n=2,982) found HbA1c -0.30% (-0.43 to -0.18) and Farahmand 2023 (46 RCTs) -0.20% (-0.29 to -0.11) (PMID 36647067), with larger effects in vitamin D-deficient patients, whereas an individual-participant analysis of 12 RCTs (n=2,994) with reference-standard 25(OH)D re-measurement found no HbA1c effect, a 2026 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (n=3,460) found HbA1c SMD 0.08 (-0.25 to 0.37), and single trials at 1,000 IU/day for 24 weeks (HbA1c 7.40% on vitamin D vs 7.27% on placebo, P=0.415; PMID 24240575) and 100,000 IU monthly for 4 months in at-risk adults (HbA1c difference +0.02%, PMID 26700109) were null. No trial has shown a reduction in cardiovascular events (D2d secondary MACE 21 vs 12, HR 1.81, 95% CI 0.89-3.69, not significant, PMID 35753926) or kidney disease (PMID 34362787). Population summary: prediabetes, modest reduction in progression, best supported when baseline 25(OH)D is low or BMI is under 30; type 2 diabetes, small and inconsistent HbA1c effect; healthy vitamin D-replete people, no evidence of benefit.

Doses studied: Prevention trials: cholecalciferol 4,000 IU/day (D2d, 2.5 years) or 20,000 IU/week (Tromso, 5 years), or the prescription active analogue eldecalcitol 0.75 ug/day (DPVD, median 2.9 years). No trial assigned a blood level: the observation that outcomes were better in people whose achieved 25(OH)D reached 125 nmol/L or higher is a post-randomization comparison, not a target dose. Type 2 diabetes trials: 1,000 IU/day to 60,000 IU (or 100,000 IU monthly) for 3-12 months.

Why this grade: This is one of several interventions in the set tested against diabetes onset rather than a blood number — the DPP lifestyle programme and metformin (PMID 11832527), Da Qing exercise (PMID 9096977), PREDIMED (PMID 24573661) and tirzepatide (PMID 39536238) were all tested against that same endpoint, and several produced considerably larger reductions in their own trials — and diabetes onset is itself a glucose-threshold diagnosis, not an event a person feels. Within that limit, pooled individual-participant data from three low-risk-of-bias RCTs plus a 2024 Endocrine Society guideline support a real but modest 15% relative reduction in progression from prediabetes to diabetes. It stops short of strong: none of the three trials was significant on its own, the absolute benefit is about 3 fewer cases per 100 people over 3 years, and glycaemic effects in established type 2 diabetes are small and disappear entirely in the highest-quality individual-participant analysis. Diabetes onset is also not a hard clinical outcome in the sense that matters most — no vitamin D trial has reduced cardiovascular events, kidney disease or death, and the D2d cardiovascular secondary analysis ran numerically the wrong way. Moderate, not strong.

The authors call for larger placebo-controlled randomized studies in this population.

For depression & low mood

Vitamin D is a case where the headline and the best studies disagree: pooling many trials shows a small average improvement in depression scores, but those pooled results come from studies of uneven quality, and one of them has since been re-analysed by other researchers who found the benefit did not hold up. The largest and most careful trials — including one that followed more than 18,000 adults for over five years, and one run specifically in older people who had both low vitamin D and real depressive symptoms — found no effect on mood at all. It is still worth knowing your level, because correcting a genuine deficiency matters for bone and muscle health whatever it does for mood.

Safety

At ordinary supplemental doses (up to about 2,000-4,000 IU/day) vitamin D is well tolerated; 2,000 IU/day was administered for a median of 5.3 years in the VITAL-DEP trial. The real risk is at high or unmonitored doses, where vitamin D can cause hypercalcemia with nausea, vomiting, constipation, confusion, excessive thirst and urination, kidney stones and kidney injury. Very high intermittent bolus dosing (large monthly or annual doses) has been linked to increased falls and fractures in older adults, so daily moderate dosing is preferred over megadoses. Get a blood 25(OH)D level rather than guessing at a dose. Vitamin D is not a substitute for antidepressant treatment or psychotherapy, and the trial evidence does not support using it to prevent depression. There is no specific bipolar-related safety concern with vitamin D, but supplement changes should still be discussed with the prescribing clinician.

Who should be careful: Do not take high-dose vitamin D without medical supervision if you have hypercalcemia, primary hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis or other granulomatous disease, lymphoma, a history of calcium-containing kidney stones, or significant kidney disease — these conditions raise the risk of dangerously high calcium.

Interactions: Thiazide diuretics combined with vitamin D and calcium can raise blood calcium. High calcium from vitamin D excess increases digoxin toxicity risk. Corticosteroids, orlistat, cholestyramine, some anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital) and rifampin reduce vitamin D levels or absorption. Take vitamin D with a meal containing fat for absorption.

What the evidence says

A 2023 meta-analysis of 41 placebo-controlled RCTs (53,235 participants) found a small pooled reduction in depressive symptoms (Hedges g -0.317, 95% CI -0.405 to -0.230, p < 0.001) but with I-squared of 88.16%, concerning risk of bias in most studies, and GRADE certainty rated very low; the signal appeared at doses of 2,000 IU/day or more. A second meta-analysis of 18 RCTs in adults with primary depression reported a smaller overall effect (SMD -0.15, 95% CI -0.26 to -0.04) present only in the subgroup with baseline 25(OH)D above 50 nmol/L (SMD -0.38), but a 2025 re-analysis of that same review found deviations from its stated eligibility criteria, an omitted high-quality trial and two influential outliers, and reported no evidence of subgroup differences and no statistically significant effect within either subgroup. Two earlier meta-analyses were also null: 6 RCTs and 1,203 participants (SMD -0.14, 95% CI -0.41 to 0.13, P = .32; GRADE low) and 9 trials with 4,923 participants (SMD 0.28, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.69, P = 0.19). Against this, VITAL-DEP randomized 18,353 adults aged 50+ to 2,000 IU/day cholecalciferol or placebo for a median 5.3 years and found no effect on incident or recurrent depression (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87 to 1.09, P = .62) and no change in PHQ-8 mood scores (mean difference 0.01, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.05); D-Vitaal enrolled the group most likely to benefit — 155 adults aged 60-80 with clinically relevant depressive symptoms, functional limitations and 25(OH)D of 15-50/70 nmol/L — gave 1,200 IU/day for 12 months, raised mean 25(OH)D from 43 to 85 nmol/L, and found no relevant difference in depressive symptoms or any secondary outcome; and a 6-month trial of 40,000 IU/week in 230 people with low 25(OH)D found no effect across four rating scales despite confirming that low 25(OH)D was cross-sectionally associated with more depressive symptoms. The clearest positive subgroup is perinatal, where a meta-analysis of 28 RCTs found vitamin D at 1,800-3,500 IU/day associated with SMD -0.52 (95% CI -0.84 to -0.20) for postpartum depression.

Doses studied: 2,000 IU/day cholecalciferol for a median 5.3 years (VITAL-DEP, null); 1,200 IU/day for 12 months (D-Vitaal, null); 40,000 IU/week for 6 months (Kjaergaard, null); the pooled meta-analytic signal appeared at doses of 2,000 IU/day or more; 1,800-3,500 IU/day in the perinatal trials. The tolerable upper intake level for adults is generally taken as 4,000 IU/day without medical monitoring.

Why this grade: Two meta-analyses report statistically significant but small pooled reductions in depressive symptoms (Hedges g -0.317 across 41 RCTs and 53,235 participants; SMD -0.15 across 18 RCTs), which is the only reason this is not graded lower — and both are weak support. The first was rated very low certainty with I-squared of 88.16% and concerning risk of bias in most studies. The second has since been formally re-analysed in the same journal, which identified deviations from the stated eligibility criteria, an omitted high-quality trial, and two influential outliers, and found no subgroup differences and no statistically significant effect in either baseline-25(OH)D subgroup. Two further meta-analyses (6 RCTs, 1,203 participants; 9 trials, 4,923 participants) found no significant effect. Every rigorous trial is null: VITAL-DEP (n=18,353, median 5.3 years), D-Vitaal (n=155, older adults selected for BOTH low 25(OH)D and clinically relevant depressive symptoms), and a 6-month high-dose trial in 230 people with low 25(OH)D. Graded down from strong on certainty; not graded to early because the trial base is large rather than preliminary. For PREVENTION of depression in adults who are not depressed, the evidence is null.

For fatigue & low energy

Genuine vitamin D deficiency can leave you weak and tired, and it is cheap to test and easy to correct. What the research does not show is that topping up vitamin D lifts energy when your level is already fine. One small study found less tiredness after a single large dose in young deficient adults, but the biggest and longest studies - on mood, and on fatigue in multiple sclerosis - found nothing. Worth checking your level; not worth taking on faith, and not worth taking in occasional huge doses.

Safety

Vitamin D is not harmless at high doses. In 2,256 women aged 70 and over, a 500,000 IU dose given once each autumn-winter increased falls (rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.30) and fractures (rate ratio 1.26, 95% CI 1.00-1.59) rather than preventing them, with the excess clustered in the three months after dosing - a caution against occasional mega-doses. Sustained excessive intake causes hypercalcaemia, hypercalciuria, kidney stones and kidney injury. Ordinary daily doses within standard reference intakes were well tolerated across the trials above, with no increase in serious adverse events in the Cochrane multiple sclerosis review.

Who should be careful: Do not supplement without medical input if you have hypercalcaemia, primary hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis or another granulomatous disease, or some lymphomas - these conditions activate vitamin D abnormally and can push calcium to dangerous levels. Williams syndrome is a contraindication. People with kidney stones or chronic kidney disease should be supervised. Avoid intermittent very-high bolus dosing.

Interactions: Combining vitamin D with thiazide diuretics and calcium raises the risk of hypercalcaemia, and rising calcium increases digoxin toxicity risk. Enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital), rifampicin and glucocorticoids speed vitamin D breakdown; cholestyramine, orlistat and long-term corticosteroids reduce its absorption or effect.

What the evidence says

The one dedicated placebo-controlled fatigue trial randomised 120 otherwise-healthy adults (mean age 29, 53% women) with serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D below 20 ug/L to a single 100,000 IU oral dose or placebo; at four weeks the Fatigue Assessment Scale fell further with vitamin D (-3.3 plus or minus 5.3 versus -0.8 plus or minus 5.3, P=0.01) and improvement was reported by 72% versus 50% (OR 2.63, 95% CI 1.23-5.62). Beyond that, the evidence comes from specific diseases and has moved against a fatigue effect as the trials have accumulated. A 2023 meta-analysis of 5 RCTs in 345 people with multiple sclerosis found a small reduction in fatigue (SMD -0.18, 95% CI -0.36 to -0.01, I-squared 0%) - but a 2025 update pooling 21 RCTs and 1,903 multiple sclerosis patients found no effect on fatigue severity or quality of life, despite finding effects on disability, relapse rate and new T2 lesions; and a Cochrane review of 12 MS trials (933 participants) rated the evidence very low quality, with one trial (158 participants) reporting reduced fatigue at 26 weeks and another (71 participants) reporting none at 96 weeks. The large, long trial of general well-being is null: in VITAL-DEP, 2,000 IU/day versus placebo in 18,353 adults aged 50 and over across a median 5.3 years produced no difference in depression or clinically relevant depressive symptoms (hazard ratio 0.97, 95% CI 0.87-1.09, P=0.62) and no change in mood scores (mean difference 0.01 points, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.05). In lupus, a 2026 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (847 participants) found vitamin D reduced disease activity (SMD -0.54, P<0.001) but produced no significant change in fatigue severity (SMD -1.77, P=0.07). Taken together: a possible short-term benefit in people who are genuinely deficient, and nothing detectable in people who are not.

Doses studied: A single oral 100,000 IU dose of vitamin D3 in the dedicated fatigue trial; 2,000 IU/day for a median 5.3 years in the large mood trial; multiple sclerosis fatigue trials used varied doses and durations with no agreed regimen.

Why this grade: For fatigue specifically the human evidence is one small dedicated placebo-controlled trial (120 people, single dose, 4-week endpoint) plus fatigue as a secondary outcome in disease-specific trials that increasingly point to no effect. The large, long, well-powered trial of general mood found nothing; Cochrane rated the multiple sclerosis fatigue evidence very low quality with trials pointing in opposite directions; and a 2025 meta-analysis of 21 multiple sclerosis RCTs in 1,903 patients - five times the sample of the smaller 2023 meta-analysis - found no effect on fatigue or quality of life at all. Correcting genuine deficiency is worthwhile on other grounds, but the fatigue evidence base is preliminary and does not reach 'moderate'.

For gut & digestion

Low vitamin D is common in people with IBS, which sparked the idea that topping it up might calm gut symptoms. Some small studies did show improvement, but they were mostly done in people who were already deficient, the best-run independent trial found no difference in symptoms at all compared with a dummy capsule, and the most recent pooled analysis came out non-significant too. Getting a low vitamin D level corrected is worth doing for your bones and general health; treating it as an IBS remedy is getting ahead of the evidence.

Safety

At the doses used in IBS trials (2,000-3,000 IU/day, or 50,000 IU weekly to fortnightly for a few weeks to months) vitamin D was well tolerated and adverse events were low; the adolescent trial recorded none. Risk rises with sustained high intake: excess vitamin D causes hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria, with nausea, thirst, constipation, confusion and kidney stones, and prolonged megadosing can cause kidney damage. Large intermittent bolus dosing is not a safe shortcut - in a randomised trial of 2,256 older women, a single annual 500,000 IU dose increased falls (rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.30) and fractures (rate ratio 1.26, 95% CI 1.00-1.59) compared with placebo. If low vitamin D is the concern, the sensible route is a blood test and a dose matched to the result, rather than open-ended high-dose self-treatment aimed at gut symptoms. None of the IBS trials reported a change in stool form, so there is no evidence base for using vitamin D to correct diarrhoea or constipation.

Who should be careful: Do not take supplemental vitamin D without medical supervision if you have hypercalcaemia, primary hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis or another granulomatous disease, or certain lymphomas - these conditions increase conversion to active vitamin D and can cause dangerous calcium elevation at ordinary doses. People with kidney stones, chronic kidney disease or on dialysis need clinician-directed dosing and monitoring.

Interactions: Thiazide diuretics plus vitamin D (especially with calcium) can raise blood calcium; digoxin toxicity risk increases if calcium rises. Some anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine), rifampicin and glucocorticoids increase vitamin D breakdown, while orlistat, cholestyramine and colestipol reduce its absorption. Do not stack multiple sources - a multivitamin, a fish oil, a calcium-plus-D product and a standalone capsule can add up unnoticed.

What the evidence says

Trials are small, largely restricted to vitamin D-deficient or insufficient participants, and inconsistent with one another. One meta-analysis of 8 studies (685 patients) found improvement in IBS symptom severity, SMD -0.77 (95% CI -1.47 to -0.07, p = 0.04), but with I-squared = 91% heterogeneity and no significant effect on quality of life (SMD 0.54, 95% CI -0.34 to 1.41, p = 0.15); a second (6 RCTs, 572 patients) reported a symptom severity difference of -34.88 points (95% CI -62.48 to -7.27) with quality of life again non-significant. Pulling the other way, a meta-analysis of 6 trials (616 patients) found no significant effect on IBS symptom severity at all (MD -45.82, 95% CI -93.62 to 1.98, p = 0.06), and the most recent review (2024) likewise found only a non-significant trend across 6 RCTs (-25.89, 95% CI -55.26 to 3.48, I-squared = 92.8%), with a quality-of-life benefit confined to populations that were deficient at baseline. The most methodologically robust individual trial randomised 135 UK community participants to 3,000 IU/day or placebo for 12 weeks; 25(OH)D rose 45.1 nmol/L versus 3.1 nmol/L on placebo, yet symptom severity change did not differ (-62.5 vs -75.2, p = 0.426) nor did quality of life (p = 0.427). Positive signals cluster in deficient cohorts - for example 50,000 IU/week for 9 weeks in IBS-D improved severity, quality of life, depression scores and visceral sensitivity (p < 0.001 to p = 0.049) while leaving bloating, anxiety and serum serotonin unchanged - which leaves open whether what is being observed is correction of deficiency rather than an effect on IBS itself. None of these trials tested subtype-targeted use, and none reported a stool-form outcome that would separate IBS-C from IBS-D.

Doses studied: 3,000 IU/day for 12 weeks (the null UK community trial); 50,000 IU/week for 9 weeks in IBS-D; 50,000 IU fortnightly for 6 months; 2,000 IU/day for 6 months in deficient adolescents. The 2024 review describes the trial literature overall as running 6-26 weeks at doses from 3,000 IU daily to 50,000 IU bi-weekly, with seven of the study populations deficient (<20 ng/mL) and three insufficient (21-29 ng/mL) at baseline.

Why this grade: The human evidence is a handful of small trials - roughly 570-780 participants pooled across meta-analyses - most conducted in vitamin D-deficient or insufficient populations, several from the same research group, with extreme statistical heterogeneity (I-squared 91-93% in pooled analyses). Meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on the primary symptom outcome: two report a benefit, while a 2022 pooled analysis and the most recent 2024 review both find no significant effect on IBS symptom severity. The largest independent, community-recruited, placebo-controlled trial was flatly null and its authors concluded there is no case for advocating vitamin D for IBS symptoms. That pattern fits 'early' - preliminary human trials only, with direction of effect unresolved - and the lower grade is taken deliberately given the null best-quality trial.

For chronic pain

Low vitamin D and chronic aching often turn up together, so it is natural to think one causes the other. Researchers have now tested this properly several times, including in people who started out with low vitamin D levels, and topping up vitamin D did not reduce their knee or back pain any more than a dummy pill did. When those studies are averaged together some reviews do find a small edge for vitamin D, but even those reviewers say it is probably too small to notice. Getting your level checked and corrected still matters for your bones — but as something aimed at chronic pain itself, the evidence does not support it.

Safety

Vitamin D is not automatically benign at high or intermittent doses. In the 2-year knee osteoarthritis trial using 50,000 IU monthly, more participants in the vitamin D arm reported at least one adverse event than in placebo (56 vs 37, P=.04). In a separate 3-to-5-year randomised trial (Sanders et al. 2010), a single annual 500,000 IU oral dose in 2,256 community-dwelling women aged 70 or over increased falls (incidence rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02-1.30) and fractures (IRR 1.26, 95% CI 1.00-1.59), with the excess falls concentrated in the first 3 months after dosing. Excess intake can cause hypercalcaemia. Do not use large intermittent 'megadoses' to chase a pain benefit that trials have not found.

Who should be careful: Do not supplement without medical supervision if you have hypercalcaemia, primary hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis or other granulomatous disease, or kidney stones or significant kidney disease, all of which change how the body handles vitamin D and calcium. Avoid annual or very high intermittent bolus dosing.

Interactions: Thiazide diuretics plus vitamin D and calcium can raise blood calcium. Digoxin toxicity risk rises with hypercalcaemia. Absorption is reduced by orlistat, cholestyramine and other bile-acid sequestrants. Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital), rifampin and glucocorticoids increase vitamin D breakdown. If you take any of these, dosing should be decided by a clinician, not by a supplement label.

What the evidence says

Randomised evidence for vitamin D supplementation in chronic musculoskeletal pain is consistently null at the level of individual trials. In a 2-year multicentre trial of 413 patients with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis and low 25-hydroxyvitamin D (12.5-60 nmol/L), monthly 50,000 IU raised 25-hydroxyvitamin D by 40.6 nmol/L versus 6.7 nmol/L on placebo yet produced no significant difference in WOMAC pain or tibial cartilage volume, and more participants reported adverse events (56 vs 37, P=.04). An earlier 2-year trial (n=146) using 2,000 IU/day escalated to exceed 36 ng/mL found knee pain fell by -2.31 with vitamin D versus -1.46 with placebo with no significant difference at any time point, and identical cartilage volume loss (-4.30% vs -4.25%, P=.96). The VITAL sub-study in 1,398 adults with chronic knee pain found no effect over a mean 5.3 years (time-by-treatment interaction P=0.41). A systematic review of 8 low back pain trials found no benefit over placebo or no intervention (mean difference 1.29, 95% CI -3.81 to 6.39, from 4 pooled trials) at very low GRADE quality, and a meta-analysis of 3 trials in chronic nonspecific musculoskeletal pain found a pooled effect of essentially zero (SMD 0.004, 95% CI -0.248 to 0.256). Two pooled analyses do point the other way and are reported here for completeness: a 4-trial meta-analysis in knee osteoarthritis (1,130 patients) found a small significant pain effect (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.63 to -0.02) but no effect on cartilage volume or joint space width, and concluded the effect is probably not clinically significant; and a 4-trial meta-analysis in chronic widespread pain (287 subjects) found a lower pain VAS (MD 0.46, 95% CI 0.09-0.89) with no dose-response relationship on meta-regression.

Doses studied: 2,000 IU/day cholecalciferol with escalation to raise serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D above 36 ng/mL (2 years); 50,000 IU monthly (2 years); a range of daily and intermittent regimens across the low back pain, chronic musculoskeletal pain and knee osteoarthritis reviews.

Why this grade: This is one of the better-tested questions in the field, and the individual trials have been consistently negative. Two independent 2-year JAMA randomised trials in symptomatic knee osteoarthritis — one of which deliberately enrolled only people with low baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D — found no effect on pain or cartilage volume. The VITAL trial found nothing over a mean 5.3 years in adults with chronic knee pain. Systematic reviews in low back pain and in chronic nonspecific musculoskeletal pain both pooled to essentially zero. The evidence is not uniformly negative and should not be presented that way: meta-analyses that pool the knee osteoarthritis trials do report a small statistically significant pain benefit (for example SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.63 to -0.02), and a 4-trial meta-analysis in chronic widespread pain/fibromyalgia was also positive. But those pooled estimates are built largely from the same trials that were individually null, the knee osteoarthritis reviewers themselves concluded the effect is probably not clinically significant, and the fibromyalgia meta-regression found no relationship between the rise in vitamin D level and the change in pain, which undercuts a causal reading. On balance, and applying the conservative rule, the grade is not_supported. Note that every trial cited here tested supplementation for pain; none tested treatment of diagnosed osteomalacia or severe deficiency, which is a separate clinical question for a clinician.
