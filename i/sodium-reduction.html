Dietary sodium reduction

Strong evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Eating less salt is one of the few changes that reliably moves blood pressure, and it helps most in people whose pressure is already high. Cutting about a teaspoon of salt a day typically drops the top number by 4 to 6 points if you have high blood pressure, but only about 1 to 2 points if your blood pressure is normal, and the gain gets smaller once your intake is already moderate. A very large trial in rural China found that swapping regular salt for a potassium-based substitute reduced strokes and deaths - though simply being told to eat less salt, on its own, has not been shown in trials to make people live longer.

Safety

Reducing sodium is itself well tolerated; the 2023 crossover trial reported only mild adverse events, in about 10% of participants on each diet. The documented physiological cost, quantified in the Graudal Cochrane review, is small increases in renin, aldosterone and noradrenaline plus small increases in cholesterol and triglycerides - findings the He Cochrane review did not reproduce for lipids at all, so the size and even the existence of that cost is disputed between research groups. Potassium-based salt substitutes are a separate risk question: SSaSS found no significant increase in serious hyperkalaemia events (3.35 vs 3.30 per 1000 person-years), but that trial was conducted in a general rural population, not in people with kidney disease. Most dietary sodium comes from packaged and restaurant food rather than the salt shaker, which is why kitchen-level and product-level changes have outperformed individual advice in trials.

Who should be careful: Potassium-containing salt substitutes should not be used without medical advice by anyone with chronic kidney disease, adrenal insufficiency, or a history of high blood potassium. Aggressive sodium restriction is not appropriate in salt-wasting kidney or adrenal conditions, in some forms of orthostatic hypotension, or for people with very high sweat losses (endurance athletes, hot-environment workers) without individualized guidance.

Interactions: Potassium-based salt substitutes can raise potassium dangerously in people taking ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, amiloride, eplerenone) or potassium supplements. Sodium intake alters lithium clearance, so any substantial change in salt intake requires monitoring in people on lithium. Sodium reduction adds to the effect of blood pressure medication, so dose adjustments should be handled by the prescriber.

What the evidence says

A Cochrane meta-analysis of 34 trials lasting at least 4 weeks (3,230 participants) found that reducing 24-hour urinary sodium by 75 mmol/day (about 4.4 g/day of salt) lowered systolic BP by 4.18 mmHg (95% CI -5.18 to -3.18) and diastolic by 2.06 mmHg; in hypertensive participants the effect was -5.39/-2.82 mmHg, in normotensive participants only -2.42/-1.00 mmHg. A separate Cochrane review by authors sceptical of population targets reached similar conclusions in white participants with hypertension (systolic -5.71 mmHg, 95% CI -6.67 to -4.74) but found only -1.14 mmHg systolic and no diastolic change in white participants with normal blood pressure, with larger but lower-certainty estimates in black (-6.64 mmHg) and Asian (-7.75 mmHg) hypertensive participants. The two reviews disagree on side effects as a matter of fact, not just interpretation: the sceptical review documented rises in renin (+1.56 ng/mL/hour), aldosterone (+104 pg/mL), noradrenaline (+62.3 pg/mL), cholesterol (+5.19 mg/dL) and triglycerides (+7.10 mg/dL), while the other found no significant change in lipids at all (cholesterol 0.05 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.11). A 2023 crossover trial in 213 adults aged 50-75 found median systolic pressure of 126 mmHg on a high-sodium and 119 mmHg on a low-sodium week, a median within-person mean arterial pressure change of 4 mmHg (IQR 0-8), and an 8 mmHg systolic difference between diet orders (95% CI 4-11), consistent across those on and off antihypertensive medication. Hard-outcome evidence is weaker and mixed: a 2014 Cochrane review of 8 trials found no all-cause mortality benefit from salt-reduction advice in hypertensives (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.86-1.15) and concluded the findings do not support individual dietary advice, its pooled cardiovascular benefit (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63-0.95) being driven by a single retirement-home trial that changed kitchen practice; observational follow-up of TOHP I and II reported a 25% lower cardiovascular event rate (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.57-0.99); and SSaSS found that 20,995 rural Chinese adults using a 75% sodium chloride / 25% potassium chloride substitute had lower rates of stroke (RR 0.86), major cardiovascular events (RR 0.87) and death (RR 0.88) over a mean 4.74 years. Observational data suggest the benefit is concentrated at high intakes: across 102,216 adults in 18 countries the association between estimated sodium excretion and systolic pressure was 2.58 mmHg per gram above 5 g/day but only 0.74 mmHg per gram below 3 g/day (P<0.001 for interaction).

Doses studied: Cochrane meta-analysis trials reduced 24-hour urinary sodium by a mean of 75 mmol/day, equivalent to about 4.4 g/day less salt; the Graudal review compared roughly 203 mmol/day with 65 mmol/day. The 2023 crossover trial compared approximately 500 mg total sodium per day with the participant's usual diet plus 2,200 mg added sodium. SSaSS used a 75% sodium chloride / 25% potassium chloride salt substitute in place of regular salt for household cooking. Public-health targets in the He 2013 review discuss moving from 9-12 g/day of salt to 5-6 g/day, with 3 g/day proposed as a longer-term target.

Why this grade: Two independent Cochrane reviews with opposing authorial stances agree that reducing sodium lowers systolic pressure by roughly 4-6 mmHg in people with hypertension, across dozens of randomized trials, and sodium restriction is a first-line non-drug recommendation. A large cluster-randomized trial (SSaSS, 20,995 participants) adds hard-outcome evidence, with lower rates of stroke, major cardiovascular events and death using a potassium-based salt substitute. The grade is qualified rather than absolute: the effect in people with normal blood pressure is small (about 1.1-2.4 mmHg), a 2014 Cochrane review found individual dietary advice alone was underpowered to show any mortality benefit and explicitly did not support it, large observational data show the pressure-sodium relationship is nonlinear and much flatter below about 3 g/day of sodium, and the two Cochrane teams openly disagree about whether sodium restriction raises cholesterol and triglycerides.

Sources (7)
  1. Effect of longer-term modest salt reduction on blood pressure.
    Meta-analysisHe FJ, Li J, MacGregor GA, 2013 (Cochrane) · PMID 23633321 · positive

    Across 34 trials (3,230 participants) of at least 4 weeks, a mean 75 mmol/24h reduction in urinary sodium lowered systolic BP 4.18 mmHg (95% CI -5.18 to -3.18) and diastolic 2.06 mmHg (95% CI -2.67 to -1.45); effects were -5.39/-2.82 mmHg in hypertensive and -2.42/-1.00 mmHg in normotensive participants. The same review found a small rise in renin, aldosterone and noradrenaline but no significant change in cholesterol (0.05 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.11), LDL, HDL or triglycerides.

  2. Effects of low sodium diet versus high sodium diet on blood pressure, renin, aldosterone, catecholamines, cholesterol, and triglyceride.
    Meta-analysisGraudal NA, Hubeck-Graudal T, Jurgens G, 2020 (Cochrane) · PMID 33314019 · mixed

    In white participants with normal blood pressure, cutting sodium from 203 to 65 mmol/day lowered systolic BP only 1.14 mmHg (95% CI -1.65 to -0.63) with no diastolic change, versus -5.71/-2.87 mmHg in white participants with hypertension (with lower-certainty estimates of -6.64 mmHg systolic in black and -7.75 mmHg in Asian hypertensive participants); sodium reduction raised renin (+1.56 ng/mL/hour), aldosterone (+104 pg/mL), noradrenaline (+62.3 pg/mL), cholesterol (+5.19 mg/dL) and triglycerides (+7.10 mg/dL).

  3. Effect of Dietary Sodium on Blood Pressure: A Crossover Trial.
    Clinical trialGupta DK, Lewis CE, Varady KA, et al., 2023 · PMID 37950918 · positive

    In 213 adults aged 50-75, median systolic BP was 126 mmHg on a high-sodium and 119 mmHg on a low-sodium week; median within-individual mean arterial pressure change was 4 mmHg (IQR 0-8), and the between-group systolic difference after week one was 8 mmHg (95% CI 4-11), similar regardless of hypertension status or medication use, with mild adverse events in about 10% on each diet.

  4. Reduced dietary salt for the prevention of cardiovascular disease.
    Systematic reviewAdler AJ, Taylor F, Martin N, et al., 2014 (Cochrane) · PMID 25519688 · null

    Across 8 randomized trials, salt-reduction advice showed no all-cause mortality benefit in hypertensives (end-of-trial RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.86-1.15) and only imprecise, non-significant estimates in normotensives; the pooled cardiovascular event benefit (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63-0.95, n=5,912) was driven by a single retirement-home trial that changed kitchen practice rather than individual behaviour, and the authors concluded the findings do not support individual dietary advice as a means of restricting salt intake.

  5. Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death.
    Randomised trialNeal B, Wu Y, Feng X, et al., 2021 (SSaSS) · PMID 34459569 · positive

    Among 20,995 rural Chinese adults with stroke history or aged 60+ with high blood pressure, a 75% NaCl / 25% KCl salt substitute reduced stroke (29.14 vs 33.65 per 1000 person-years; RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77-0.96), major cardiovascular events (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80-0.94) and death (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.82-0.95) over a mean 4.74 years, with no significant excess of hyperkalaemia serious adverse events (3.35 vs 3.30 per 1000 person-years; RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.80-1.37).

  6. Long term effects of dietary sodium reduction on cardiovascular disease outcomes: observational follow-up of the trials of hypertension prevention (TOHP).
    Meta-analysisCook NR, Cutler JA, Obarzanek E, et al., 2007 · PMID 17449506 · positive

    Ten to 15 years after TOHP I and II, participants originally randomized to sodium reduction (net reductions of 44 and 33 mmol/24h) had a 25% lower risk of a cardiovascular event (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.57-0.99, P=0.04); the mortality difference was not significant (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.51-1.26, P=0.34). This was observational follow-up after the trials ended, not randomized comparison.

  7. Association of urinary sodium and potassium excretion with blood pressure.
    Journal articleMente A, O'Donnell MJ, Rangarajan S, et al., 2014 (PURE) · PMID 25119606 · mixed

    Across 102,216 adults in 18 countries, each 1-g higher estimated sodium excretion was associated with 2.11 mmHg higher systolic BP, but the relationship was nonlinear: 2.58 mmHg per gram above 5 g/day, 1.74 mmHg per gram at 3-5 g/day, and only 0.74 mmHg per gram below 3 g/day (P<0.001 for interaction), implying diminishing returns from cutting further once intake is already moderate. This is observational, with a single fasting morning urine specimen used as a surrogate for intake.
