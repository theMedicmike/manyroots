Herbs

Ziziphus jujuba seed (suan zao ren)

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Sour jujube seed is one of the oldest and most widely used sleep herbs in Chinese medicine, and there is a real body of trial literature behind the classical formula it anchors. But the seed on its own has only been tested against a dummy pill in one 12-person study, where just one of several sleep measures came out ahead. A bigger trial of a supplement containing it found no benefit over placebo on its main measure, and independent reviewers have repeatedly judged the wider Chinese-formula research too poorly conducted to draw firm conclusions from.

Safety

Ziziphus seed was well tolerated at 2 g/day for 4 weeks with only minor adverse events in the one placebo-controlled single-herb trial, and the classical formula produced only mild adverse events (lethargy, diarrhoea, dizziness) in a 90-person trial. But the safety record is thinner than the volume of literature suggests: a systematic review found only 3 of 12 Suanzaoren decoction trials reported adverse events at all and concluded the evidence is insufficient to draw safety conclusions. In the 171-person complex trial, two of four treatment-related adverse events led to permanent discontinuation. Important product-identification note: sour jujube SEED (suan zao ren, from Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa) is a different preparation from jujube FRUIT (da zao) sold as a dried food - doses and evidence do not transfer between them, and several trials use 'sour date' as the name for the seed preparation. Safety in pregnancy, breastfeeding and children has not been established.

Who should be careful: Not established as safe in pregnancy, lactation or children. Caution if daytime lethargy would be hazardous, since lethargy was among the reported adverse events in the formula trial.

Interactions: Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids, sedating antihistamines and other CNS depressants. Because most trials tested the multi-herb Suan Zao Ren Tang formula rather than the isolated seed, and because most of those trials did not report adverse events at all, interaction data specific to the single herb are essentially absent rather than reassuring. Note also that the largest trial involving sour date used a product that also contained hops, so its tolerability data do not describe the seed alone.

What the evidence says

The only placebo-controlled trial of the seeds alone is a randomised, double-blind, crossover feasibility study in 12 people with insomnia given 2 g/day of encapsulated Ziziphus spinosa granules for four-week periods: PSQI was significantly better on the herb than placebo (t = -2.276, df = 10, 95% CI -3.3 to -0.04, p = 0.046) and sleep diaries showed improvement in total sleep time, sleep efficiency and sleep onset latency after the herb but not placebo, while the Insomnia Severity Index and all other outcomes showed no significant difference, period effects were present, and the study was powered for feasibility rather than efficacy (Shergis 2020). Working against this, a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 171 randomised adults with mild insomnia tested LZComplex3 - lactium, sour date and hops plus magnesium and vitamin B6 - for 2 weeks after a 7-day single-blind placebo run-in and found PSQI improved in both arms with a significant effect of day (F = 30.40, p < 0.001) but no effect of treatment (F = 0.14, p = 0.713); the only positive signals were a between-group difference in ISI category change at day 3 and a stress-reactivity difference, with most secondary outcomes improving similarly in both groups (Scholey 2017). A 2015 meta-analysis of 14 insomnia RCTs and 1602 participants found that the only herbal monopreparations with usable evidence were valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling - Ziziphus was not among them - and that no herbal medicine beat placebo or active control on any of thirteen efficacy measures. At the formula level, a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial of Suan Zao Ren Tang in 90 methadone-maintained adults with PSQI > 5 found significant improvement in mean total PSQI (P = 0.007) and sleep efficiency (P = 0.017) after 4 weeks, with mild adverse events, and a meta-analysis of 12 RCTs and 1311 chronic-insomnia patients reported an improved clinical effective rate (RR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.16-1.29, p < 0.00001) and lower PSQI (decoction alone MD -1.94, 95% CI -3.80 to -0.07, p = 0.04). But a systematic review of 12 Suanzaoren decoction RCTs found every included trial scored no more than 3 of 8 on methodological quality with only 3 of 12 reporting adverse events, and a broader review of 217 Chinese herbal medicine insomnia RCTs found only 8 with a Jadad score of 3 or more, with meta-analysis of those finding Chinese herbal medicine similar to placebo - while noting Suan Zao Ren was the single most frequently used herb across the whole literature.

Doses studied: Single herb: 2 g/day of encapsulated Ziziphus spinosa granules for 4 weeks, the only placebo-controlled single-herb dose tested (Shergis 2020, n = 12). Formula-level trials used the classical Suan Zao Ren Tang decoction for 4 weeks (Chan 2015). No dose has been established in an adequately powered efficacy trial of the seed itself.

Why this grade: Direct placebo-controlled evidence for the single herb consists of one crossover feasibility trial in 12 participants, in which only one of several sleep outcomes reached significance and period effects were present. A much larger placebo-controlled trial (n = 171) of a complex containing sour date was null on its primary sleep outcome, and a 2015 meta-analysis of 14 insomnia RCTs did not find Ziziphus among the herbal monopreparations with usable trial evidence at all. Trials of the classical Suan Zao Ren multi-herb formula are more numerous and mostly positive, but that formula is a different intervention from the isolated seed, and two independent systematic reviews judged the underlying trials to be of poor methodological quality, with meta-analysis of the higher-quality studies finding Chinese herbal medicine similar to placebo. One tiny positive single-herb trial set against substantial null and low-quality evidence is 'early', not 'moderate'.

Sources (7)
  1. Medicinal seeds Ziziphus spinosa for insomnia: A randomized, placebo-controlled, cross-over, feasibility clinical trial.
    Shergis JL, Hyde A, Meaklim H, Varma P, Da Costa C, Jackson ML, 2020 · PMID 33385511 · mixed

    Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled crossover feasibility trial in 12 people with insomnia (2 g/day encapsulated Ziziphus spinosa granules, four weeks per period, four-week washout): PSQI was significantly better on the herb than placebo (t = -2.276, df = 10, 95% CI -3.3 to -0.04, p = 0.046), and sleep diaries showed improvement in total sleep time, sleep efficiency and sleep onset latency after the herb but not placebo. All other outcomes - Insomnia Severity Index, quality of life, mood, functional impairment - showed no statistically significant difference between treatments, and period effects were present. Well tolerated with only minor adverse events.

  2. Exploring the Effect of Lactium™ and Zizyphus Complex on Sleep Quality: A Double-Blind, Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Scholey A, Benson S, Gibbs A, Perry N, Sarris J, Murray G, 2017 · PMID 28218661 · null

    Double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 171 randomised adults with mild insomnia (160 modified intention-to-treat) of LZComplex3, which the full text states contains lactium, sour date and hops plus magnesium and vitamin B6, for 2 weeks after a 7-day single-blind placebo run-in. Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index improved in both arms with a significant effect of day (F = 30.40, p < 0.001) but no effect of treatment on the primary outcome (F = 0.14, p = 0.713) and no treatment-by-day interaction (F = 1.13, p = 0.340). Most secondary outcomes improved similarly in both groups; the exceptions were a between-group difference in Insomnia Severity Index category change at day 3 and a stress-reactivity difference. Two of four treatment-related adverse events in the active group led to permanent discontinuation.

  3. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Leach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · negative

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomised controlled trials involving 1602 participants with insomnia. Only four orally administered herbal monopreparations had usable trial evidence - valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling - and Ziziphus was not among them. There was no statistically significant difference between any herbal medicine and placebo, or any herbal medicine and active control, on any of thirteen measures of clinical efficacy, and the authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to support the use of herbal medicine for insomnia.

  4. Chinese herbal medicine for insomnia: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials.
    Yeung WF, Chung KF, Poon MM, et al., 2012 · PMID 22440393 · null

    Systematic review of 217 randomised controlled trials of Chinese herbal medicine for insomnia found only 8 with a Jadad score of 3 or more, and 7 of those 8 had at least one domain at high risk of bias. Meta-analyses of the higher-quality studies found Chinese herbal medicine similar to Western medication (3 studies) and similar to placebo (3 studies); the authors concluded current evidence is insufficient to support efficacy. Suan Zao Ren (Ziziphus jujuba) was the single most frequently used herb across the literature reviewed.

  5. Efficacy and safety of Suanzaoren decoction for primary insomnia: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials.
    Xie CL, Gu Y, Wang WW, et al., 2013 · PMID 23336848 · null

    Systematic review of 12 RCTs enrolling 1376 adults: the methodological quality of every included trial scored no more than 3 of 8, with insufficient randomisation detail, absent allocation concealment, lack of blinding, no placebo control, no intention-to-treat analysis, no follow-up and small samples. Only 3 of 12 trials reported adverse events. Despite apparently positive reported findings, the authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to support efficacy of Suanzaoren decoction for insomnia and insufficient data to draw conclusions on safety.

  6. Clinical Efficacy of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Suan Zao Ren Tang, for Sleep Disturbance during Methadone Maintenance: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Chan YY, Chen YH, Yang SN, Lo WY, Lin JG, 2015 · PMID 26346534 · positive

    Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 90 methadone-maintained adults with sleep disturbance and PSQI > 5 (45 per arm, all analysed): 4 weeks of Suan Zao Ren Tang produced statistically significant improvement in mean total PSQI (P = 0.007) and average sleep efficiency (P = 0.017) versus placebo. Adverse events - lethargy, diarrhoea and dizziness - were all mild in severity. This tested the multi-herb classical formula, not Ziziphus seed alone.

  7. Suanzaoren Decoction for the treatment of chronic insomnia: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Liu XX, Ma YQ, Wang YG, Zhong FX, Yin XP, Zhang QM, 2022 · PMID 36459033 · positive

    Meta-analysis of 12 RCTs with 1311 chronic-insomnia patients: Suanzaoren decoction alone or combined with Western medicine improved the clinical effective rate (RR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.16-1.29, p < 0.00001), reduced the recurrence rate (RR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.28-0.80, p = 0.005), lowered PSQI scores (decoction plus Western medicine MD -3.35, 95% CI -5.22 to -1.47; decoction alone MD -1.94, 95% CI -3.80 to -0.07, p = 0.04) and reduced the adverse-effect rate (RR = 0.30, 95% CI 0.22-0.40, p < 0.00001). The authors state that included studies varied in quality and that larger, higher-quality, multi-centre RCTs are still needed.
