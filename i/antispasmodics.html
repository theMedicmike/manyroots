Conventional medicine

Antispasmodics (hyoscine, dicyclomine, otilonium, pinaverium)

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Antispasmodics are muscle-relaxing pills meant to calm the cramping and squeezing in the gut that drives IBS pain. Across many older studies they helped a bit more than a dummy pill — roughly one extra person in five to seven felt better — but most of those studies were small and not very rigorously run, and the benefit is not spread evenly across the class: mebeverine, one of the most commonly prescribed of these drugs in the UK and Europe, was no better than placebo when its own trials were pooled. They are worth a trial for pain and cramping, especially taken before meals, but expect a modest effect rather than a fix, and know that the drying, constipating side effects can make constipation-type IBS worse.

Safety

The characteristic side effects of the anticholinergic agents are dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary hesitancy, tachycardia and drowsiness or confusion; in the main dicyclomine trial the majority of reported adverse effects were attributable to the drug's anticholinergic activity, at a dose of 160 mg/day (PMID 7016973). At ordinary doses tolerability is closer to placebo: adverse effects occurred in 5% of patients with no difference between arms in a 712-patient hyoscine trial (PMID 2210587), and adverse event frequency did not differ from placebo in the otilonium dose-ranging study (PMID 25232263). Otilonium and pinaverium are poorly absorbed from the gut but are not marketed in the United States. Long-term safety is reassuring at the population level: in a 1:1 propensity score-matched cohort of 669,083 US adults with IBS drawn from a nationwide electronic health record database, antispasmodic use was not associated with increased all-cause mortality (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.89-1.00) (Mehravar 2026, PMID 41951879) — observational data, but the largest long-term safety dataset available in this population. Because these drugs act on symptoms and not on any disease process, most clinicians use them intermittently or before meals rather than continuously. Coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease should be excluded before settling on an IBS diagnosis; the 2021 ACG guideline (PMID 33315591) suggests coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin in anyone with IBS-type symptoms plus diarrhoea.

Who should be careful: The anticholinergic antispasmodics (hyoscine/scopolamine butylbromide, hyoscyamine, dicyclomine) should not be used in narrow-angle glaucoma, myasthenia gravis, obstructive uropathy or significant prostatic enlargement, mechanical gastrointestinal obstruction or paralytic ileus, or in a severe ulcerative colitis flare because of toxic megacolon risk. They are generally a poor choice in constipation-predominant IBS, where the constipating effect can worsen the dominant symptom. Caution in older adults, in whom confusion, falls and urinary retention are more likely.

Interactions: Effects are additive with any other anticholinergic load: sedating antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, some antipsychotics, bladder antimuscarinics, and certain Parkinson's medicines. Combining an anticholinergic antispasmodic with low-dose amitriptyline meaningfully increases dry mouth, constipation and cognitive side effects. Slowed gastric emptying can alter the absorption of other oral drugs.

What the evidence says

The Cochrane review of 56 RCTs (3,725 patients), which included 29 antispasmodic trials in 2,333 patients, found antispasmodics improved abdominal pain in 58% versus 46% on placebo (13 studies, 1,392 patients; RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.12-1.55; NNT 7), global assessment in 57% versus 39% (22 studies, 1,983 patients; RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.25-1.77; NNT 5) and symptom score in 37% versus 22% (4 studies, 586 patients; RR 1.86, 95% CI 1.26-2.76; NNT 3), with significant subgroups for cimetropium/dicyclomine, peppermint oil, pinaverium and trimebutine. A 2008 meta-analysis of 22 trials (1,778 patients) gave a relative risk of persistent symptoms of 0.68 (0.57-0.81), with consistent evidence only for otilonium (0.55, 0.31-0.97) and hyoscine (0.63, 0.51-0.78). The class is not uniform: a meta-analysis of eight mebeverine trials (555 patients) found no significant benefit for clinical improvement (RR 1.13, 0.59-2.16, P=0.7056) or abdominal pain (RR 1.33, 0.92-1.93, P=0.129). Quality is the other weak point — in a network meta-analysis of 51 RCTs (4,644 patients) only 13 were at low risk of bias, no active treatment was significantly superior to another on direct or indirect comparison, and peppermint oil (RR 0.63, 0.48-0.83) and tricyclic antidepressants (RR 0.66, 0.53-0.83), not any antispasmodic, ranked first and second for global symptoms. Individual trials are inconsistent: a 93-patient otilonium dose-ranging RCT found no significant between-group differences on its primary efficacy variables of intensity and frequency of abdominal discomfort, bloating or pain, though 40 and 80 mg beat placebo on a combined global discomfort index and on return to regular bowel habit; and in a 356-patient otilonium trial the primary endpoint was met only narrowly (-0.90 vs -0.65 weekly pain episodes, p=0.03) while pain intensity, responder proportions, safety and quality-of-life scores did not differ from placebo. The placebo response is large — 64% of placebo patients were responders in a 712-patient hyoscine trial in which 76% responded to hyoscine. The AGA rates antispasmodics as a conditional recommendation with low certainty in both IBS-D and IBS-C. Most agents with the best trial data (otilonium, pinaverium, trimebutine, cimetropium) are not marketed in the United States, where prescribing defaults to dicyclomine and hyoscyamine; the principal dicyclomine trial ran 2 weeks at 160 mg/day, well above the doses ordinarily used.

Doses studied: Otilonium bromide 40 mg three times daily for 15 weeks (Clave 2011) and 20, 40 or 80 mg three times daily for 4 weeks in a dose-ranging study (Chmielewska-Wilkon 2014). Hyoscine-N-butylbromide 30 mg daily for 4 weeks (Schafer 1990). Dicyclomine 40 mg four times daily — 160 mg/day — for 2 weeks (Page 1981), several times what is ordinarily prescribed today. The pooled analyses did not report a single common dose across the class. In practice these agents are taken before meals or as needed rather than continuously.

Why this grade: Two large pooled analyses show a real but modest benefit over placebo for abdominal pain and global symptoms, so this is more than preliminary evidence. It falls well short of "strong": the underlying trials are mostly old and at high risk of bias, a network meta-analysis found no antispasmodic significantly superior to any other treatment and ranked peppermint oil and tricyclics above them, one dose-ranging RCT was null on its primary efficacy variables, a separate meta-analysis of mebeverine — one of the most widely prescribed agents in this class — found no significant benefit at all, and both AGA guideline panels rate the certainty of evidence as low with only a conditional recommendation. The grade describes the class average; individual agents range from reasonably supported (otilonium, hyoscine) to unsupported (mebeverine).

Sources (9)
  1. Bulking agents, antispasmodics and antidepressants for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome.
    Ruepert L, Quartero AO, de Wit NJ, et al., 2011 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews) · PMID 21833945 · positive

    The review covered 56 RCTs (3,725 patients), including 29 antispasmodic trials (2,333 patients). Abdominal pain improved in 58% on antispasmodics versus 46% on placebo (13 studies, 1,392 patients; RR 1.32, 95% CI 1.12-1.55, P<0.001; NNT 7); global assessment improved in 57% versus 39% (22 studies, 1,983 patients; RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.25-1.77, P<0.0001; NNT 5); symptom score improved in 37% versus 22% (4 studies, 586 patients; RR 1.86, 95% CI 1.26-2.76, P<0.01; NNT 3). Significant subgroups were cimetropium/dicyclomine, peppermint oil, pinaverium and trimebutine. The same review found no benefit at all for bulking agents on any outcome. Selection bias was unclear for many included studies because randomisation and allocation-concealment methods were not described, and adverse events were not assessed as an outcome.

  2. Effect of fibre, antispasmodics, and peppermint oil in the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome: systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Ford AC, Talley NJ, Spiegel BMR, et al., 2008 (BMJ) · PMID 19008265 · positive

    22 trials of antispasmodics in 1,778 patients gave a relative risk of persistent symptoms of 0.68 (95% CI 0.57-0.81). Consistent evidence of efficacy was seen only for otilonium (4 trials, 435 patients, RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.31-0.97) and hyoscine (3 trials, 426 patients, RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.51-0.78); the class effect is driven by a handful of agents rather than being uniform. In the same review, peppermint oil (4 trials, 392 patients, RR 0.43, 0.32-0.59) outperformed the antispasmodics.

  3. A systematic review of efficacy and tolerability of mebeverine in irritable bowel syndrome.
    Darvish-Damavandi M, Nikfar S, Abdollahi M, 2010 (World Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 20128021 · null

    Eight randomised trials including 555 patients (six comparing mebeverine with placebo) gave a pooled relative risk for clinical improvement of 1.13 (95% CI 0.59-2.16, P=0.7056) and for relief of abdominal pain of 1.33 (95% CI 0.92-1.93, P=0.129) — neither statistically significant. Mebeverine 200 mg was no better than 135 mg for global improvement (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.96-1.30, P=0.168) or abdominal pain (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.87-1.34, P=0.463). The authors conclude that mebeverine is mostly well tolerated with no significant adverse effects, but that its efficacy in global improvement of IBS is not statistically significant. This is a widely prescribed member of the class with no demonstrated benefit over placebo.

  4. Efficacy of soluble fibre, antispasmodic drugs, and gut-brain neuromodulators in irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Black CJ, Yuan Y, Selinger CP, et al., 2020 (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology) · PMID 31859183 · mixed

    Of 51 RCTs (4,644 patients), only 13 were at low risk of bias. For failure to improve global IBS symptoms there were no significant differences between any active treatments on direct or indirect comparison; peppermint oil ranked first (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.48-0.83, P-score 0.84) and tricyclic antidepressants second (RR 0.66, 0.53-0.83, P-score 0.77) — no antispasmodic topped the ranking. For abdominal pain, tricyclics again ranked first (RR 0.53, 0.34-0.83) though on only 4 RCTs and 92 patients, and no active treatment was superior on indirect comparison. Tricyclics were more likely than placebo to cause adverse events (RR 1.59, 1.26-2.06). The authors concluded there is likely considerable uncertainty around the findings because of poor methodological rigour, and that long-term efficacy beyond 4-12 weeks is unknown.

  5. Otilonium bromide in irritable bowel syndrome: a dose-ranging randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial.
    Chmielewska-Wilkon D, Reggiardo G, Egan CG, 2014 (World Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 25232263 · mixed

    In 93 patients randomised to otilonium 20, 40 or 80 mg three times daily or placebo for 4 weeks, the primary efficacy variables — intensity and frequency of abdominal discomfort, bloating and pain — were reduced over time but showed no significant differences between groups, and there was no difference for mucus in stool or incomplete/difficult evacuation. Secondary results did favour otilonium: evacuation frequency fell more on 80 mg than placebo (-41.9% vs -8.36%, P<0.01), return to regular bowel habit was greater on 40 mg than the 21.7% seen on placebo (P<0.01), diarrhoea frequency fell dose-dependently (P<0.001), and a combined global discomfort index favoured 40 mg (P=0.013) and 80 mg (P=0.001). Adverse event frequency did not differ from placebo. A null primary endpoint with positive secondary endpoints.

  6. Randomised clinical trial: otilonium bromide improves frequency of abdominal pain, severity of distention and time to relapse in patients with irritable bowel syndrome.
    Clave P, Acalovschi M, Triantafillidis JK, et al., 2011 (Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics) · PMID 21679214 · mixed

    In 356 patients over 15 weeks, otilonium 40 mg three times daily beat placebo on the primary endpoint of weekly frequency of abdominal pain episodes, but only narrowly (-0.90 +/- 0.88 vs -0.65 +/- 0.91, p=0.03), and also on abdominal bloating (-1.2 vs -0.9, p=0.02) and patient-assessed global efficacy (1.3 vs 1.0, p=0.047). Intensity of abdominal pain, the proportion of patient responders, safety and quality-of-life scores were similarly affected by drug and placebo. During follow-up, withdrawal due to symptom relapse was lower on otilonium (10% vs 27%, p=0.009). Both arms improved substantially, illustrating the large placebo response in IBS trials.

  7. [The treatment of irritable colon. Efficacy and tolerance of buscopan plus, buscopan, paracetamol and placebo in ambulatory patients with irritable colon].
    Schafer E, Ewe K, 1990 (Fortschritte der Medizin; German, English abstract) · PMID 2210587 · positive

    In 712 patients treated for 4 weeks, responders (marked or some improvement) were 76% on hyoscine-N-butylbromide 30 mg/day and 81% on hyoscine plus paracetamol, versus 72% on paracetamol alone and 64% on placebo; both hyoscine-containing arms were significantly better than placebo. The absolute gain over placebo was about 12 percentage points against a 64% placebo response rate. Adverse effects occurred in 38 patients (5%) with no difference between treatment groups, and none required treatment. The first author's stated affiliation is the manufacturer, Boehringer Ingelheim.

  8. Treatment of the irritable bowel syndrome with Bentyl (dicyclomine hydrochloride).
    Page JG, Dirnberger GM, 1981 (Journal of Clinical Gastroenterology) · PMID 7016973 · positive

    The principal randomised placebo-controlled trial of dicyclomine — the antispasmodic most used in the United States — ran only 2 weeks and used 40 mg four times daily (160 mg/day), several times the doses ordinarily prescribed. Over that period dicyclomine was superior to placebo on physicians' global evaluation, patients' self-evaluation, abdominal pain, abdominal tenderness and bowel habits. The authors report that the majority of adverse effects were related to the anticholinergic activity of the drug. The short duration, very high dose and 1981 vintage are why US practice rests on thinner evidence than the otilonium and pinaverium data.

  9. AGA Clinical Practice Guideline on the Pharmacological Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome With Diarrhea.
    Lembo A, Sultan S, Chang L, et al., 2022 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 35738725 · mixed

    Using GRADE methodology, the AGA panel made only a conditional recommendation for antispasmodics in IBS-D, rating the certainty of evidence as low. The companion IBS-C guideline (Chang 2022, PMID 35738724) likewise makes a conditional recommendation for antispasmodics at low certainty, placing them below the secretagogues in that setting.
