Linaclotide and other secretagogues for IBS-C

Strong evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Linaclotide is a capsule that pulls fluid into the bowel and quietens pain-signalling nerves, and it is the best-evidenced prescription option for IBS when constipation is the problem. In large trials, roughly a third of people met a strict standard of getting both less pain and more complete bowel movements, versus about 14 to 21 in 100 on a dummy capsule — so it helps a substantial minority, not everyone. The main downside is the obvious one: it can cause diarrhoea, which led about one in twenty people to stop taking it, and it is not for people whose IBS runs to diarrhoea, where it would make things worse.

Safety

Diarrhoea is the predictable and dominant adverse effect — it is the drug working too well — and it is the reason 4-6% of people stop; pooled across the pivotal trials the relative risk of diarrhoea leading to discontinuation was 14.75 (95% CI 4.04-53.81), rated high-quality evidence (Atluri 2014, PMID 24351035). Taking the capsule on an empty stomach at least 30 minutes before the first meal reduces the risk relative to taking it with food. Most diarrhoea events in the pooled analysis were mild or moderate and there were no serious adverse events of diarrhoea across 3,853 patients treated up to 104 weeks. In a propensity-matched cohort of 669,083 US adults with IBS, secretagogues were not associated with increased all-cause mortality (Mehravar 2026, PMID 41951879) — observational data, but the largest long-term signal available. Other secretagogues differ in their tolerability profile: tenapanor has the highest absolute discontinuation risk of this class, and lubiprostone commonly causes nausea rather than diarrhoea. The trial populations were about 90% female and roughly three-quarters white, so how well the numbers transfer to other groups is not established. Roughly two-thirds of patients on linaclotide still do not meet the strict composite responder definition, so it is a good bet rather than a certainty, and a trial period with a clear stopping rule is reasonable.

Who should be careful: US prescribing information contraindicates linaclotide in children under 2 years, because of deaths from severe dehydration in juvenile animal studies, and in known or suspected mechanical gastrointestinal obstruction. It is for constipation-predominant IBS only: in diarrhoea-predominant IBS it would be expected to worsen the dominant symptom by its intended mechanism, and it has not been studied for that indication. Stop and seek advice for severe diarrhoea, and hold the dose if severe diarrhoea occurs.

Interactions: Linaclotide acts locally in the gut lumen and is minimally absorbed, so systemic drug interactions are not a significant concern. The practical interaction is pharmacodynamic: combining it with laxatives, magnesium, other secretagogues or prokinetics can cause additive diarrhoea, and severe diarrhoea can in turn affect the absorption or effect of other oral medicines, including hormonal contraceptives and narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.

What the evidence says

Two pivotal phase 3 trials establish the effect. In a 26-week trial of 804 patients, with efficacy assessed over the first 12 weeks, 33.7% of those on linaclotide 290 micrograms daily met the FDA composite responder endpoint versus 13.9% on placebo (p<0.0001; NNT 5.1, 95% CI 3.9-7.1), with the pain component met by 48.9% versus 34.5% and the bowel-movement component by 47.6% versus 22.6%; significant differences from placebo persisted across the full 26 weeks. In a 12-week trial of 800 patients with a randomised withdrawal period, 33.6% versus 21.0% met the FDA endpoint (p<0.0001; NNT 8.0, 95% CI 5.4-15.5), and symptoms returned but did not worsen below baseline on withdrawal. A network meta-analysis of 15 RCTs and 8,462 patients found linaclotide, lubiprostone, plecanatide and tenapanor all superior to placebo, with linaclotide 290 micrograms ranked first on the FDA endpoint, abdominal pain and complete spontaneous bowel movements, and tenapanor first for bloating; the AGA translates this into a strong recommendation at high certainty for linaclotide and conditional recommendations at moderate certainty for the others. The counterweights are magnitude, quality and harm rather than a failed trial: no null or negative phase 3 efficacy trial of linaclotide in IBS-C was identified in this search, but an independent GRADE meta-analysis of three RCTs in 1,773 patients rated the evidence for the FDA endpoint and adequate relief as moderate rather than high quality, described linaclotide as "moderately effective", and noted that generalisability is limited by a predominantly white and female trial population — the pivotal trials were about 90% female. Roughly two-thirds of treated patients still do not meet the responder definition, the placebo response was 14-21%, and the network meta-analysis extracted data only at 12 weeks, so long-term relative efficacy is unknown. Diarrhoea is the dominant adverse effect: the same GRADE meta-analysis put the relative risk of diarrhoea leading to discontinuation at 14.75 (95% CI 4.04-53.81, high-quality evidence); it caused discontinuation in 4.5-5.7% of linaclotide patients versus 0.2-0.3% on placebo in the pivotal trials, and in a pooled analysis of 3,853 patients treated up to 104 weeks across both chronic idiopathic constipation and IBS-C, 4.0% discontinued for diarrhoea in the RCTs and 3.8% in long-term studies. A 2025 safety meta-analysis of 54 trials calculated a number needed to harm of 35 for linaclotide (p<0.01), compared with 16 for tenapanor (p<0.01), 58 for tegaserod, 59 for plecanatide and a non-significant 53 for lubiprostone.

Doses studied: Linaclotide 290 micrograms orally once daily, taken on an empty stomach at least 30 minutes before the first meal, for 12 to 26 weeks in the pivotal IBS-C trials. (The 145 and 72 microgram doses studied in the same programme are for chronic idiopathic constipation, not IBS-C.) Comparators in the network meta-analysis included tenapanor 50 mg twice daily, plecanatide 3 and 6 mg once daily and lubiprostone 8 micrograms twice daily.

Why this grade: Several large phase 3 RCTs (804 and 800 patients) show consistent benefit on the FDA composite endpoint, a network meta-analysis of 15 RCTs and 8,462 patients ranks linaclotide 290 micrograms first, and the AGA makes a strong recommendation with high certainty of evidence — the only IBS drug in either AGA guideline to receive one. That meets the bar for guideline first-line support with consistent RCT evidence. The grade is not unanimous: an independent GRADE appraisal of the same trials rated the efficacy evidence moderate rather than high and called the drug "moderately effective". The absolute benefit is partial — roughly two-thirds of treated patients still do not meet the responder definition — and diarrhoea is a real, dose-related and common harm.

Sources (7)
  1. Linaclotide for irritable bowel syndrome with constipation: a 26-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to evaluate efficacy and safety.
    Chey WD, Lembo AJ, Lavins BJ, et al., 2012 (American Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 22986437 · positive

    Among 804 IBS-C patients (mean age 44, 90% female, 78% white), with efficacy evaluated over the first 12 weeks of a 26-week treatment period, 33.7% on linaclotide 290 micrograms daily met the FDA composite endpoint versus 13.9% on placebo (p<0.0001; NNT 5.1, 95% CI 3.9-7.1). Pain response was 48.9% versus 34.5% (NNT 7.0, 95% CI 4.7-13.1) and complete spontaneous bowel movement response 47.6% versus 22.6% (NNT 4.0, 95% CI 3.2-5.4). Statistically significant differences from placebo were also observed across the full 26 weeks. Adverse event incidence was similar between groups except for diarrhoea, which caused discontinuation in 4.5% of linaclotide patients versus 0.2% on placebo.

  2. A 12-week, randomized, controlled trial with a 4-week randomized withdrawal period to evaluate the efficacy and safety of linaclotide in irritable bowel syndrome with constipation.
    Rao S, Lembo AJ, Shiff SJ, et al., 2012 (American Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 22986440 · positive

    Of 800 patients (mean age 43.5, 90.5% female, 76.9% white), 136/405 (33.6%) on linaclotide met the FDA endpoint versus 83/395 (21.0%) on placebo (p<0.0001; NNT 8.0, 95% CI 5.4-15.5). A 30% or greater reduction in abdominal pain for at least 6 of 12 weeks was reached by 50.1% versus 37.5% (p=0.0003). During the randomised withdrawal period, patients switched from linaclotide to placebo saw symptoms return but with no worsening relative to baseline, arguing against rebound. Diarrhoea led to discontinuation in 5.7% of linaclotide versus 0.3% of placebo patients.

  3. Effect of linaclotide in irritable bowel syndrome with constipation (IBS-C): a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Atluri DK, Chandar AK, Bharucha AE, Falck-Ytter Y, 2014 (Neurogastroenterology and Motility) · PMID 24351035 · mixed

    Three RCTs enrolling 1,773 patients were pooled with GRADE appraisal. Compared with placebo, fewer linaclotide patients failed to achieve the FDA endpoint (1,604 patients, RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.76-0.85), adequate IBS symptom relief (1,773 patients, RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.65-0.82) or clinically meaningful improvement in IBS quality of life (1,659 patients, RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.72-0.86). The incidence of diarrhoea leading to discontinuation was far higher with linaclotide (RR 14.75, 95% CI 4.04-53.81). Quality of evidence was rated MODERATE for the FDA endpoint and adequate relief, HIGH for diarrhoea and LOW for quality of life, and the authors conclude linaclotide is "moderately effective". They note generalisability may be limited by a predominantly white, female study population, absent data on prior therapy, and too few patients to identify rare adverse events.

  4. Efficacy of Secretagogues in Patients With Irritable Bowel Syndrome With Constipation: Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis.
    Black CJ, Burr NE, Quigley EMM, Moayyedi P, Houghton LA, Ford AC, 2018 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 30144426 · mixed

    Across 15 RCTs and 8,462 patients, linaclotide, lubiprostone, plecanatide and tenapanor were all superior to placebo, with linaclotide 290 micrograms ranked first on the FDA endpoint, the primary endpoint used in each trial, abdominal pain and complete spontaneous bowel movements, and tenapanor 50 mg twice daily first for bloating. Efficacy was similar among drugs and doses for most endpoints. Total adverse events were significantly greater with linaclotide 290 and 500 micrograms and plecanatide 3 mg than placebo, and diarrhoea was significantly more common with every drug except lubiprostone 8 micrograms twice daily. Data were extracted only at 12 weeks, so long-term relative efficacy is unknown.

  5. AGA Clinical Practice Guideline on the Pharmacological Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome With Constipation.
    Chang L, Sultan S, Lembo A, Verne GN, Smalley W, Heidelbaugh JJ, 2022 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 35738724 · positive

    Using GRADE, the AGA panel made a strong recommendation for linaclotide at high certainty of evidence — the only such rating in either AGA IBS guideline — with conditional recommendations at moderate certainty for tenapanor, plecanatide, tegaserod and lubiprostone, and at low certainty for polyethylene glycol laxatives, tricyclic antidepressants and antispasmodics. The panel made a conditional recommendation against the use of SSRIs (low certainty).

  6. The Safety of Pharmacotherapy for Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Busam JA, Batta N, Shah ED, et al., 2025 (American Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 40471839 · mixed

    Across 54 trials, the number needed to harm — one extra discontinuation due to an adverse event — was 35 for linaclotide (p<0.01), 16 for tenapanor (p<0.01), 58 for tegaserod (p=0.03), 59 for plecanatide (p<0.01) and a non-significant 53 for lubiprostone (p=0.59). Tenapanor had the highest absolute discontinuation risk among IBS-C drugs. Many adverse events were transient without long-term sequelae.

  7. Safety and tolerability of linaclotide for the treatment of chronic idiopathic constipation and irritable bowel syndrome with constipation: pooled Phase 3 analysis.
    Nee JW, Johnston JM, Shea EP, et al., 2019 (Expert Review of Gastroenterology & Hepatology) · PMID 30791771 · mixed

    Pooling six RCTs and two long-term studies across both chronic idiopathic constipation and IBS-C populations and the 72, 145 and 290 microgram doses — 3,853 linaclotide-treated patients for up to 104 weeks — diarrhoea was the most common treatment-emergent adverse event (90.5% of cases mild or moderate in the RCTs), occurring at 1.1 events per patient-year versus 0.2 on placebo. In the RCTs, 6.9% of linaclotide versus 3.0% of placebo patients discontinued for any adverse event and 4.0% versus 0.3% specifically for diarrhoea; in long-term studies 9.4% discontinued for any adverse event and 3.8% for diarrhoea. Serious adverse events were rare, with no serious adverse events of diarrhoea.
