Creatine

Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

Creatine helps muscle and brain cells recycle energy, which makes it a reasonable idea to test against fatigue - but it has barely been tested that way. The one study in people with ME/CFS had 14 participants, no comparison group, and everyone knew what they were taking, so the improvement it reported could easily be expectation. A small multiple sclerosis trial found creatine did not change how tired people felt. In fibromyalgia it made muscles measurably stronger, but that trial did not measure tiredness at all - so the honest answer is that nobody has properly asked the question yet.

Safety

Creatine monohydrate is one of the better-characterised supplements: an International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand concluded that supplementation up to 30 g/day for five years is safe and well tolerated across populations from infants to the elderly - noting that this society has sports-nutrition industry ties and several of the stand's co-authors are affiliated to supplement companies. The 16-week fibromyalgia trial reported no side effects and assessed kidney function; the ME/CFS study had no dropouts for tolerability. The common practical effects are 1-2 kg of water weight early on and, with large single doses, bloating or loose stools, which splitting the dose usually resolves.

Who should be careful: People with existing kidney disease, a single kidney, or taking nephrotoxic drugs should not start creatine without their clinician's involvement. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding is not established. One diagnostic caution matters: creatine supplementation raises serum creatinine without kidney damage, which can be misread as kidney impairment - tell whoever orders your blood tests that you are taking it.

Interactions: No well-documented drug interactions. Caution alongside nephrotoxic drugs (high-dose NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, cyclosporine) is precautionary rather than evidence-based. Caffeine has been proposed to blunt creatine's performance effect, but this has not been shown consistently.

What the evidence says

The closest thing to direct evidence is a 6-week open-label feasibility study in which 14 people with ME/CFS took 16 g/day creatine monohydrate and 11 completed: brain creatine rose in both regions measured (P=0.004 and P=0.012), fatigue and Stroop reaction times decreased (P=0.036 and P=0.014 for congruent and incongruent trials) and hand-grip strength increased (P=0.0004) - but with no placebo arm and no blinding these results cannot be separated from expectation, and the authors themselves framed it as encouragement for a placebo-controlled trial. The controlled trials in fatigue-adjacent conditions do not fill the gap. A double-blind crossover in 11 people with multiple sclerosis found 14 days of creatine did not improve knee extension or flexion work (P=0.81 and P=0.93), power (P=0.31 and P=0.29), or habitual fatigue. A 16-week randomised double-blind trial in fibromyalgia raised intramuscular phosphorylcreatine by 80.3% versus -2.7% on placebo (P=0.04) and improved leg-press and chest-press strength (P=0.02 and P=0.002) and isometric strength (P=0.007), with no general change in aerobic conditioning, pain, cognitive function, sleep quality or quality of life - fatigue was not among its reported outcomes, so it says nothing either way about tiredness. Where creatine does have meta-analytic support is memory rather than fatigue: pooled across 8 RCTs it modestly improved memory (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.04-0.53, I-squared 66%, P=0.02), driven entirely by adults aged 66-76 (SMD 0.88, 95% CI 0.22-1.55) with no effect in those aged 11-31 (SMD 0.03, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.20). A larger 2024 review of 16 RCTs (492 participants) similarly found benefit for memory at moderate certainty and for processing speed at low certainty, but no significant improvement in overall cognitive function or executive function.

Doses studied: 16 g/day for 6 weeks in the open-label ME/CFS study; 20 g/day for 7 days followed by 5 g/day for 7 days in the multiple sclerosis crossover; creatine monohydrate over 16 weeks in the fibromyalgia trial, at a dose not stated in its published abstract; the cognition meta-analyses pooled roughly 2.2-20 g/day for 5 days to 24 weeks.

Why this grade: No adequately powered placebo-controlled trial has tested creatine with fatigue as the primary outcome in an unexplained-fatigue population. The one study in ME/CFS enrolled 14 people, had no placebo arm and no blinding. Of the two controlled trials in fatigue-adjacent conditions, the multiple sclerosis crossover explicitly reported no improvement in habitual fatigue but enrolled only 11 people for 14 days, while the fibromyalgia trial improved muscle strength and did not report fatigue as an outcome at all. The meta-analytic support that does exist is for memory, a different outcome. That is preliminary human evidence, not a null verdict: the evidence base is too small to call it either way.

Sources (6)
  1. Six-Week Supplementation with Creatine in Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS): A Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy Feasibility Study at 3 Tesla
    Godlewska BR, et al., 2024 · PMID 39408275 · positive

    14 ME/CFS patients took 16 g/day creatine for 6 weeks (11 completed): brain creatine rose in both regions (P=0.004 and P=0.012), fatigue and Stroop reaction times decreased (P=0.036 and P=0.014 for congruent and incongruent trials) and grip strength increased (P=0.0004). Open-label, uncontrolled, unblinded - the authors present it as justification for a placebo-controlled trial, not as evidence of effect.

  2. Effect of creatine supplementation on muscle capacity in individuals with multiple sclerosis
    Malin SK, et al., 2008 · PMID 22433042 · null

    Double-blind crossover in 11 people with multiple sclerosis: 14 days of creatine (20 g/day for 7 days then 5 g/day for 7 days) did not improve knee extension or flexion total work (P=0.81 and P=0.93) or power (P=0.31 and P=0.29). Authors concluded creatine did not improve muscle capacity or habitual fatigue. The only cited trial that explicitly reports a fatigue outcome, and it is null - but with just 11 participants over 14 days.

  3. Creatine supplementation in fibromyalgia: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
    Alves CRR, et al., 2013 · PMID 23554283 · mixed

    16-week randomised double-blind trial: creatine raised intramuscular phosphorylcreatine 80.3% versus -2.7% on placebo (P=0.04) and improved leg press (P=0.02), chest press (P=0.002) and isometric strength (P=0.007), but produced no general change in aerobic conditioning, pain, cognitive function, sleep quality or quality of life. Fatigue was NOT among the reported outcomes, so this trial neither supports nor refutes a fatigue effect. No side effects reported and kidney function was assessed.

  4. Effects of creatine supplementation on memory in healthy individuals: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
    Prokopidis K, et al., 2023 · PMID 35984306 · positive

    8 RCTs in the meta-analysis: creatine improved memory versus placebo (SMD 0.29, 95% CI 0.04-0.53, I-squared 66%, P=0.02), driven by older adults aged 66-76 (SMD 0.88, 95% CI 0.22-1.55) with no effect in those aged 11-31 (SMD 0.03, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.20). Memory, not fatigue, is the outcome supported.

  5. The effects of creatine supplementation on cognitive function in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis
    Xu C, et al., 2024 · PMID 39070254 · mixed

    16 RCTs, 492 participants aged 20.8-76.4: improvements in memory (SMD 0.31, 95% CI 0.18-0.44, moderate certainty), attention time (SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.58 to -0.03) and processing speed (SMD -0.51, 95% CI -1.01 to -0.01), but no significant improvement in overall cognitive function or executive function; certainty low outside memory.

  6. International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine
    Kreider RB, et al., 2017 · PMID 28615996 · mixed

    Position stand concluding that short- and long-term creatine supplementation up to 30 g/day for 5 years is safe and well tolerated in healthy individuals and patient populations from infants to the elderly. Addresses exercise performance and safety rather than fatigue, and is issued by a society with sports-nutrition industry ties - several co-authors are affiliated to supplement companies.
