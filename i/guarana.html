Guarana (Paullinia cupana) and caffeine-containing herbs

Not supported by evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

Guarana is essentially a concentrated natural source of caffeine, and it has been tested mainly in people exhausted by cancer treatment. One early study looked promising, but the follow-up studies — including several by the same researchers — did not find any benefit over a dummy capsule, and one study in head and neck cancer found people on guarana did worse on several quality-of-life measures. Caffeine can genuinely make you feel more alert for a few hours when you are sleep-deprived, but that is not the same as fixing tiredness that rest does not fix, and late-day caffeine can wreck the sleep you actually need.

Safety

Guarana is a concentrated caffeine source, so its safety profile is largely caffeine's. It was well tolerated in the breast cancer chemotherapy trial (no CTCAE grade 2-4 toxicities, no worsening of sleep quality, anxiety or depression), and no significant toxicity difference was seen in the purified-extract trials. But in the head-and-neck chemoradiotherapy trial the guarana arm had significantly worse overall, functional and symptom quality-of-life domains after the second chemotherapy cycle, along with worse weight loss and greater analgesic use — a real signal that it is not benign in every setting, even though CTCAE-graded toxicity did not differ. The practical caution for anyone with persistent fatigue is circular harm: caffeine consumed late in the day fragments sleep, and poor sleep is itself a leading driver of daytime fatigue, so a caffeine-based fix can quietly deepen the problem it is masking. Caffeine dependence and withdrawal headaches are also common with regular use. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established for guarana supplements, and caffeine intake is generally restricted in pregnancy.

Who should be careful: Not appropriate as a fatigue strategy for people with insomnia or fragmented sleep, poorly controlled anxiety, arrhythmia, or uncontrolled hypertension. It should not be used to paper over unexplained tiredness: fatigue lasting weeks needs a workup for anemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnea, diabetes, depression, medication side effects and other treatable causes. In ME/CFS in particular, stimulants that let a person push past their limit can precipitate post-exertional symptom worsening; pacing rather than pushing is the guidance in that condition.

Interactions: Guarana's caffeine adds to caffeine from coffee, tea, cola, energy drinks and combination supplements, and product labels frequently do not state total caffeine content — in one trial a 500 mg guarana dose delivered 130 mg of caffeine, so unintentional stacking is easy. Caffeine can compound the effects of other stimulants and can aggravate anxiety, tremor, palpitations and insomnia. Discuss it with a prescriber if you take medication for a heart rhythm problem, high blood pressure or anxiety.

What the evidence says

Guarana's fatigue evidence is confined almost entirely to cancer-related fatigue, and replication has failed. The original signal came from a randomized crossover trial in 75 breast cancer patients with progressive fatigue after their first chemotherapy cycle, where guarana 50 mg twice daily for 21 days significantly improved FACIT-F, FACT-ES and Brief Fatigue Inventory global scores versus placebo at days 21 and 49 (p < 0.01), although the Chalder scale improved only at day 21 (p < 0.01) and not day 49 (p = 0.27). Subsequent randomized comparisons did not reproduce it: a crossover trial in 36 breast cancer patients receiving adjuvant radiotherapy (75 mg/day) found no statistically significant difference from placebo on any measured score; an early-discontinuation study enrolled 40 patients into an open induction phase and randomized the 33 who improved or stabilized, finding no significant difference between purified PC-18 and placebo on BFI, FACIT, HADS or PSQI; and two further double-blind RCTs of purified PC-18 (37.5 mg twice daily in one, 7.5 or 12.5 mg twice daily in the other) found it not superior to placebo on either the Chalder or BFI scales, which the authors attributed partly to unexpectedly high placebo antifatigue activity possibly from the magnesium silicate excipient. The meta-analyses conflict and the independent ones are null: a 2021 systematic review of 7 studies in 427 patients pooled three breast cancer trials and found guarana no better than placebo (mean difference -0.02, 95% CI -1.54 to 1.50, p = 0.98) with GRADE certainty very low, and a 2026 meta-analysis of 14 phytotherapy RCTs found the guarana subgroup non-significant, while a 2024 meta-analysis of 5 sources in 229 patients did report benefit (SMD -0.77, 95% CI -1.34 to -0.21, p = 0.007) but with high heterogeneity (I-squared 78%) and a senior author shared with the trials it pools. A safety-relevant negative: in 60 head-and-neck cancer patients on chemoradiotherapy, the guarana arm showed significantly worse overall (p = 0.0054), functional (p = 0.018) and symptom (p = 0.0042) quality-of-life domains after the second chemotherapy cycle, plus worse weight loss (p = 0.0074) and more analgesic use (p = 0.0253), and the authors concluded guarana is not beneficial in that population. Separately, caffeine itself does have randomized evidence, but for a different question: a Cochrane review of 13 trials in shift work and jet lag found caffeine improved orientation and attention (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.83 to -0.27) and memory (SMD -1.08, 95% CI -2.07 to -0.09) versus placebo with no trial measuring injury, and an acute crossover trial found 500 mg guarana (130 mg caffeine) did not improve cognitive performance, heart rate variability or mood versus placebo — so acute alertness under circadian disruption should not be read across to persistent fatigue that rest does not fix.

Doses studied: Guarana dry extract 50 mg twice daily for 21 days (the original positive breast cancer crossover trial and the negative head-and-neck trial); 75 mg/day during radiotherapy (null); purified dry extract PC-18 at 37.5 mg twice daily (null in both a maintenance-phase randomization and a double-blind RCT), and at 7.5 mg or 12.5 mg twice daily (also not superior to placebo). For scale on caffeine content, a 500 mg guarana dose used in an acute crossover trial delivered 130 mg of caffeine — so a stated milligram dose of guarana tells you little about how much caffeine you are getting unless the product is standardized.

Why this grade: Graded not_supported. Adequate randomized evidence now exists in the one population where guarana has been meaningfully studied — cancer-related fatigue — and it is predominantly null. The original positive crossover trial has repeatedly failed to replicate, including in two double-blind RCTs by the same research group using a purified extract, in a randomized maintenance phase after an open induction period, and in a radiotherapy trial. A 2021 systematic review with meta-analysis found guarana no better than placebo (mean difference -0.02, 95% CI -1.54 to 1.50, p = 0.98) with GRADE certainty rated very low, and a 2026 phytotherapy meta-analysis of 14 RCTs again found the guarana subgroup non-significant. One 2024 meta-analysis did find benefit (SMD -0.77, 95% CI -1.34 to -0.21, p = 0.007), but with substantial heterogeneity (I-squared 78%), only 229 patients, an unpublished conference abstract among its five included sources, and a senior author who is also senior author on the originating positive trial and on the failed replications — so it is not an independent check. A head-and-neck trial additionally found the guarana arm significantly worse on several quality-of-life domains. Following the rule to take the lower grade when torn, not_supported is the honest call. Outside oncology there are no trials of guarana for persistent fatigue at all; caffeine's real evidence is for acute alertness under sleep loss, which is a different clinical question.

Sources (10)
  1. The use of guarana (Paullinia cupana) as a dietary supplement for fatigue in cancer patients: a systematic review with a meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisde Araujo et al., 2021 · PMID 34146166 · null

    Systematic review of 7 studies in 427 cancer patients; meta-analysis of the three breast cancer trials with sufficient data found guarana did NOT reduce cancer-related fatigue versus placebo (mean difference -0.02, 95% CI -1.54 to 1.50, p = 0.98). Quality of evidence by GRADE was rated very low, and the authors concluded guarana was not superior to placebo and that better-quality studies are needed.

  2. Natural supplementation to effectively treat cancer-induced fatigue: evidence of a meta-analysis on the use of guaraná.
    Meta-analysisMaselli-Schoueri, Aguiar Junior and Del Giglio, 2024 · PMID 39536249 · positive

    Meta-analysis of 4 full articles plus 1 conference abstract, 229 patients, 2009-2023. Reported a significant benefit of guarana for cancer-related fatigue (SMD -0.77, 95% CI -1.34 to -0.21; Z = 2.68, p = 0.007) despite high heterogeneity (I-squared 78%, chi-squared 18.51, df 4, p = 0.0010), and called for larger prospective RCTs to validate the finding. Important caveat for weighing this against the null meta-analyses: the senior author is also senior author of the originating positive guarana trial and of the null replication trials, so this pooled result is not an independent check on the original signal.

  3. Purified Dry Paullinia cupana (PC-18) Extract for Chemotherapy-Induced Fatigue: Results of Two Double-Blind Randomized Clinical Trials.
    Randomised trialSette et al., 2017 · PMID 29190155 · null

    Two double-blind RCTs in early breast cancer patients whose fatigue scores rose after the first adjuvant chemotherapy cycle. In both, purified PC-18 (37.5 mg twice daily in the first; 7.5 or 12.5 mg twice daily in the second) was NOT superior to placebo on either the Chalder or Brief Fatigue Inventory scales. The authors attributed the result partly to unexpectedly high placebo antifatigue activity, possibly from the roughly 100 mg of magnesium silicate excipient in every capsule; serum magnesium rose significantly after placebo. No significant toxicities were observed.

  4. Purified dry extract of Paullinia cupana (guaraná) (PC-18) for chemotherapy-related fatigue in patients with solid tumors: an early discontinuation study.
    Randomised trialdel Giglio et al., 2013 · PMID 24237188 · null

    40 patients with solid tumours whose Brief Fatigue Inventory scores rose after one week of chemotherapy took open-label PC-18 37.5 mg twice daily for a 3-week induction phase, in which scores improved or stabilized in 36 of 40 (mean BFI difference 2.503, 95% CI 1.716-3.375, p = .0002). The 33 responders were then randomized to PC-18 or placebo for 3 weeks, and no significant differences were observed between arms on BFI, FACIT, HADS or PSQI. The authors invoke a conditioning effect to explain the better-than-expected placebo scores; the randomized comparison itself was null.

  5. Placebo-Controlled, Double-Blind, Randomized Study of a Dry Guarana Extract in Patients with Head and Neck Tumors Undergoing Chemoradiotherapy: Effects on Fatigue and Quality of Life.
    Journal articleMartins, Ferreira and Del Giglio, 2016 · PMID 27322597 · negative

    60 patients with stage I-IV head and neck squamous cell carcinoma on chemoradiotherapy, guarana 50 mg twice daily vs placebo. Significant WORSENING of overall (p = 0.0054), functional (p = 0.018) and symptom (p = 0.0042) quality-of-life domains in the guarana arm after the second chemotherapy cycle, with worse weight loss (p = 0.0074) and more analgesic use (p = 0.0253) after the third. The abstract also reports the guarana group improving after the FIRST cycle on pain (p = 0.0133), social eating (p = 0.0227), swallowing (p = 0.0254), coughing (p = 0.0107) and weight loss (p = 0.012), improvement across three EORTC-QLQ-C30 domains, and no between-group difference in CTCAE toxicity — the internal picture is mixed, but the authors' stated conclusion is that guarana is not beneficial for this population.

  6. Effectiveness of guaraná (Paullinia cupana) for postradiation fatigue and depression: results of a pilot double-blind randomized study.
    Randomised trialda Costa Miranda et al., 2009 · PMID 19388866 · null

    Randomized double-blind crossover pilot trial in 36 breast cancer patients receiving adjuvant radiotherapy (28 daily fractions of 180 cGy); guarana 75 mg daily vs placebo with crossover at the midpoint. No statistically significant difference between guarana and placebo on any measured fatigue or depression score, and no significant within-group change during either treatment period.

  7. Efficacy of Phytotherapy for Cancer-Related Fatigue: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Narrative reviewMatsas et al., 2026 · PMID 41745077 · null

    14 RCTs searched through October 2025. Phytotherapy overall gave a modest improvement in cancer-related fatigue (SMD 0.31, 95% CI 0.08-0.53, p = 0.022, I-squared 56.7%), but in subgroup analysis guarana did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect (nor did ginseng); only the heterogeneous 'other formulations' group did. Most trials were at high or unclear risk of bias and GRADE certainty was rated very low.

  8. Guarana (Paullinia cupana) improves fatigue in breast cancer patients undergoing systemic chemotherapy.
    Randomised trialde Oliveira Campos et al., 2011 · PMID 21612429 · positive

    Randomized crossover trial in 75 breast cancer patients with progressive fatigue after their first chemotherapy cycle (32 guarana, 43 placebo); guarana 50 mg twice daily for 21 days, 7-day washout, then crossover. Significantly improved FACIT-F, FACT-ES and BFI global scores versus placebo at days 21 and 49 (p < 0.01); the Chalder scale improved at day 21 (p < 0.01) but not day 49 (p = 0.27). No CTCAE grade 2-4 toxicities and no worsening of sleep quality, anxiety or depression. This is the originating positive result that later randomized trials failed to replicate.

  9. Effects of Acute Guarana (Paullinia cupana) Ingestion on Mental Performance and Vagal Modulation Compared to a Low Dose of Caffeine.
    Randomised trialTalik et al., 2024 · PMID 38931247 · null

    Double-blind crossover trial in 20 participants comparing 100 mg caffeine, 500 mg guarana containing 130 mg caffeine, and placebo, with Simon and 2N-Back cognitive tests before and 60 minutes after ingestion. Response time and heart rate variability were unaffected by treatment (p > 0.05); accuracy on the 2N-Back improved post-ingestion only in the PLACEBO condition (p = 0.033). Neither guarana nor low-dose caffeine influenced cognitive performance or gave consistent benefit on mood or mental workload, and there was no effect on cognition following physical fatigue.

  10. Caffeine for the prevention of injuries and errors in shift workers.
    Meta-analysisKer et al., 2010 · PMID 20464765 · positive

    Cochrane review of 13 RCTs in jet lag and shift work disorder. Caffeine improved orientation and attention (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.83 to -0.27) and memory (SMD -1.08, 95% CI -2.07 to -0.09) versus placebo and reduced errors; concept formation and reasoning (SMD -0.41, 95% CI -1.04 to 0.23) and perception (SMD -0.77, 95% CI -1.73 to 0.20) crossed zero, and verbal function and language showed no benefit (SMD 0.18, 95% CI -0.50 to 0.87). NO trial measured injury. There was a high risk of bias for allocation concealment and selective outcome reporting; most participants were young and studied under simulated conditions, so generalizability to real-world work is unclear. This addresses acute sleepiness, not persistent fatigue.
