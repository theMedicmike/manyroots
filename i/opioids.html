A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For chronic pain

ImportantThe highest-harm intervention on this page - and still not one to stop on your own. After long use, reducing the dose needs a plan made with a prescriber.

Opioids are the intervention where the research most clearly contradicts the intuition that a stronger drug means better relief. In the largest and longest trial, people given opioids for a year did no better on daily functioning than people given non-opioid medicines — and their pain scores were slightly worse, with more side effects. Guidelines now urge real caution about starting them for ongoing joint or back pain. If you are already taking opioids, this is a conversation to have with your prescriber, not a reason to stop on your own, because stopping suddenly can be genuinely dangerous.

Safety

This is the highest-harm intervention in this group. Randomised data show vomiting in 5.9% versus 2.3% on placebo, withdrawal due to adverse events 2.64 times more likely with tramadol than placebo in osteoarthritis, and serious adverse events in 110/2459 versus 22/1153. In chronic low back pain the number needed to harm (5) was lower than the number needed to treat for meaningful benefit (16). Beyond trial-captured harms, long-term use carries risks the trials were too short and too selective to measure — Cochrane specifically notes the included trials did not report addiction, overdose, sleep-disordered breathing, opioid-induced hyperalgesia or hypogonadism. Expect constipation, sedation, nausea, cognitive slowing and increased fall and fracture risk in older adults. Never stop long-term opioids abruptly: abrupt discontinuation or rapid tapering carries its own serious risks and must be managed with a prescriber. Ask about a take-home naloxone supply if you or anyone in your household uses opioids.

Who should be careful: Should not be initiated without a prescriber's individualised risk assessment. Particularly hazardous in people with untreated sleep apnea or significant respiratory disease, a personal or family history of substance use disorder, active depression or suicidality, and in older adults at fall risk. Avoid in pregnancy where possible because of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome. Tramadol carries additional seizure risk and should be avoided in people with a seizure disorder or on drugs that lower the seizure threshold.

Interactions: Combining opioids with benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedative-hypnotics, muscle relaxants or alcohol substantially increases the risk of respiratory depression and fatal overdose — this is the single most important interaction. Tramadol and tapentadol add serotonin syndrome risk when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs including duloxetine, triptans, linezolid or MAOIs. Opioids compound constipation with anticholinergics. Codeine and tramadol depend on CYP2D6 metabolism, so effects vary unpredictably between individuals and with CYP2D6-inhibiting drugs such as fluoxetine, paroxetine and bupropion.

What the evidence says

Evidence for opioids in chronic musculoskeletal pain shows small short-term effects that do not persist or outperform alternatives. In the SPACE randomised trial, 240 patients with chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis were followed 12 months: pain-related function did not differ (mean BPI interference 3.4 opioid vs 3.3 non-opioid; difference 0.1, 95% CI -0.5 to 0.7), pain intensity was significantly better in the non-opioid group (4.0 vs 3.5; difference 0.5, 95% CI 0.0 to 1.0), and medication-related adverse symptoms were significantly more common with opioids (1.8 vs 0.9; difference 0.9, 95% CI 0.3 to 1.5). A meta-analysis of 96 RCTs (26,169 participants) found opioids reduced pain by 0.69 cm (95% CI -0.82 to -0.56) on a 10-cm scale where the minimally important difference is 1 cm, improved SF-36 physical function by 2.04 points where 5 is the threshold, and produced comparable pain relief to NSAIDs (WMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.54 to 0.34). In the osteoarthritis network meta-analysis, all opioids had 53% probability or less of exceeding the minimal clinically important pain reduction, while 83.3% showed increased dropout due to adverse events and 89.5% increased any adverse event. A responder analysis of chronic low back pain trials found the number needed to harm (5) was lower than the number needed to treat for meaningful benefit (16). Cochrane data in chronic low back pain do show short-term efficacy versus placebo (strong opioids SMD -0.43, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.33) but note there are no placebo-controlled RCTs establishing long-term effectiveness or safety.

Doses studied: SPACE used a treat-to-target strategy starting with immediate-release morphine, oxycodone, or hydrocodone/acetaminophen, titrated by response. Meta-analyses pooled tramadol across 37.5-400 mg/day and strong opioids (morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, oxymorphone and tapentadol) across a wide dose range, with transdermal buprenorphine analysed separately; the osteoarthritis network meta-analysis examined 19 opioid preparations or doses, with oxymorphone 80 mg/day the most harmful.

Why this grade: Adequate evidence exists and does not support opioids for chronic musculoskeletal pain. The pivotal 12-month pragmatic randomised trial (SPACE) found opioids not superior to non-opioid medication on pain-related function, with pain intensity actually favouring the non-opioid arm and more adverse symptoms in the opioid arm; its authors stated the results do not support initiating opioid therapy for chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis. A 96-trial meta-analysis found the average benefit versus placebo (-0.69 cm on a 10-cm scale) falls below the 1-cm minimally important difference, and no better than NSAIDs. A 192-trial osteoarthritis network meta-analysis found every opioid had 53% probability or less of exceeding the clinically important threshold, while 83-89% carried increased adverse event and dropout risk, concluding the clinical benefit does not outweigh the harm. In chronic low back pain a responder analysis found patients were more likely to quit because of a side effect (number needed to harm 5) than to gain a meaningful benefit (NNT 16). Short-term placebo-controlled trials do show small statistical benefit, which is why some clinicians still use opioids selectively, but that does not lift the grade above not_supported for this condition. Nothing here is a reason for anyone currently on long-term opioids to stop abruptly.

  1. Effect of Opioid vs Nonopioid Medications on Pain-Related Function in Patients With Chronic Back Pain or Hip or Knee Osteoarthritis Pain: The SPACE Randomized Clinical Trial.
    Randomised trialKrebs EE, Gravely A, Nugent S, et al., 2018 (JAMA) · PMID 29509867 · null

    Pragmatic 12-month randomised trial, 240 patients (mean age 58.3, 97.5% completed) with moderate to severe chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis pain. Groups did not differ on pain-related function over 12 months (BPI interference 3.4 opioid vs 3.3 non-opioid; difference 0.1, 95% CI -0.5 to 0.7; P=.58). Pain intensity was significantly better in the non-opioid group (BPI severity 4.0 vs 3.5; difference 0.5, 95% CI 0.0 to 1.0; P=.03), and medication-related adverse symptoms were significantly more common with opioids (1.8 vs 0.9; difference 0.9, 95% CI 0.3 to 1.5; P=.03). Authors concluded the results do not support initiating opioid therapy for these conditions.

  2. Opioids for Chronic Noncancer Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisBusse JW, Wang L, Kamaleldin M, et al., 2018 (JAMA) · PMID 30561481 · mixed

    96 RCTs, 26,169 participants. Versus placebo, opioids reduced pain by a weighted mean difference of -0.69 cm (95% CI -0.82 to -0.56) on a 10-cm visual analogue scale where the minimally important difference is 1 cm, with an 11.9% (95% CI 9.7% to 14.1%) modelled risk difference for achieving that difference. Physical function improved 2.04 points (95% CI 1.41 to 2.68) on the SF-36 physical component score where the MID is 5. Vomiting occurred in 5.9% with opioids versus 2.3% with placebo. Pain relief was statistically indistinguishable from NSAIDs (WMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.54 to 0.34).

  3. Effectiveness and safety of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioid treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis: network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisda Costa BR, Pereira TV, Saadat P, et al., 2021 (BMJ) · PMID 34642179 · negative

    192 trials, 102,829 participants, 19 opioid preparations or doses. All opioids had a probability of 53% or less of producing a more pronounced treatment effect than the minimal clinically relevant reduction in pain. 83.3% of opioid preparations showed an increased risk of dropout due to adverse events and 89.5% an increased risk of any adverse event; oxymorphone 80 mg/day was worst (51% dropouts, 88% any adverse event). Authors concluded the clinical benefit of opioid treatment, regardless of preparation or dose, does not outweigh the harm it might cause in osteoarthritis.

  4. PEER systematic review of randomized controlled trials: Management of chronic low back pain in primary care.
    Systematic reviewKolber MR, Ton J, Thomas B, et al., 2021 (Can Fam Physician) · PMID 33483410 · negative

    63 RCTs reporting responder analyses across 15 treatments for chronic low back pain. Patients treated with opioids had a greater likelihood of discontinuing treatment owing to an adverse event (number needed to harm 5) than of continuing treatment to derive any clinically meaningful benefit (NNT 16). By contrast, moderate-certainty evidence supported exercise (RR 1.71, 95% CI 1.37 to 2.15; NNT 7), oral NSAIDs (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.78; NNT 6) and duloxetine (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.38; NNT 10), with exercise the only intervention showing sustained benefit to 48 weeks.

  5. Tramadol for osteoarthritis.
    Systematic reviewToupin April K, Bisaillon J, Welch V, et al., 2019 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 31132298 · null

    22 RCTs; 3871 participants randomised to tramadol alone or with acetaminophen. Moderate-quality evidence of no important benefit on pain (4% absolute improvement, 95% CI 3% to 5%) or physical function (4%, 95% CI 2% to 6%) versus placebo; 15 per 100 improved by 20% on tramadol versus 10 per 100 on placebo. Withdrawal due to adverse events was markedly higher (RR 2.64, 95% CI 2.17 to 3.20), and serious adverse events occurred in 110/2459 on tramadol versus 22/1153 on placebo (RR 1.78, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.84).

  6. Opioids compared to placebo or other treatments for chronic low-back pain.
    Meta-analysisChaparro LE, Furlan AD, Deshpande A, Mailis-Gagnon A, Atlas S, Turk DC, 2013 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 23983011 · positive

    15 trials, 5540 participants, minimum 4 weeks' duration. Tramadol was better than placebo for pain (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.66 to -0.44; low quality) and function (SMD -0.18, 95% CI -0.29 to -0.07). Strong opioids (morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, oxymorphone, tapentadol) were better than placebo for pain (SMD -0.43, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.33) and function (SMD -0.26, 95% CI -0.37 to -0.15; moderate quality). Transdermal buprenorphine, analysed separately, showed no difference from placebo on function (SMD -0.14, 95% CI -0.53 to 0.25; very low quality). Two trials found no difference between opioids and antidepressants. Trials had high drop-out rates and short duration, did not report addiction, overdose, sleep apnea, hyperalgesia or hypogonadism, and the authors state there are no placebo-RCTs supporting long-term effectiveness or safety.

  7. CDC Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain - United States, 2022.
    GuidelineDowell D, Ragan KR, Jones CM, Baldwin GT, Chou R, 2022 (MMWR Recomm Rep) · PMID 36327391 · mixed

    GRADE-based national guideline for outpatients aged 18 and over, updating the 2016 CDC guideline and covering acute (under 1 month), subacute (1-3 months) and chronic (over 3 months) pain; it does not apply to sickle cell disease, cancer, or palliative and end-of-life care. It structures decisions around four areas: whether to initiate opioids at all, opioid and dose selection, duration and follow-up, and assessing risk and addressing potential harms including opioid use disorder, overdose and death. The guideline explicitly warns that its recommendations should not be applied as inflexible standards of care across patient populations.

For depression & low mood

ImportantDo not stop or cut down a prescribed opioid on your own, and do not treat this grade as a reason to. Both stopping and reducing the dose carry raised suicide risk, so any change belongs with your prescriber. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 in the US (veterans press 1).

Opioid painkillers are on this page because people ask whether they lift mood, and the answer is that nobody has properly tested it - the large reviews of opioids collected no mood data at all. A related but different medicine, buprenorphine, has been trialled for depression and the effect was small and inconsistent. Nothing here is a reason to change what you take. Coming off these medicines, or cutting the dose, is itself linked to higher suicide risk, so that decision belongs with your prescriber and not with a webpage.

Safety

If you are having thoughts of suicide, tell someone now: in the US call or text 988, and veterans press 1. The most actionable finding on this page is about stopping, not starting. In 14,596 high-dose chronic opioid therapy patients, discontinuation raised death by suicide compared with a stable or increasing dose, and the risk was higher when discontinuation had been preceded by a dose reduction (aHR 4.47) than when it was abrupt (aHR 3.63) - so tapering first was not protective, and a supervised reduction cannot be presented as risk-free. A large claims cohort of prescribed dose reductions likewise found more emergency or hospital encounters for a mental health crisis. Set against that, a 34-study meta-analysis of tapering programmes found benefit on pain and psychological measures overall, though on evidence its authors rate at poor risk of bias in 30 of the included studies, and it did not measure suicide. What follows is not that reduction should never happen - it is that reduction and discontinuation are the risky moments and belong in a plan made with a prescriber, with mental-health follow-up built in rather than assumed. Ask about take-home naloxone and make sure a household member knows where it is; tolerance falls during and after a taper, so a dose that was previously tolerated can be fatal if use resumes.

Who should be careful: Prescribing decisions belong with a clinician and this entry does not make them. The relevant point for this page is that guidelines discourage long-term opioid treatment where chronic pain and major depression occur together - which describes many readers of a depression page - because that combination is associated with higher daily doses, longer duration and more adverse events. Buprenorphine/samidorphan is investigational for depression, is not approved for it, and should not be sought as a treatment for it.

Interactions: St John's Wort, graded on this same page, cut oxycodone exposure by 50% and shortened its half-life in a randomised crossover study, with a significant fall in the drug effect people reported - so self-treating low mood with St John's Wort while taking an opioid can undercut pain control. SSRIs and SNRIs, also graded here, inhibit CYP2D6 and alter tramadol: paroxetine raised parent tramadol concentrations and cut the active metabolite by 40-67%, changing the response on some pain measures while the effect was retained on others, so treat this as partial attenuation rather than abolition. Serotonin syndrome is a recognised concern when tramadol is combined with serotonergic antidepressants; the risk with tricyclics specifically is mechanistic and unquantified here, because the review most often cited for it excluded tricyclic cases. Additive sedation and respiratory depression with benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedating antihistamines and alcohol. Constipation stacks with amitriptyline, antispasmodics and loperamide - see the gut and digestion page, where opioid-induced constipation is graded in its own right.

What the evidence says

SURROGATE ONLY throughout, and the strands differ in what they measured. No mood data: the Cochrane overview of 16 reviews (61 studies, 18,679 participants, most 6-16 weeks) found no data on depressive symptoms or mood disturbance anywhere, alongside any adverse event RR 1.42 (1.22-1.66) and serious adverse events RR 2.75 (2.06-3.67) versus placebo. Buprenorphine strand: FORWARD-3 null (P=0.782), FORWARD-4 null (P=0.109), FORWARD-5 positive (P=0.018 on MADRS-6), pooled across 11 studies (N=1,699) Hedges' g 0.17 (0.05-0.29), driven by six buprenorphine/samidorphan trials. Association strand, demoted on retrieval: new-onset depression in 9-12% of three cohorts (70,997 VHA; 13,777 and 22,981 others), rising with duration (31-90 days HR 1.18-1.33; over 90 days HR 1.35-2.05) but NOT with dose; escalation rate mattered independently (27.5 vs 14.1 new diagnoses per 1,000 person-years, HR 1.58, 1.30-1.93). Against that, the first prospective test (760 participants, monthly PHQ-9 over a year) found daily versus non-daily use not significantly associated with depression trajectories, the authors concluding the excess may reflect high-risk patients receiving daily opioids rather than an effect of exposure; and a 10-article synthesis found depression aES 1.18 (0.98-1.41), an interval including no effect, though any mood outcome was raised (1.80, 1.40-2.30). Benefit denominator: across 96 RCTs and 26,169 participants, opioids versus placebo reduced pain by 0.69 cm on a 10-cm scale (95% CI -0.82 to -0.56) and improved physical function by 2.04 SF-36 points (1.41-2.68) - statistically significant, small on the authors' reading - while the pragmatic 12-month SPACE trial (240 patients, 97.5% completion) found no advantage over non-opioid therapy for pain-related function (difference 0.1, -0.5 to 0.7) and significantly WORSE pain intensity (difference 0.5, 0.0 to 1.0). Stopping and reducing: in 14,596 Oregon Medicaid high-dose patients, discontinuation raised death by suicide against a stable or rising dose whether abrupt (aHR 3.63, 1.42-9.25) or preceded by dose reduction (aHR 4.47, 1.68-11.88) - so a taper before stopping was not protective. A large claims cohort identified tapering from prescribed dose reductions and found more mental-health-crisis encounters (aIRR 1.74, 1.50-2.01 after correction; that paper carries a substantive erratum for a programming error and overdose miscoding, and the overdose direction remains unresolved between the two largest cohorts). A 34-study meta-analysis of tapering programmes found an overall benefit (effect size 0.917, 0.61-1.22, P<0.001) on pain and psychological measures, but its authors rate risk of bias poor in 30 of the included studies, and it did not measure suicide.

Doses studied: No dose or duration has been tested for depression, because the question has not been trialled. In the association cohorts the signal tracked duration and escalation rate rather than milligrams: morphine-equivalent dose bands of 1-50, 51-100 and over 100 mg/day were not significantly associated with new-onset depression, while a rapidly increasing dose was. The buprenorphine/samidorphan trials used a fixed 2 mg/2 mg adjunctive dose over 5-6 weeks in people already on an antidepressant, which is a different drug and a different purpose from opioid analgesia.

Why this grade: Scoped to a prescribed opioid analgesic as an intervention for depression, and it rests on one clause of the scale, not several: adequate evidence has been gathered and it is null. The 2017 Cochrane overview of 16 reviews - 14 opioid agents given for two weeks or longer, 61 studies, 18,679 randomised participants - reports no data at all on depressive symptoms or mood disturbance in any included review, while finding serious adverse events raised against placebo (RR 2.75, 95% CI 2.06-3.67). The only therapeutic signal in this drug family belongs to buprenorphine and the buprenorphine/samidorphan combination, tested as an add-on rather than as analgesia, and that programme mostly failed: FORWARD-3 missed its primary endpoint outright (MADRS-10 change -4.8 vs -4.6, mean difference -0.3, P=0.782, with no difference in response or remission), FORWARD-4 missed (-1.8 on MADRS-10 at week 5, P=0.109), FORWARD-5 met it narrowly (-1.5 on MADRS-6, P=0.018), and an 11-study meta-analysis put the pooled effect at Hedges' g 0.17 (95% CI 0.05-0.29). The guideline clause does NOT apply: the 2022 CDC guideline governs this class for pain and is silent on mood disorders, so it is cited as null, not negative - silence is not opposition. The nearest real recommend-against statement is narrower, that guidelines discourage long-term opioid treatment where chronic pain and major depression co-occur. This entry does NOT claim opioid analgesics cause depression. Three propensity-weighted retrospective cohorts found new depression diagnoses rising with duration (over 90 days, HR 1.35 to 2.05) and with escalation rate rather than milligrams (HR 1.58 for rapid increase), but the same investigators' first prospective cohort found daily versus non-daily use not significantly associated with depression trajectories, and the one published synthesis found the adjusted association small and not significant (1.18, 95% CI 0.98-1.41). Adverse selection is documented: depressed patients are twice as likely to transition to long-term use. Every outcome in that strand is a health-system diagnosis code, which is a threshold-defined diagnosis and not a hard clinical event.

  4. Prescription opioid use and 12-month depression trajectories.
    Journal articleScherrer, Salas, Naidu, et al. (incl. Sullivan), 2026 (Psychiatry Research) · PMID 41924766 · null

    First prospective cohort to model prescription opioid use against depression trajectories: participants with a new period of prescription opioid use recruited from two health care systems, invited to complete twelve monthly surveys over a year; from the 760 participants with 3 or more monthly surveys the authors computed PHQ-9 latent growth trajectories, with a 3-class solution (severe-increasing, moderate-stable, low-decreasing) fitting best. Predictors of the severe-increasing class were pain-related functional impairment (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.11-1.74), more pain sites (OR 1.20, 1.10-1.31) and smoking (OR 2.38, 1.27-4.47); higher emotional support (OR 0.92, 0.89-0.94) and ability to participate in social roles (OR 0.96, 0.93-0.99) were protective. The authors' stated conclusion: the lack of an independent link suggests increased depression rates in daily opioid-exposed patients "may be a result of high risk patients receiving daily opioids rather than a direct effect of the opioid exposure." All authors report no conflict of interest. SURROGATE ONLY (PHQ-9 trajectories).

  6. Depression Effects on Long-term Prescription Opioid Use, Abuse, and Addiction.
    Narrative reviewSullivan, 2018 (Clinical Journal of Pain) · PMID 29505419 · mixed

    Review of prospective cohort, retrospective cohort and other observational studies of depression and opioid use, abuse and addiction, by a co-author of the Scherrer cohorts. Opens by stating that treatment guidelines discourage long-term opioid treatment for patients with chronic pain and major depression, but that this treatment occurs commonly, producing higher daily doses, longer duration and more adverse events — On confounding: depressed patients initiate opioid therapy only slightly more often than non-depressed patients but are TWICE as likely to transition to long-term use, an adverse selection the author describes as a process of self-selection; depressed patients appear to continue opioid use at lower pain intensity and higher physical function than non-depressed patients.   7. Patient outcomes after opioid dose reduction among patients with chronic opioid therapy.
    Journal articleHallvik, El Ibrahimi, Johnston, et al., 2022 (Pain) · PMID 33863865 · mixed

    Retrospective cohort of Oregon Medicaid recipients with high-dose chronic opioid therapy, with claims linked to prescription drug monitoring program data and death data from vital statistics, 2014 to 2017; Cox proportional hazard models over the following year. Of 14,596 high-dose COT patients, 4,191 (28.7%) abruptly discontinued, 1,648 (11.3%) reduced dose before discontinuing, 6,480 (44.4%) reduced dose but never discontinued, and 2,277 (15.6%) had a stable or increasing dose. The other PubMed-linked erratum (JAMA 2022;327(7):688, doi 10.1001/jama.2021.22379, "Cancer Line" by K.R.   9. Effectiveness of pain medication tapering in chronic pain patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisWuyts, Goudman, Crunelle, et al., 2024 (British Journal of Anaesthesia) · PMID 39358186 · mixed

  10. Opioids for Chronic Noncancer Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.

For gut & digestion

ImportantDo not stop a prescribed opioid on your own to fix your bowels. After long use, reducing the dose needs a plan made with a prescriber.

Opioids constipate almost everyone who takes them for long, and unlike some side effects this one does not reliably fade. If you are on codeine, tramadol, morphine, oxycodone or a fentanyl patch and your bowels have stopped working properly, the painkiller is the likely reason. This matters because the fix is usually not to stop the opioid and suffer: ordinary laxatives help many people, and there is a specific class of prescription drugs that blocks the opioid effect in the gut without taking away the pain relief. That last point is worth knowing - in the trials, these drugs did not undo the analgesia. Talk to a prescriber rather than quietly putting up with it, and treat a bowel that has not moved for days with abdominal pain and vomiting as urgent.

Safety

🔴 A bowel that has not opened for several days together with abdominal pain, distension or vomiting can mean obstruction or faecal impaction - that needs urgent assessment, not another dose of laxative. Opioid effects on the gut are not limited to constipation: nausea and dry mouth were more common still in the trial data (21% and 25%). Do not stop a prescribed opioid abruptly to fix your bowels - withdrawal is unpleasant and, after long use, dose reduction needs to be planned with a prescriber. The reversal drugs are not interchangeable with laxatives and are prescription-only; mu-opioid antagonists raised rates of diarrhoea and abdominal pain in the pooled trials. If the opioid is being taken for chronic non-cancer pain, the honest conversation is about whether it is still helping at all - see the chronic pain page on this site.

Who should be careful: For the mu-opioid antagonists used to treat this: known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction or a history of it, and any situation where perforation is a possibility. Methylnaltrexone and related agents are avoided where the gut wall integrity is in question. For the opioids themselves, prescribing decisions belong with a clinician and are outside the scope of this entry.

Interactions: Anything else that slows the bowel adds to the effect: antispasmodics (hyoscine, dicyclomine), tricyclic antidepressants including low-dose amitriptyline, sedating antihistamines, and loperamide - several of which are separately graded on this same page, which is exactly the kind of stacking that is easy to miss. Ondansetron for opioid-related nausea also constipates. Conversely, mu-opioid antagonists can precipitate opioid withdrawal symptoms in some people, and the Cochrane evidence on that was of very low certainty.

What the evidence says

How common: Moore and McQuay 2005 pooled 34 randomised trials of oral opioids in non-cancer pain (5,546 patients, 4,212 contributing adverse-event data). Any oral opioid produced higher adverse-event rates than placebo; the commonest were dry mouth (25%), nausea (21%) and constipation (15%), and 22% of patients on opioids withdrew because of adverse events. IMPORTANT LIMITATION the authors state themselves: most of these trials ran under four weeks and few titrated the dose, so the figures have limited applicability to long-term use - which is exactly how opioids are taken for chronic pain. Treat 15% as a floor, not an estimate. What works: Ford and colleagues 2013 pooled 14 RCTs of mu-opioid receptor antagonists (4,101 patients) - superior to placebo for opioid-induced constipation, relative risk of failure to respond 0.69 (95% CI 0.63-0.75); methylnaltrexone 0.66 (0.54-0.84, 6 RCTs, 1,610 patients), naloxone 0.64 (0.56-0.72, 4 trials, 798), alvimopan 0.71 (0.65-0.78, 4 RCTs, 1,693). Total adverse events, diarrhoea and abdominal pain were all commoner with active therapy, but reversal of analgesia did NOT occur more often than with placebo. Cochrane 2022 (10 RCTs, 1,343 adults with cancer or in palliative care): oral naldemedine doubled the chance of spontaneous laxation over two weeks (RR 2.00, 95% CI 1.59-2.52, 2 trials, 418 participants, I-squared 0%, NNT for benefit 3, 95% CI 3-4; moderate-certainty evidence), with little to no difference in serious adverse events (RR 3.34, 0.85-13.15, low certainty) but over double the rate of non-serious adverse events. All trials in that review were vulnerable to bias, most often through unreported blinding of outcome assessors.

Doses studied: No dose threshold makes this safe: the gut effect is a direct receptor action, present across the trials regardless of which oral opioid was used. The adverse-event review covered all oral opioids in non-cancer pain, with about 90% of patients taking them for moderate rather than severe pain, so this is not a phenomenon confined to high-dose or end-of-life prescribing. The reversal agents were studied at standard doses; notably, in one trial a lower dose of naldemedine (0.1 mg) produced fewer spontaneous laxations than a higher dose (0.4 mg), RR 0.69 (0.53-0.89).

Why this grade: A HARM edge, like NSAIDs on this page. Opioids are not a treatment for digestive symptoms - they are a direct and predictable cause of them, through an effect on opioid receptors in the gut wall. In a systematic review of 34 randomised trials of oral opioids in non-cancer pain (5,546 patients), constipation affected 15% of participants and 22% of all patients withdrew because of adverse events. The effect is established firmly enough that opioid-induced constipation has its own name, its own diagnostic criteria and its own drug class: 14 RCTs of mu-opioid receptor antagonists in 4,101 patients found them superior to placebo (relative risk of failing to respond 0.69, 95% CI 0.63-0.75), and a 2022 Cochrane review found naldemedine doubled the chance of spontaneous laxation with a number needed to treat of just 3. You do not build a drug class to reverse an effect that is not real. Adequate evidence of net harm to the organ system this page is about makes this not_supported by the rule here. This is not a verdict on opioids for pain - that is graded separately on the chronic pain page, and it is also not favourable.
