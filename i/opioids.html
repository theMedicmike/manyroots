Conventional medicine

Opioids for chronic non-cancer pain

Not supported by evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Opioids are the intervention where the research most clearly contradicts the intuition that a stronger drug means better relief. In the largest and longest trial, people given opioids for a year did no better on daily functioning than people given non-opioid medicines — and their pain scores were slightly worse, with more side effects. Guidelines now urge real caution about starting them for ongoing joint or back pain. If you are already taking opioids, this is a conversation to have with your prescriber, not a reason to stop on your own, because stopping suddenly can be genuinely dangerous.

Safety

This is the highest-harm intervention in this group. Randomised data show vomiting in 5.9% versus 2.3% on placebo, withdrawal due to adverse events 2.64 times more likely with tramadol than placebo in osteoarthritis, and serious adverse events in 110/2459 versus 22/1153. In chronic low back pain the number needed to harm (5) was lower than the number needed to treat for meaningful benefit (16). Beyond trial-captured harms, long-term use carries risks the trials were too short and too selective to measure — Cochrane specifically notes the included trials did not report addiction, overdose, sleep-disordered breathing, opioid-induced hyperalgesia or hypogonadism. Expect constipation, sedation, nausea, cognitive slowing and increased fall and fracture risk in older adults. Never stop long-term opioids abruptly: abrupt discontinuation or rapid tapering carries its own serious risks and must be managed with a prescriber. Ask about a take-home naloxone supply if you or anyone in your household uses opioids.

Who should be careful: Should not be initiated without a prescriber's individualised risk assessment. Particularly hazardous in people with untreated sleep apnea or significant respiratory disease, a personal or family history of substance use disorder, active depression or suicidality, and in older adults at fall risk. Avoid in pregnancy where possible because of neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome. Tramadol carries additional seizure risk and should be avoided in people with a seizure disorder or on drugs that lower the seizure threshold.

Interactions: Combining opioids with benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedative-hypnotics, muscle relaxants or alcohol substantially increases the risk of respiratory depression and fatal overdose — this is the single most important interaction. Tramadol and tapentadol add serotonin syndrome risk when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs including duloxetine, triptans, linezolid or MAOIs. Opioids compound constipation with anticholinergics. Codeine and tramadol depend on CYP2D6 metabolism, so effects vary unpredictably between individuals and with CYP2D6-inhibiting drugs such as fluoxetine, paroxetine and bupropion.

What the evidence says

Evidence for opioids in chronic musculoskeletal pain shows small short-term effects that do not persist or outperform alternatives. In the SPACE randomised trial, 240 patients with chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis were followed 12 months: pain-related function did not differ (mean BPI interference 3.4 opioid vs 3.3 non-opioid; difference 0.1, 95% CI -0.5 to 0.7), pain intensity was significantly better in the non-opioid group (4.0 vs 3.5; difference 0.5, 95% CI 0.0 to 1.0), and medication-related adverse symptoms were significantly more common with opioids (1.8 vs 0.9; difference 0.9, 95% CI 0.3 to 1.5). A meta-analysis of 96 RCTs (26,169 participants) found opioids reduced pain by 0.69 cm (95% CI -0.82 to -0.56) on a 10-cm scale where the minimally important difference is 1 cm, improved SF-36 physical function by 2.04 points where 5 is the threshold, and produced comparable pain relief to NSAIDs (WMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.54 to 0.34). In the osteoarthritis network meta-analysis, all opioids had 53% probability or less of exceeding the minimal clinically important pain reduction, while 83.3% showed increased dropout due to adverse events and 89.5% increased any adverse event. A responder analysis of chronic low back pain trials found the number needed to harm (5) was lower than the number needed to treat for meaningful benefit (16). Cochrane data in chronic low back pain do show short-term efficacy versus placebo (strong opioids SMD -0.43, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.33) but note there are no placebo-controlled RCTs establishing long-term effectiveness or safety.

Doses studied: SPACE used a treat-to-target strategy starting with immediate-release morphine, oxycodone, or hydrocodone/acetaminophen, titrated by response. Meta-analyses pooled tramadol across 37.5-400 mg/day and strong opioids (morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, oxymorphone and tapentadol) across a wide dose range, with transdermal buprenorphine analysed separately; the osteoarthritis network meta-analysis examined 19 opioid preparations or doses, with oxymorphone 80 mg/day the most harmful.

Why this grade: Adequate evidence exists and does not support opioids for chronic musculoskeletal pain. The pivotal 12-month pragmatic randomised trial (SPACE) found opioids not superior to non-opioid medication on pain-related function, with pain intensity actually favouring the non-opioid arm and more adverse symptoms in the opioid arm; its authors stated the results do not support initiating opioid therapy for chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis. A 96-trial meta-analysis found the average benefit versus placebo (-0.69 cm on a 10-cm scale) falls below the 1-cm minimally important difference, and no better than NSAIDs. A 192-trial osteoarthritis network meta-analysis found every opioid had 53% probability or less of exceeding the clinically important threshold, while 83-89% carried increased adverse event and dropout risk, concluding the clinical benefit does not outweigh the harm. In chronic low back pain a responder analysis found patients were more likely to quit because of a side effect (number needed to harm 5) than to gain a meaningful benefit (NNT 16). Short-term placebo-controlled trials do show small statistical benefit, which is why some clinicians still use opioids selectively, but that does not lift the grade above not_supported for this condition. Nothing here is a reason for anyone currently on long-term opioids to stop abruptly.

Sources (7)
  1. Effect of Opioid vs Nonopioid Medications on Pain-Related Function in Patients With Chronic Back Pain or Hip or Knee Osteoarthritis Pain: The SPACE Randomized Clinical Trial.
    Krebs EE, Gravely A, Nugent S, et al., 2018 (JAMA) · PMID 29509867 · null

    Pragmatic 12-month randomised trial, 240 patients (mean age 58.3, 97.5% completed) with moderate to severe chronic back pain or hip or knee osteoarthritis pain. Groups did not differ on pain-related function over 12 months (BPI interference 3.4 opioid vs 3.3 non-opioid; difference 0.1, 95% CI -0.5 to 0.7; P=.58). Pain intensity was significantly better in the non-opioid group (BPI severity 4.0 vs 3.5; difference 0.5, 95% CI 0.0 to 1.0; P=.03), and medication-related adverse symptoms were significantly more common with opioids (1.8 vs 0.9; difference 0.9, 95% CI 0.3 to 1.5; P=.03). Authors concluded the results do not support initiating opioid therapy for these conditions.

  2. Opioids for Chronic Noncancer Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Busse JW, Wang L, Kamaleldin M, et al., 2018 (JAMA) · PMID 30561481 · mixed

    96 RCTs, 26,169 participants. Versus placebo, opioids reduced pain by a weighted mean difference of -0.69 cm (95% CI -0.82 to -0.56) on a 10-cm visual analogue scale where the minimally important difference is 1 cm, with an 11.9% (95% CI 9.7% to 14.1%) modelled risk difference for achieving that difference. Physical function improved 2.04 points (95% CI 1.41 to 2.68) on the SF-36 physical component score where the MID is 5. Vomiting occurred in 5.9% with opioids versus 2.3% with placebo. Pain relief was statistically indistinguishable from NSAIDs (WMD -0.60, 95% CI -1.54 to 0.34).

  3. Effectiveness and safety of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioid treatment for knee and hip osteoarthritis: network meta-analysis.
    da Costa BR, Pereira TV, Saadat P, et al., 2021 (BMJ) · PMID 34642179 · negative

    192 trials, 102,829 participants, 19 opioid preparations or doses. All opioids had a probability of 53% or less of producing a more pronounced treatment effect than the minimal clinically relevant reduction in pain. 83.3% of opioid preparations showed an increased risk of dropout due to adverse events and 89.5% an increased risk of any adverse event; oxymorphone 80 mg/day was worst (51% dropouts, 88% any adverse event). Authors concluded the clinical benefit of opioid treatment, regardless of preparation or dose, does not outweigh the harm it might cause in osteoarthritis.

  4. PEER systematic review of randomized controlled trials: Management of chronic low back pain in primary care.
    Kolber MR, Ton J, Thomas B, et al., 2021 (Can Fam Physician) · PMID 33483410 · negative

    63 RCTs reporting responder analyses across 15 treatments for chronic low back pain. Patients treated with opioids had a greater likelihood of discontinuing treatment owing to an adverse event (number needed to harm 5) than of continuing treatment to derive any clinically meaningful benefit (NNT 16). By contrast, moderate-certainty evidence supported exercise (RR 1.71, 95% CI 1.37 to 2.15; NNT 7), oral NSAIDs (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.78; NNT 6) and duloxetine (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.38; NNT 10), with exercise the only intervention showing sustained benefit to 48 weeks.

  5. Tramadol for osteoarthritis.
    Toupin April K, Bisaillon J, Welch V, et al., 2019 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 31132298 · null

    22 RCTs; 3871 participants randomised to tramadol alone or with acetaminophen. Moderate-quality evidence of no important benefit on pain (4% absolute improvement, 95% CI 3% to 5%) or physical function (4%, 95% CI 2% to 6%) versus placebo; 15 per 100 improved by 20% on tramadol versus 10 per 100 on placebo. Withdrawal due to adverse events was markedly higher (RR 2.64, 95% CI 2.17 to 3.20), and serious adverse events occurred in 110/2459 on tramadol versus 22/1153 on placebo (RR 1.78, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.84).

  6. Opioids compared to placebo or other treatments for chronic low-back pain.
    Chaparro LE, Furlan AD, Deshpande A, Mailis-Gagnon A, Atlas S, Turk DC, 2013 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 23983011 · positive

    15 trials, 5540 participants, minimum 4 weeks' duration. Tramadol was better than placebo for pain (SMD -0.55, 95% CI -0.66 to -0.44; low quality) and function (SMD -0.18, 95% CI -0.29 to -0.07). Strong opioids (morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, oxymorphone, tapentadol) were better than placebo for pain (SMD -0.43, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.33) and function (SMD -0.26, 95% CI -0.37 to -0.15; moderate quality). Transdermal buprenorphine, analysed separately, showed no difference from placebo on function (SMD -0.14, 95% CI -0.53 to 0.25; very low quality). Two trials found no difference between opioids and antidepressants. Trials had high drop-out rates and short duration, did not report addiction, overdose, sleep apnea, hyperalgesia or hypogonadism, and the authors state there are no placebo-RCTs supporting long-term effectiveness or safety.

  7. CDC Clinical Practice Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Pain - United States, 2022.
    Dowell D, Ragan KR, Jones CM, Baldwin GT, Chou R, 2022 (MMWR Recomm Rep) · PMID 36327391 · mixed

    GRADE-based national guideline for outpatients aged 18 and over, updating the 2016 CDC guideline and covering acute (under 1 month), subacute (1-3 months) and chronic (over 3 months) pain; it does not apply to sickle cell disease, cancer, or palliative and end-of-life care. It structures decisions around four areas: whether to initiate opioids at all, opioid and dose selection, duration and follow-up, and assessing risk and addressing potential harms including opioid use disorder, overdose and death. The guideline explicitly warns that its recommendations should not be applied as inflexible standards of care across patient populations.
