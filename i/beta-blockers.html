Beta blockers

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache Not supported by evidence · for high blood pressure

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For migraine & headache

ImportantNever stop a beta blocker suddenly. Rebound withdrawal can trigger angina, an abnormal heart rhythm or a heart attack, and it must be tapered with a prescriber.

Propranolol is an old, cheap blood-pressure tablet that has been used for decades to make migraines less frequent. The evidence that it beats a dummy pill is real and has been found more than once, but the size of the benefit is small - roughly one and a half fewer headaches a month - and much of the research is old and not very well done. There is essentially no good trial evidence for it in chronic migraine (15 or more headache days a month).

Safety

Common effects are fatigue, exercise intolerance, cold hands and feet, dizziness or lightheadedness from low blood pressure or slow pulse, sleep disturbance and vivid dreams, and reduced libido. Beta blockers can mask the warning signs of hypoglycaemia (tremor, palpitations) in people using insulin or sulfonylureas, and can blunt heart-rate response to exercise. Depression and mood flattening are reported, though the association is debated. Abrupt discontinuation after regular use can cause rebound tachycardia, hypertension and, in people with coronary disease, angina or infarction - taper rather than stopping suddenly. The high dropout rates that made the older trials methodologically unsatisfactory are themselves an honest signal about tolerability. Propranolol crosses into breast milk in small amounts and is generally considered compatible with breastfeeding, but discuss with a clinician.

Who should be careful: Asthma and reactive airway disease (non-selective beta blockade can provoke bronchospasm); severe COPD; second- or third-degree heart block or sick sinus syndrome without a pacemaker; symptomatic bradycardia; decompensated heart failure; severe hypotension; severe peripheral arterial disease or Raynaud phenomenon; untreated phaeochromocytoma. Use caution in diabetes with hypoglycaemia unawareness, in depression, and in pregnancy (beta blockers are associated with fetal growth restriction and neonatal bradycardia and hypoglycaemia - a clinician decision).

Interactions: Additive bradycardia and heart block with verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin and amiodarone - the combination with intravenous verapamil is particularly hazardous. Propranolol raises rizatriptan levels, so a rizatriptan dose reduction is advised. Additive hypotension with other antihypertensives and nitrates. May increase levels of drugs cleared by CYP2D6 and CYP1A2 (and propranolol levels rise with fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, paroxetine and quinidine). Can blunt the effect of, and prolong reactions to, adrenaline. NSAIDs can reduce the antihypertensive effect.

What the evidence says

Beta blockers are daily PREVENTIVES with no role in treating an attack. The largest synthesis - 108 RCTs including 50 placebo-controlled trials - found propranolol reduced episodic migraine headaches by 1.5 per month at 8 weeks (95% CI -2.3 to -0.65) and increased the chance of a 50%-or-greater reduction (RR 1.4, 1.1-1.7), with trial sequential analysis indicating these results were unlikely to be a type I error and a network analysis suggesting the benefit is a beta-blocker class effect. An independent 2015 network meta-analysis of placebo-controlled prophylaxis trials, including 39 beta-blocker trials, put propranolol at -1.3 headaches/month (95% CI -2.0 to -0.62) and metoprolol at -0.94 (-1.4 to -0.46), broadly agreeing. The 2019 review rated the placebo-controlled episodic migraine evidence high quality but rated everything else low: comparative trials against flunarizine, topiramate and valproate were largely single, underpowered studies, so no conclusion about relative effectiveness is possible. The Cochrane review of 58 trials and 5,072 participants reached the same conclusion - clear short-term superiority over placebo across 26 placebo comparisons - but flagged unsatisfactory methodological quality with high dropout rates poorly reported and handled, no long-term follow-up data, and sample sizes insufficient to establish equivalence with comparators; that review is now marked WITHDRAWN in the Cochrane Library and its literature search stopped in May 2003. Evidence in chronic migraine is close to absent rather than negative: a 2023 network meta-analysis and a 2024 NIHR health technology assessment both searched for propranolol trials in chronic migraine and found none meeting inclusion criteria, because studies were too small, predated the definition of chronic migraine, or did not define it. Evidence in the adjacent condition of tension-type headache is a single trial. The 2025 ACP guideline evaluated metoprolol and propranolol among eight drug classes for initiating preventive monotherapy in episodic migraine, but all three of its recommendations are conditional and based on low-certainty evidence.

Doses studied: Oral propranolol and metoprolol taken daily for PREVENTION, not during an attack. The pooled reviews retrieved report outcomes at 8 weeks without specifying a single standard dose; the 2015 network meta-analysis pools placebo-controlled trials of propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol and timolol without a single common dose either. Metoprolol and propranolol were the two beta blockers ACP evaluated in 2025.

Why this grade: A 2019 systematic review of 108 RCTs found propranolol clearly beats placebo for episodic migraine prevention, with trial sequential analysis arguing against chance, and an earlier network meta-analysis of 39 placebo-controlled beta-blocker trials independently agrees. Graded down from strong because the effect is small (about 1.5 fewer headaches per month), the Cochrane review of 58 trials judged methodological quality unsatisfactory and is now marked WITHDRAWN with a literature search that stopped in 2003, comparative trials were single and underpowered, two 2023-2024 evidence syntheses found NO eligible propranolol trials in chronic migraine at all, and the 2025 ACP guideline rates all preventive recommendations conditional with low-certainty evidence.

Sources (6)
  1. Beta-blockers for the prevention of headache in adults, a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisJackson et al., 2019 (PLoS One) · PMID 30893319 · positive

    108 RCTs (50 placebo-controlled, 58 comparative effectiveness). Propranolol reduced episodic migraine headaches by 1.5 per month at 8 weeks (95% CI -2.3 to -0.65) and was more likely to produce a 50% reduction (RR 1.4, 1.1-1.7); trial sequential analysis found these unlikely to be type I error. Benefit appeared to be a beta-blocker class effect. In chronic migraine the estimate was borderline (RR 2.0, 95% CI 1.0-4.3). Trials comparing beta-blockers to other interventions were largely single, underpowered trials rated low quality, making definitive conclusions about comparative effectiveness impossible. Only one trial existed for tension-type headache.

  2. A Comparative Effectiveness Meta-Analysis of Drugs for the Prophylaxis of Migraine Headache.
    Network meta-analysisJackson et al., 2015 (PLoS One) · PMID 26172390 · positive

    Network meta-analysis of placebo-controlled migraine prophylaxis trials, including 39 beta-blocker trials and 53 head-to-head trials. Among drugs with at least 3 trials that beat placebo for episodic migraine: propranolol -1.3 headaches/month (95% CI -2.0 to -0.62) and metoprolol -0.94 (-1.4 to -0.46), alongside amitriptyline (SMD -1.2, -1.7 to -0.82), topiramate (-1.1, -1.9 to -0.73) and valproate (-1.5, -2.1 to -0.8). Several beta-blockers (atenolol, bisoprolol, timolol) were effective on fewer than 3 trials. Authors conclude selection should be tailored to patient preferences, characteristics and side-effect profiles rather than to a clear efficacy winner.

  3. WITHDRAWN: Propranolol for migraine prophylaxis.
    Systematic reviewLinde & Rossnagel, 2017 withdrawal record (Cochrane; literature search to May 2003) · PMID 28212466 · mixed

    58 trials, 5,072 participants; 26 placebo comparisons and 47 comparisons with other drugs. The 26 placebo-controlled trials showed clear short-term effects of propranolol over placebo, but the review states the methodological quality of the majority of trials was unsatisfactory, principally high dropout rates insufficiently reported and handled in the analysis. No long-term follow-up data existed, so it is unclear whether effects are stable after stopping. Comparisons with calcium antagonists, other beta-blockers and other drugs yielded no clear-cut differences, with sample sizes insufficient in most trials to establish equivalence. The record is marked WITHDRAWN in the Cochrane Library and its searches ran only to May 2003.

  4. Prevention of Episodic Migraine Headache Using Pharmacologic Treatments in Outpatient Settings: A Clinical Guideline From the American College of Physicians.
    GuidelineQaseem et al., 2025 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 39899861 · mixed

    Guideline for adults with 1-14 headache days per month. Beta-blockers (metoprolol, propranolol) were among the eight drug classes evaluated for initiating preventive monotherapy, alongside antiseizure medications, CGRP antibodies, gepants, ARBs, ACE inhibitors, SSRIs/SNRIs and amitriptyline. All three ACP recommendations have conditional strength and low-certainty evidence - a direct statement that no preventive drug has high-certainty support.

  5. Preventive drug treatments for adults with chronic migraine: a systematic review with economic modelling.
    Network meta-analysisMistry et al., 2024 (Health Technol Assess) · PMID 39365169 · null

    Systematic review of preventive drugs in chronic migraine (11 RCTs, 7,352 adults). The authors state they found no trials of the commonly used drugs propranolol or amitriptyline that met inclusion criteria, and that topiramate was the only oral drug for which data could be included. Their stated limitation is that there was not sufficient quality evidence to support the use of other oral drugs in chronic migraine.

  6. Clinical effectiveness of pharmacological interventions for managing chronic migraine in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisNaghdi et al., 2023 (J Headache Pain) · PMID 38057728 · null

    No eligible studies were identified for propranolol (or amitriptyline or candesartan) in chronic migraine. The reasons given were that the studies did not define chronic migraine, were undertaken before the definition of chronic migraine existed, or were too small - meaning the relative effectiveness of propranolol against the CGRP antibodies, onabotulinumtoxinA and topiramate cannot currently be determined.

For high blood pressure

ImportantNever stop a beta blocker suddenly. Rebound withdrawal can trigger angina, an abnormal heart rhythm or a heart attack, and it must be tapered with a prescriber.

Beta blockers lower blood pressure, and compared with taking nothing they do prevent some cardiovascular events. But lined up against the other blood-pressure drugs they come out behind - more strokes, and no survival advantage. That is why they are no longer a first choice for high blood pressure on its own. This is not a verdict on beta blockers generally: if you are also being treated for heart failure, a previous heart attack, angina or a fast heart rhythm, a beta blocker may be exactly the right drug for you, and propranolol has real evidence for preventing migraine. Never stop one suddenly.

Safety

Common effects are tiredness, cold hands and feet, reduced exercise capacity and a slow pulse; in the Cochrane review people were 41 percent more likely to stop a beta blocker for side effects than a renin-angiotensin system inhibitor (RR 1.41, 1.29-1.54). Never stop a beta blocker abruptly - sudden withdrawal can trigger rebound angina, arrhythmia or a heart attack, and it must be tapered under medical supervision. In people over 65 the stroke signal is specific to atenolol.

Who should be careful: Severe or uncontrolled asthma and reactive airways disease (risk of bronchospasm); second- or third-degree heart block and severe bradycardia without a pacemaker; decompensated heart failure (beta blockers are used in stable heart failure, but starting or increasing one during decompensation is harmful); severe peripheral arterial disease; untreated pheochromocytoma.

Interactions: Verapamil and diltiazem - additive bradycardia and AV block, a combination that can be dangerous. Insulin and sulfonylureas - beta blockers blunt the warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia such as tremor and palpitations, so low blood sugar can arrive without warning; this matters for anyone also being treated for type 2 diabetes. Clonidine - stopping clonidine while on a beta blocker can cause severe rebound hypertension. Digoxin and amiodarone - additive slowing of the heart. NSAIDs can blunt the blood-pressure-lowering effect.

What the evidence says

Cochrane 2017 (Wiysonge, 13 RCTs): vs placebo - all-cause mortality RR 0.99 (0.88-1.11, moderate certainty); total CVD RR 0.88 (0.79-0.97, low certainty); stroke RR 0.80 (0.66-0.96, low certainty); CHD RR 0.93 (0.81-1.07). vs CCBs - mortality RR 1.07 (1.00-1.14); CVD RR 1.18 (1.08-1.29); stroke RR 1.24 (1.11-1.40). vs RAS inhibitors - stroke RR 1.30 (1.11-1.53); discontinuation for adverse events RR 1.41 (1.29-1.54). In the one trial of people 65 and over, atenolol raised coronary heart disease versus diuretics (RR 1.63, 1.15-2.32). Thomopoulos 2020 (84 trials, 165,850): in the 5 trials confined to hypertensive patients (18,724 people, 5.1 years, baseline 163/94 mmHg) a -10.5/-7.0 mmHg fall came with 22 percent fewer major cardiovascular events (95% CI 6-34) - but across 24 head-to-head comparisons beta blockers were about 20 percent worse for stroke and 6 percent worse for all-cause death. Age matters: Kuyper 2014 (21 trials, 145,811) found atenolol RAISED stroke in the elderly (RR 1.17, 1.05-1.30) but LOWERED it in people under 60 (RR 0.78, 0.64-0.95). Drug choice may matter too: a 2017 network meta-analysis (13 trials, 90,935) found lipophilic beta blockers (metoprolol, propranolol, oxprenolol) had lower cardiovascular mortality than atenolol (OR 0.72, 0.54-0.97). SURROGATE ONLY: Cochrane 2016 (56 RCTs, 7,812) put the blood-pressure fall at -10/-8 mmHg with heart rate down 11 bpm, on low-quality evidence - a number moving, not an event prevented. No outcome trials exist for the newer vasodilating beta blockers such as nebivolol.

Doses studied: The outcome trials were dominated by atenolol 50-100 mg daily; three-quarters of all beta-blocker participants in the Cochrane review took atenolol. Other agents in the trials included metoprolol, propranolol and oxprenolol. Trials ran at least one year, with the hypertension-only comparisons averaging about 5 years of follow-up. No outcome trial has tested nebivolol or other newer vasodilating beta blockers for hypertension.

Why this grade: Scoped to beta blockers as a FIRST-CHOICE drug for high blood pressure with no other reason to take them. The evidence is large and adequate, and it is null on the outcome that matters: in the 2017 Cochrane review (13 RCTs, 40,245 people on beta blockers, three-quarters of them on atenolol) all-cause mortality versus placebo was RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.88-1.11) - no mortality benefit, on moderate-certainty evidence. Total cardiovascular disease was reduced versus placebo (RR 0.88, 0.79-0.97) but only on LOW-certainty evidence, and coronary heart disease was unchanged (RR 0.93, 0.81-1.07). Against active comparators beta blockers were consistently worse: versus calcium-channel blockers, mortality RR 1.07 (1.00-1.14), total CVD RR 1.18 (1.08-1.29) and stroke RR 1.24 (1.11-1.40); versus renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, stroke RR 1.30 (1.11-1.53). The Cochrane authors conclude the effects are inferior to those of other antihypertensive drugs, and note that most of the trials carry a high risk of bias. A separate 2020 meta-analysis of 84 trials (165,850 people) found beta blockers about 20 percent worse than other agents for stroke and 6 percent worse for all-cause death. Guidelines no longer place them first-line for uncomplicated hypertension. Adequate evidence that is null on mortality, inferior on stroke, and recommended against by guidelines is not_supported by the rule on this site - even though these drugs do lower blood pressure and do beat placebo on composite events.

  1. Beta-blockers for hypertension.
    Meta-analysisWiysonge CS, Bradley HA, Volmink J, Mayosi BM, Opie LH; Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2017 · PMID 28107561 · mixed

    13 RCTs, 40,245 participants on beta blockers (three-quarters atenolol). All-cause mortality vs placebo RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.88-1.11). Total CVD vs placebo RR 0.88 (0.79-0.97, low certainty); stroke RR 0.80 (0.66-0.96); CHD RR 0.93 (0.81-1.07). Worse than CCBs for mortality (RR 1.07, 1.00-1.14), CVD (RR 1.18, 1.08-1.29) and stroke (RR 1.24, 1.11-1.40); worse than RAS inhibitors for stroke (RR 1.30, 1.11-1.53).   2. Beta-blockers in hypertension: overview and meta-analysis of randomized outcome trials.
    Meta-analysisThomopoulos C, Bazoukis G, Tsioufis C, Mancia G; J Hypertens 2020 · PMID 32649628 · mixed

    84 trials, 165,850 patients. In 5 trials restricted to hypertensive patients (18,724 people, 5.1 years, baseline 163/94 mmHg), -10.5/-7.0 mmHg accompanied a 22 percent reduction in major cardiovascular events (95% CI 6-34). But across 24 comparison trials (103,764 patients) beta blockers were less protective than other agents, with averaged risk ratio increases of 20 percent for stroke and 6 percent for all-cause death.

  3. Atenolol vs nonatenolol β-blockers for the treatment of hypertension: a meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisKuyper LM, Khan NA; Can J Cardiol 2014 · PMID 24750981 · mixed

    21 hypertension trials, 145,811 participants. In the elderly, atenolol raised stroke risk versus other antihypertensives (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.05-1.30). In people under 60, atenolol reduced stroke versus other agents (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.64-0.95). Non-atenolol beta blockers in the elderly: RR 1.22 (0.99-1.50), not statistically significant. No trials of newer-generation beta blockers were found.

  4. Comparative efficacy of β-blockers on mortality and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with hypertension: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisZhang Y, Sun N, Jiang X, Xi Y; J Am Soc Hypertens 2017 · PMID 28760243 · positive

    13 trials, 90,935 participants. Lipophilic beta blockers (metoprolol, propranolol, oxprenolol) had lower cardiovascular mortality than the hydrophilic atenolol (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54-0.97), with a non-significant trend for all-cause mortality (OR 0.86, 0.72-1.03). Suggests some of the class-level underperformance is driven by atenolol specifically.

  5. Blood pressure lowering efficacy of beta-1 selective beta blockers for primary hypertension.
    Meta-analysisWong GWK, Boyda HN, Wright JM; Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016 · PMID 26961574 · positive

    SURROGATE OUTCOME ONLY. 56 RCTs, 7,812 people with primary hypertension. Beta-1 selective blockers lowered blood pressure by an average of -10/-8 mmHg and heart rate by 11 bpm versus placebo, on low-quality evidence. Unlike other drug classes they did not reduce pulse pressure. A blood-pressure reduction is not an event prevented.
