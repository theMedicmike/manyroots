For chronic pain

Moving your body regularly - walking, strength work, gentle stretching, whatever you will actually keep doing - is the single best-studied thing for long-lasting muscle and joint pain, and it is what medical guidelines tell doctors to try first. It usually takes the edge off pain rather than removing it, and the benefit fades if you stop. Be aware that in hip arthritis, where exercise has been tested against a convincing dummy treatment, the extra benefit on pain was too small to be sure of. No particular type of exercise clearly beats the others, so the one you enjoy and will stick with is the right one.

Safety

Generally very safe, and the harms are well characterised rather than merely unreported. In the chronic low back pain Cochrane review, one or more adverse effects occurred in 37 of 112 exercise groups (33%) versus 12 of 42 comparison groups (29%), mostly minor (e.g. muscle soreness); among the 12 studies measuring harms systematically, the median was 0.14 events per participant in exercise groups versus 0.12 in comparison groups. In the knee osteoarthritis review, withdrawal rates were comparable (14% exercise vs 15% control, OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.15), eight studies reported increased knee or low back pain attributed to the intervention, and no study reported a serious adverse event. The 2026 Cochrane hip review rated the evidence on adverse events as very uncertain, while finding that adding exercise to an existing treatment probably slightly reduced adverse events (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.97). Some soreness in the first few weeks is expected and typically settles.

Who should be careful: New, severe or rapidly worsening pain, or pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, bowel or bladder change, or progressive numbness or weakness, needs medical assessment before starting, since these can signal a specific cause rather than non-specific chronic pain. People with cardiac or respiratory disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or uncontrolled diabetes should get clinician clearance before vigorous exercise. Recent fracture, joint replacement or spinal surgery requires a supervised, staged return.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Exercise lowers blood glucose, so people using insulin or sulfonylureas may need dose or monitoring adjustments. Opioids, sedatives, benzodiazepines and some antihypertensives impair balance and blood-pressure regulation and raise fall risk during balance-demanding or vigorous activity.

What the evidence says

The 2021 Cochrane review of exercise for chronic low back pain pooled 249 randomised trials and found moderate-certainty evidence of a clinically important pain reduction versus no treatment, usual care or placebo (MD -15.2 points on a 0-100 scale, 95% CI -18.3 to -12.2); the effect on functional limitations was smaller (MD -6.8, 95% CI -8.3 to -5.3) and did not meet the review's prespecified 10-point threshold for clinical importance, and no difference was found versus manual therapy (MD 1.0, 95% CI -3.1 to 5.1). For knee osteoarthritis, the 2015 Cochrane review extracted data from 54 studies and found high-quality evidence from 44 trials (3537 participants) that land-based exercise reduced pain (SMD -0.49, 95% CI -0.39 to -0.59), equivalent to about 12 points on a 0-100 scale, but the sustained effect at 2-6 months fell to about 6 points (SMD -0.24, 95% CI -0.35 to -0.14). The picture is weaker at the hip: the 2026 Cochrane hip-osteoarthritis review (18 studies, 1368 participants) found that against an attention control or placebo, exercise may have little to no effect on pain (MD -6.31 on 0-100, 95% CI -12.98 to 0.35; low certainty), and that although exercise probably reduces pain (MD -7.19, 95% CI -10.70 to -3.68) and improves function (MD -8.79, 95% CI -12.00 to -5.41) versus no treatment or usual care, those improvements are unlikely to be clinically meaningful. A Cochrane overview of 21 reviews (381 studies, 37,143 participants) rated the evidence as low quality because of small samples and short follow-up, and found pain effects were not consistent across conditions or time points. In fibromyalgia (chronic widespread pain), aerobic exercise gave low-quality evidence of a small pain reduction (MD -11.06, 95% CI -18.34 to -3.77) but little difference in fatigue (MD -6.48, 95% CI -14.33 to 1.38).

Doses studied: Trials varied widely in exercise type, delivery and dose, and no single exercise type proved superior. The Cochrane knee-osteoarthritis review found individually delivered programmes tended to produce larger effects than class-based or home-based programmes, though between-study heterogeneity was marked. Durability is the weak point: knee-osteoarthritis benefit was measured as sustained only two to six months after formal treatment stopped, and the Cochrane chronic-pain overview reported that planned follow-up was limited to less than one year in all but six of its 21 reviews.

Why this grade: Guideline first-line for both chronic low back pain (2017 ACP strong recommendation) and hip/knee osteoarthritis (2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation strong recommendation), backed by very large Cochrane meta-analyses (249 trials in back pain; 54 studies in knee osteoarthritis) showing a consistent direction of benefit on pain. Graded strong on the strength of that guideline and meta-analytic base, not on effect size, and the effect size is genuinely modest: the back-pain effect on function did not reach the review's clinical-importance threshold; knee-osteoarthritis benefits shrink substantially by 2-6 months after supervision ends; the 2026 Cochrane hip-osteoarthritis review found little to no effect on pain against an attention control or placebo and judged the gains over usual care unlikely to be clinically meaningful; and a Cochrane overview of 21 reviews rated the overall chronic-pain evidence base as low quality.

For high blood pressure

Moving your body regularly is one of the few non-drug things that reliably brings blood pressure down — usually about 4 to 8 points off the top number in the pooled trials, which is real but on average less than a blood pressure pill delivers. Walking, weights, intervals and even wall-sits all show an effect, so the best kind is the one you will actually keep doing. Three honest caveats: the benefit is much larger if your blood pressure is high to begin with and close to nothing if it is already normal, it fades if you stop, and these studies measured the blood pressure number itself, not whether people had fewer heart attacks or strokes.

Safety

Exercise is safe for the large majority of adults with elevated or stage 1/2 blood pressure, and the main real-world harm is musculoskeletal injury: in the Cochrane walking review only 21 of 73 trials reported adverse events at all, 16 of those reported none, and knee injury accounted for five of the eight events described. Heavy resistance work and sustained isometric holds transiently raise blood pressure sharply during the effort itself, so build up gradually rather than starting at maximal loads. Blood pressure that is very high and untreated should be brought under control before starting vigorous exercise.

Who should be careful: Do not start a vigorous or heavy-resistance programme without clinician clearance if you have uncontrolled severe hypertension, unstable angina, decompensated heart failure, an uncontrolled arrhythmia, known aortic aneurysm, or proliferative retinopathy. Anyone with established cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or new chest pain, breathlessness or fainting on exertion should be assessed first.

Interactions: Exercise adds to the effect of antihypertensive medication and can cause lightheadedness or fainting after a session, particularly with diuretics, alpha-blockers or nitrates; hydrate and cool down gradually. Beta-blockers blunt the heart-rate response, so heart-rate-based intensity targets will read falsely low — use perceived effort instead. People on insulin or sulfonylureas should watch for exercise-related hypoglycaemia.

What the evidence says

In the largest network meta-analysis (270 RCTs, 15,827 participants), every exercise mode lowered resting BP versus non-exercise control: aerobic training -4.49/-2.53 mmHg, dynamic resistance -4.55/-3.04 mmHg, combined training -6.04/-2.54 mmHg, HIIT -4.08/-2.50 mmHg and isometric training -8.24/-4.00 mmHg. A dose-response meta-analysis restricted to adults with hypertension (34 trials, 1,787 participants) found -1.78 mmHg systolic per additional 30 min/week of aerobic exercise, with the greatest decrement at 150 min/week (-7.23/-5.58 mmHg), though GRADE certainty was low for systolic BP. A Cochrane review of walking alone (73 trials, 5,763 participants; 5,060 in the systolic pooling) found -4.11 mmHg systolic at moderate certainty and -1.79 mmHg diastolic at low certainty. The 2013 AHA scientific statement grades aerobic exercise Class I, Level of Evidence A, dynamic resistance Class IIA/B and isometric handgrip only Class IIB, Level of Evidence C. Against this: how much you gain depends on where you start — a 64-study meta-analysis of dynamic resistance training (2,344 participants) found roughly 6/5 mmHg in samples with hypertension, 3/3 mmHg in prehypertension and about 0/1 mmHg in samples with normal blood pressure, and an overall effect of only -3.0/-2.1 mmHg. In a 391-trial network meta-analysis, antihypertensive medications lowered systolic BP by 3.96 mmHg more than exercise across all populations, only 56 of 197 exercise trials enrolled hypertensive participants, and risk of bias was higher in exercise trials — although within hypertensive populations the same analysis found no detectable difference between the major drug classes and endurance or dynamic resistance exercise. Isometric resistance training's large office effect (-7.47/-3.17 mmHg across 12 studies, 415 participants) did not produce significant reductions in 24-hour or daytime ambulatory blood pressure. And an older systematic review restricted to trials of at least six months' duration found no significant sustained exercise effect at all (net systolic -0.8 mmHg, 95% CI -5.9 to +4.2 in hypertensive participants; -0.2 mmHg, -2.8 to +2.4 in normotensive participants).

Doses studied: Aerobic: greatest effect at ~150 min/week (non-linear dose-response peak). Walking interventions averaged 153 min/week over an average 15 weeks. Dynamic resistance: pooled trials trained 2.8 (SD 0.6) days/week for 14.4 (SD 7.9) weeks at moderate intensity. Isometric: the pooled isometric reports do not specify a common protocol; the largest network meta-analysis identified the isometric wall squat as the most effective submode for systolic BP and running as the most effective for diastolic.

Why this grade: Multiple large meta-analyses and network meta-analyses of hundreds of RCTs show consistent blood pressure reduction across every exercise mode, and the American Heart Association grades aerobic exercise Class I, Level of Evidence A — its strongest category. Graded strong for blood pressure lowering only, not for cardiovascular event prevention, which these trials did not measure. Four caveats are carried in the summaries rather than reflected in the grade: medications outperform exercise on average across all populations, though not detectably within hypertensive populations; the size of the effect depends heavily on starting blood pressure and falls to roughly zero in people whose blood pressure is already normal; isometric training's large office-BP effect does not appear in 24-hour or daytime ambulatory monitoring, and isometric handgrip carries only a Class IIB, Level C guideline rating; and the one review restricted to trials of six months or longer found no significant sustained effect at all.

For anxiety & stress

Moving your body regularly — brisk walking, running, lifting weights — is linked to lower anxiety and stress across a very large body of studies, alongside all its other health benefits. The honest catch is that many of those studies are of poor quality, and in people whose anxiety is already high the results have been inconsistent: in one careful review, only 2 of 13 such trials clearly showed a benefit. It is a low-risk habit worth building, but for significant anxiety it works best alongside proven treatment rather than instead of it.

Safety

Exercise is among the safest interventions studied and carries broad physical-health benefits beyond mood. Be aware, though, that none of the four anxiety syntheses cited here reported adverse-event or dropout data, so tolerability within these specific trials is uncharacterised rather than demonstrated. The realistic risks are musculoskeletal — soreness and strain from doing too much too soon. Start gradually and build volume and intensity over weeks.

Who should be careful: People with known or suspected cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, recent surgery, or a long period of inactivity should get medical clearance before starting vigorous exercise. Stop and seek care for chest pain, unusual breathlessness, or fainting during exertion.

Interactions: No pharmacological drug interactions. Practical ones matter: beta-blockers blunt the heart-rate response, making heart-rate-based intensity targets unreliable, and people using insulin or sulfonylureas should plan for exercise-related drops in blood glucose.

What the evidence says

Evidence for exercise in adult anxiety and psychological distress is broad but of uneven quality. An umbrella review of 97 systematic reviews (1,039 trials; 128,119 participants) found physical activity had a medium effect on anxiety versus usual care (median effect size -0.42, IQR -0.66 to -0.26) and on psychological distress (-0.60, 95% CI -0.78 to -0.42), with higher-intensity activity associated with greater improvement — though 77 of the 97 reviews scored critically low on AMSTAR. A meta-analysis of 16 resistance-training articles (922 participants) found randomized resistance exercise significantly reduced anxiety symptoms versus non-active controls (delta = 0.31, 95% CI 0.17-0.44), with larger effects in healthy participants (0.50) than in those with a physical or mental illness (0.19). The picture is markedly weaker in people selected for high anxiety: a systematic review of 12 RCTs in anxious adults concluded that benefits were seen for select groups but that small samples, concurrent therapies and inadequate assessment of adherence and fitness precluded definitive conclusions, and its 2023 update of 25 RCTs (1,831 participants) found that among the 13 trials enrolling people with elevated anxiety, only 2 unequivocally reduced anxiety with exercise — leading the authors to conclude that considerable uncertainty remains about exercise's value for anxious individuals. None of these syntheses reported adverse-event data, so exercise tolerability in anxiety trials is uncharacterised rather than demonstrated.

Doses studied: Highly variable across trials and not specified in the syntheses cited here. The umbrella review found higher-intensity activity associated with greater anxiety improvement and effectiveness diminishing with longer-duration interventions; the resistance-training meta-analysis found effects did not vary significantly by program length, session duration, frequency, intensity, sex or age.

Why this grade: A large umbrella review of RCT meta-analyses and a resistance-training meta-analysis show consistent small-to-medium anxiety reductions, but the underlying evidence is methodologically shaky — 77 of 97 included reviews scored critically low on AMSTAR — and the picture is weakest exactly where it matters most: in trials that enrolled people selected for elevated anxiety, only 2 of 13 found anxiety unequivocally reduced. Broad but shaky positive evidence with a weak signal in anxious populations lands at moderate, not strong.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Moving regularly — a mix of brisk aerobic activity and strength training, ideally 150 minutes a week or more — reliably lowers your three-month average blood sugar (HbA1c) by roughly half a percentage point if you have type 2 diabetes. That is a real, repeatable change, but it is a lab number: no trial has shown that exercise on its own prevents heart attacks, strokes or deaths in people with diabetes, and the one large trial of diet-plus-activity that went looking found none. Short walks after meals flatten the after-meal sugar spike in small studies lasting days to weeks, which is promising rather than proven. Exercise is worth doing for many reasons, but it is not a replacement for any medicine your doctor has prescribed.

Safety

Exercise is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and a lower HbA1c from training is not a reason to stop, skip or reduce a dose — that decision belongs to the prescriber. Exercise trials in type 2 diabetes report more adverse events, mostly musculoskeletal, in the exercise arms than in controls, but no excess of serious events. Start low and build up; supervised programmes produced larger HbA1c reductions than unsupervised advice. One participant died during the 30-day washout period of the post-meal walking crossover; the investigators judged that participation did not contribute.

Who should be careful: Get medical clearance before starting vigorous or resistance training if you have chest pain, known heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, proliferative (advanced) diabetic retinopathy — heavy lifting and breath-holding can raise eye pressure and provoke bleeding — foot ulcers or severe peripheral neuropathy, where weight-bearing impact needs modification and careful footwear, or autonomic neuropathy, which blunts the heart-rate response and causes dizziness on standing. The DARE trial required a negative stress test or cardiologist clearance before enrolment.

Interactions: Insulin and sulfonylureas raise the risk of hypoglycaemia during exercise and for many hours afterwards - the risk extends overnight, so an evening or unusually hard session can cause a low while you sleep (PMID 32397690). Monitor glucose, check before bed after a hard session, carry fast-acting carbohydrate, and ask the prescriber about dose adjustment. Beta-blockers can mask hypoglycaemia symptoms and blunt heart-rate targets. SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, ertugliflozin and similar) need a separate mention: ketoacidosis on these drugs is rare, but extensive exercise is listed among its recognised precipitants, alongside severe acute illness, dehydration, low-carbohydrate diets and insulin omission or dose reduction (PMID 28003053) - and it can occur with a normal-looking glucose reading, so a reassuring meter result does not rule it out. If you take one, do not stack a big jump in training on top of fasted sessions, cutting carbohydrate or skipping meals, keep fluids up, read the euglycaemic DKA warning on this page, and ask your prescriber for sick-day and exercise rules before you start. In type 1 diabetes exercise is beneficial but glucose management around it is more complex, and this evidence base should not be applied directly.

What the evidence says

All of the glycaemic evidence below is surrogate evidence — HbA1c, fasting glucose, fitness and postprandial glucose curves — not prevented heart attacks, strokes, amputations, kidney failure or deaths. In established type 2 diabetes, a meta-analysis of 47 RCTs (8538 patients, at least 12 weeks) found structured exercise training lowered HbA1c by -0.67 percentage points (95% CI -0.84 to -0.49) versus control, with aerobic -0.73, resistance -0.57 and combined -0.51; more than 150 min/week gave -0.89 versus -0.36 for 150 min or less, and physical-activity advice alone, without dietary advice, was not associated with any HbA1c change. A 2025 meta-analysis of 100 RCTs (7195 participants) reported combined training -0.74 (95% CI -0.91 to -0.57), HIIT -0.71, continuous aerobic -0.62 and resistance -0.36 percentage points, with supervised programmes of 150-210 min/week most effective, plus fasting glucose reductions of 0.60-1.13 mmol/L across modalities. Individual RCTs are less uniform: DARE (n=251, 22 weeks) found aerobic -0.51 and resistance -0.38 percentage points versus control, with combined training a further -0.46 to -0.59 beyond either alone, whereas HART-D (n=262, 9 months) found only the combined group significant (-0.34, 95% CI -0.64 to -0.03) with aerobic-only (-0.24) and resistance-only (-0.16) not significantly different from control; the Italian IDES trial (n=606, 12 months) found twice-weekly supervised aerobic plus resistance training gave -0.30 percentage points (95% CI -0.49 to -0.10) over counselling alone. For prevention, the exercise-only arm of the Da Qing trial (impaired glucose tolerance, 6 years) reduced diabetes risk by 46% against a 67.7% cumulative incidence in controls — but the 2017 Cochrane review, pooling the exercise-alone comparisons (2 trials, 397 participants), rated that evidence very low quality and concluded there is no firm evidence that physical activity alone influences the risk of type 2 diabetes or, especially, its complications. Post-meal walking evidence is surrogate and short-term: in a 2-week randomised crossover in 41 adults with type 2 diabetes, 10-minute walks after each main meal lowered the 3-hour post-meal glucose incremental area by 12% (ratio of geometric means 0.88, 95% CI 0.78-0.99) versus one 30-minute daily walk, with a 22% reduction after the evening meal. No randomised trial shows exercise alone reduces cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes, and the Look AHEAD diet-plus-activity trial (n=5145, median 9.6 years) did not (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83-1.09).

Doses studied: Structured aerobic exercise of 150 minutes/week or more at moderate intensity, with greater HbA1c benefit above 150 min/week; resistance training 2-3 sessions/week; combined aerobic plus resistance three times weekly for 22 weeks (DARE) or 9 months (HART-D: 12 kcal/kg/week aerobic, or 10 kcal/kg/week plus two resistance sessions); twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance training plus counselling for 12 months (IDES); supervised programmes prescribing 150-210 min/week were most effective in the 2025 meta-analysis. Post-meal walking: 10 minutes after each main meal for 2 weeks.

For depression & low mood

Moving your body regularly - walking, jogging, strength training, yoga, tai chi - is linked to lower depression scores across hundreds of trials, and the more intense the prescription, the larger the effect. The honest complication is that when researchers look only at the most rigorously conducted studies, where people assessing the outcome did not know who exercised, the benefit largely disappears. Exercise is worth doing and is good for you for many reasons, but the confidence that it lifts depression specifically is weaker than the headlines suggest.

Safety

Exercise is broadly safe and carries well-established benefits for cardiovascular, metabolic, and musculoskeletal health independent of mood. Harms reporting in this literature is poor: only 6 of 39 trials in the Cochrane review reported adverse events, and the 35-trial review concluded that data on serious adverse events and adverse events were too scarce to allow conclusions. Acceptability appears comparable to control conditions, with dropout-based risk ratio 1.00 (95% CI 0.97-1.04); in the largest network meta-analysis, strength training and yoga were the best-tolerated modalities. Practical risks are the ordinary ones - musculoskeletal injury and overuse, particularly when a previously inactive person starts at high intensity, which is also the intensity band associated with the largest reported mood effects.

Who should be careful: People with known or suspected cardiac disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or significant respiratory or musculoskeletal limitation should get clinical clearance before starting vigorous exercise. Exercise requires careful handling in anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and other conditions involving compulsive exercise, where increasing activity can worsen the primary disorder. In bipolar disorder, the trial evidence here is confined to unipolar major depression and does not establish benefit or safety; activity changes that disrupt sleep and routine warrant clinical supervision.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions with antidepressants. Exercise showed no significant difference from pharmacological treatment (SMD -0.11) or psychological therapy (SMD -0.03) in Cochrane pooling, though those comparisons rest on few small trials. Exercise is not established as a replacement for antidepressant medication, and stopping medication should be discussed with the prescribing clinician. People on medications affecting heart rate, blood pressure, blood glucose, or balance should factor that into intensity choices with their clinician.

What the evidence says

A network meta-analysis of 218 unique studies (495 arms, 14,170 participants) found moderate reductions in depression versus active controls for walking or jogging (Hedges' g -0.62, 95% CI -0.80 to -0.45, n=1,210, k=51), yoga (-0.55, 95% CI -0.73 to -0.36), strength training (-0.49, 95% CI -0.69 to -0.29), mixed aerobic exercise (-0.43, n=1,286, k=51), and tai chi or qigong (-0.42), with effects proportional to prescribed intensity; however, only one of the 218 studies met Cochrane criteria for low risk of bias, and the authors rated confidence as low for walking or jogging and very low for every other modality. This bias-sensitivity is reproducible. A Cochrane review of 39 trials (2,326 participants) reported SMD -0.62 (95% CI -0.81 to -0.42) for exercise versus control across 35 trials, but only -0.18 (95% CI -0.47 to 0.11), non-significant, when restricted to the 6 trials (464 participants) with adequate allocation concealment, intention-to-treat analysis, and blinded outcome assessment; the same review found no significant difference between exercise and psychological therapy (SMD -0.03, 95% CI -0.32 to 0.26) or pharmacological treatment (SMD -0.11, 95% CI -0.34 to 0.12). An independent review of 35 trials (2,498 participants) found the identical pattern: SMD -0.66 (95% CI -0.86 to -0.46) overall collapsing to -0.11 (95% CI -0.41 to 0.18, p=0.45) in the 4 trials least affected by bias, and relative risk of no remission 0.78 (95% CI 0.68-0.90) overall versus 0.95 (95% CI 0.74-1.23) in the 2 least-biased trials. The two reviews disagree about durability, and neither reading should be presented as settled: the Cochrane pooling of 8 trials (377 participants) with long-term follow-up found a small effect still favouring exercise (SMD -0.33, 95% CI -0.63 to -0.03), while the 2017 review found no significant effect on secondary outcomes including quality of life and depression at follow-up. Two carefully designed trials support the cautious reading: a pragmatic 361-participant primary care trial of facilitated physical activity successfully increased activity (adjusted OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.32-3.89, P=0.003) but produced no mood benefit at 4 months (adjusted BDI difference -0.54, 95% CI -3.06 to 1.99, P=0.68), none at 8 or 12 months, and no reduction in antidepressant use. A 202-participant four-arm placebo-controlled trial found remission rates of 45% (supervised exercise), 40% (home exercise), 47% (sertraline), and 31% (placebo) that did not reach significance (p=0.057), with post-treatment HAM-D scores in the active groups not significantly different from placebo (p=0.23). One 18-participant trial in the Cochrane review reported exercise as more effective than bright light therapy; a trial that small settles nothing and should not be read as a head-to-head result.

Doses studied: Trials typically prescribed supervised or facilitated sessions around three times weekly for 8-16 weeks. Effects were proportional to the intensity prescribed. Modalities with the largest pooled effects were walking or jogging, yoga, strength training, mixed aerobic exercise, and tai chi or qigong.

Why this grade: A very large body of randomised evidence exists and points positively (218-study network meta-analysis, 14,170 participants), but the effect is strongly bias-sensitive in a reproducible way: in two independent systematic reviews the benefit shrank to non-significance when analysis was restricted to trials with adequate allocation concealment, intention-to-treat analysis, and blinded outcome assessment. The largest and most favourable synthesis rated its own confidence as low to very low because only 1 of 218 studies met Cochrane low-risk-of-bias criteria. Two well-conducted trials - a 361-participant primary care trial and a 202-participant placebo-controlled trial - found no significant mood advantage. Moderate, not strong.

For fatigue & low energy

Moving your body more can lift energy for some people, but where the evidence is strongest matters a lot. It is best studied in people tired from cancer treatment, where regular gentle aerobic exercise reliably helps a modest amount — about as much as talking therapy does. In ordinary unexplained tiredness the picture is much weaker once studies use a fair comparison group, and when one trial in multiple sclerosis enrolled only badly fatigued people, aerobic training did not help enough for them to notice. For people with ME/CFS or post-exertional crashes, step-by-step exercise programmes are no longer recommended in the UK and can make things worse.

Safety

Persistent fatigue should be worked up for a cause before an activity plan is built around it, because anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, medication effects, heart failure and depression all present this way and none of them are addressed by exercise. In the PACE trial, serious adverse reactions were recorded in 2 of 160 participants in the graded exercise arm (1%), the same rate as specialist medical care alone (2 of 160), but Cochrane rated the evidence on serious adverse reactions in this population as very low certainty (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.14 to 6.97), so reassurance about safety is not well founded. In multiple sclerosis, Cochrane found one fall across 45 trials and similar relapse counts in exercise (25) and control (26) groups, though relapses were poorly defined and reported. Anyone whose fatigue comes with chest pain, breathlessness at rest, fainting, or unexplained weight loss needs medical assessment before increasing activity, not an exercise plan.

Who should be careful: Fixed-increment graded exercise programmes are recommended against by NICE (2021) for people with ME/CFS, and the same caution applies to anyone whose defining feature is post-exertional malaise, including some people with long COVID: exertion that feels tolerable at the time can be followed hours or days later by a prolonged worsening. Unstable cardiac disease, uncontrolled arrhythmia, acute infection, and untreated severe orthostatic intolerance all require medical clearance first.

Interactions: Not a drug, so no pharmacological interactions. Practical interactions do exist: beta-blockers blunt heart-rate response so heart-rate-based intensity targets can mislead; insulin and sulfonylureas raise hypoglycaemia risk around exercise; and sedating medications increase fall risk during activity.

What the evidence says

In cancer-related fatigue, a meta-analysis of 113 randomised trials (11,525 participants) found a weighted effect size of 0.30 (95% CI 0.25-0.36) for exercise, essentially matched by psychological interventions at 0.27 (95% CI 0.21-0.33) and larger than pharmaceutical options at 0.09 (95% CI 0.00-0.19); the authors recommended exercise or psychological interventions as first-line for this population (Mustian 2017). A Cochrane review of 56 trials (4,068 participants) pooled 38 comparisons (1,461 exercise, 1,187 control) for a standardised mean difference of -0.27 (95% CI -0.37 to -0.17), driven by aerobic exercise and by breast and prostate cancer, with no significant effect for resistance training, alternative exercise forms, or haematological malignancies (Cramp 2012). Outside cancer the effect depends heavily on the comparison group: a meta-analysis of chronic exercise and feelings of energy and fatigue found an overall effect of 0.37, but investigations using a placebo control that tested exercise alone found no effect at all (Puetz 2006). In multiple sclerosis a Cochrane review of 45 trials (2,250 participants) found SMD -0.53 (95% CI -0.73 to -0.33) favouring exercise, but its authors cautioned that most trials did not enrol people who were actually fatigued, did not target therapy on fatigue, and did not use a validated fatigue measure as the primary outcome (Heine 2015) — and the trial that did all three was effectively null, with 16 weeks of aerobic training beating a low-intensity control by 4.708 CIS20r points (95% CI 1.003-8.412), short of the 8-point threshold pre-specified as clinically relevant, unsustained at follow-up, with no effect on participation (Heine 2017). In ME/CFS, Cochrane reported a moderate-certainty fatigue reduction versus passive control at end of treatment (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -1.01 to -0.31; 7 studies, 840 participants) but very-low-certainty, non-significant long-term results (SMD -0.62, 95% CI -1.32 to 0.07; 4 studies, 670 participants), with searches running only to May 2014 and all participants diagnosed by 1994 CDC or Oxford criteria that do not require post-exertional malaise (Larun 2024). The 2021 NICE guideline reversed prior UK advice and states that programmes based on fixed incremental increases in activity or exercise should not be offered for ME/CFS (Kingdon 2022); a large group of clinicians and researchers — including the PACE trial's own investigators — published a formal dissent arguing NICE mishandled the trial evidence (White 2023). These are different literatures and should not be blurred together.

Doses studied: The ME/CFS trials pooled by Cochrane ran 12 to 26 weeks; seven of the eight used aerobic exercise such as walking, swimming, cycling or dancing at intensities described as ranging from very low to quite rigorous, and one used anaerobic exercise (Larun 2024). The multiple sclerosis trial used a 16-week aerobic training programme against a low-intensity control (Heine 2017). The cancer-related-fatigue meta-analyses pooled a wide mix of supervised and home-based programmes, and Cochrane states plainly that the optimal type, intensity and timing has still not been determined (Cramp 2012).

Why this grade: Graded on the whole body of evidence for adults with persistent fatigue, not on its single strongest population. In cancer-related fatigue the evidence approaches strong (two large meta-analyses, consistent benefit, first-line status — though shared equally with psychological therapy, not held alone). That does not transfer. The best placebo-controlled synthesis in non-clinical fatigued adults found no effect of exercise alone; the one multiple sclerosis trial that enrolled only severely fatigued people, targeted fatigue directly and used a validated fatigue primary outcome found no clinically meaningful benefit over a low-intensity control; and in ME/CFS the 2021 NICE guideline recommends against fixed-increment graded exercise therapy. Population-dependent effects plus a guideline recommendation against it in one population put the conservative overall grade at moderate.

For migraine & headache

Getting regular exercise is one of the better-supported everyday things you can do to have fewer migraine days over time. The research is genuinely encouraging but not airtight — study groups have been small and people obviously know whether they are exercising, which can inflate results. Worth knowing: this is about prevention over weeks and months. Exercise is not something to try during an attack, and for some people exertion itself can set an attack off.

Safety

Exercise is generally well tolerated in migraine trials, but 'well tolerated' is not the same as 'harmless', and harms are inconsistently reported in this literature. A 2025 meta-analysis of dropout across migraine exercise trials found a pooled dropout rate of 11.6% in exercise arms versus 10.1% in comparator arms — numerically higher with exercise but not statistically different, with heterogeneity the authors said prevents firm conclusions. The migraine-specific caution is different from ordinary exercise risk: in a headache-clinic sample of 103 patients, 38% reported that exercise had triggered a migraine attack at some point and more than half of that subgroup had given up the sport involved — though those authors explicitly warned that a headache-clinic population cannot be generalised to everyone with migraine. Starting gradually and building intensity slowly is the usual clinical approach for that reason. Exercise is a preventive strategy only: exertion typically worsens an attack already underway and has never been tested as an abortive.

Who should be careful: Not appropriate as a treatment during an acute attack. People with uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or other conditions requiring medical clearance for vigorous exercise should be assessed before starting high-intensity or interval training. Anyone whose headache pattern is new, changing, or accompanied by neurological symptoms needs a diagnosis before starting any self-management program.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Exercise does not replace prescribed acute or preventive migraine medication and was studied in most trials alongside usual care rather than instead of it.

What the evidence says

Evidence here is entirely PREVENTIVE; no trial has tested exercise as an acute/abortive treatment, and physical exertion commonly aggravates an attack in progress. A network meta-analysis of 21 clinical trials (1195 patients) ranked strength training (MD -3.55 monthly migraine days, 95% CI -6.15 to -0.95), high-intensity aerobic exercise (-3.13, -5.28 to -0.97) and moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (-2.18, -3.25 to -1.11) above topiramate (-0.98, -4.16 to 2.20) and amitriptyline (+3.82). A larger network meta-analysis of 28 studies (1501 participants) found high-intensity aerobic exercise (SMD -1.30) and moderate-intensity continuous aerobic exercise (SMD -1.01) superior to pharmacological treatment alone for attack frequency, but graded the evidence very low quality. A 2025 multilevel network and dose-response meta-analysis of 27 RCTs (n=1611) found combined aerobic plus resistance training best (SMD -1.85, 95% CrI -2.53 to -1.18) with an optimal window of roughly 300-600 MET-min/week over 8-10 weeks, again at very low to low certainty. Against this, a randomized trial of 91 adults comparing 40 minutes of exercise three times weekly against relaxation and against topiramate found reductions of 0.93, 0.83 and 0.97 attacks respectively, with no significant difference between groups (p=0.95) and no untreated control arm, so superiority over the natural course was never demonstrated. A 2026 systematic review of cervical strengthening and stabilization exercise (12 studies, 5 RCTs) found a significant effect on migraine-related disability (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.19-1.18) but no significant effect on headache frequency (SMD 0.61, -0.25 to 1.47) or intensity (SMD 0.41, -0.35 to 1.18). On tolerability, a 2025 meta-analysis of attrition in migraine exercise trials found a pooled dropout rate of 11.6% in exercise arms versus 10.1% in comparators — numerically higher with exercise but not statistically different, with heterogeneity the authors said prevents firm conclusions.

Doses studied: Most trials used 30-50 minutes of aerobic exercise 2-3 times per week for 8-12 weeks (e.g. 40 minutes three times weekly). A 2025 dose-response analysis identified an optimal window of approximately 300-600 MET-min/week (roughly 70-135 minutes of moderate-intensity or 45-90 minutes of vigorous activity weekly) at 4.5-5.5 METs, sustained for 8-10 weeks.

Why this grade: Roughly two dozen randomized trials and several network meta-analyses consistently point the same direction for PREVENTION (fewer migraine days per month), and exercise outperformed placebo and amitriptyline in one network meta-analysis. But every recent synthesis rates the underlying certainty as very low to low, trial samples are small and mostly unblinded, effect estimates are unstable across analyses and are reported in incommensurable units (mean differences of -2.18 to -3.55 migraine days per month in one network meta-analysis; standardised mean differences of -0.35 to -1.85 in another), and the best-controlled single trial found exercise no better than relaxation or topiramate with no untreated control arm. Neck/cervical exercise specifically was null for headache frequency and intensity. There is no acute (abortive) evidence at all. Held at moderate rather than strong because of the certainty ratings and effect instability; not lowered to early because the trial base is large rather than preliminary.

For insomnia

Getting regular movement — brisk walking, jogging, yoga, or tai chi — measurably helps many people with insomnia sleep better, though the effect builds over weeks and months rather than the same night you exercise. The studies are encouraging but mostly small, so exercise is best thought of as a genuinely useful support for sleep rather than a stand-alone cure. It also carries all the other health benefits, which is more than most sleep remedies can say.

Safety

Exercise was well tolerated across the meta-analyzed trials, with high adherence and retention in the key 6-month RCT, and it carries broad cardiometabolic and mood co-benefits (the Hartescu trial also found reduced depression and anxiety scores). Expect benefits to accrue over weeks to months, not the same night.

Who should be careful: People with cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or who are sedentary and beginning vigorous exercise should get medical clearance first. Musculoskeletal injury risk applies as with any new exercise program; choose modalities appropriate to mobility and balance (relevant for older adults).

Interactions: No drug interactions in the pharmacological sense. Timing may matter for some individuals — vigorous late-evening sessions can feel activating; trials showing benefit generally used daytime or scheduled programs.

What the evidence says

Evidence for exercise in insomnia is positive but of mostly low certainty. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (n=1,348 insomnia patients) found yoga increased diary total sleep time by 110.9 min (95% CI 58.7-163.1; moderate certainty), walking or jogging reduced Insomnia Severity Index scores by 9.6 points (95% CI -12.1 to -7.0; low certainty), and Tai Chi reduced PSQI by 4.6 points (low certainty); only 4 of 22 trials were low risk of bias. A network meta-analysis of 35 trials in 3,519 older adults likewise found several exercise regimens improved sleep quality more than usual care. In a 6-month RCT of inactive adults with insomnia, ≥150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity reduced ISI by an average of 4 points and improved depression and anxiety, but did not significantly reduce daytime fatigue. Countervailing day-level data in women with insomnia found daily exercise was NOT associated with that night's subjective or objective sleep — benefits emerged only over 16 weeks, and poor sleep actually shortened next-day exercise.

Doses studied: At least 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity (public-health guideline level) over 6 months in the key RCT; meta-analyzed programs included yoga, Tai Chi, walking or jogging, aerobic, and strength training, typically delivered as multi-week to multi-month structured programs.

Why this grade: Multiple RCT-based meta-analyses (22 RCTs in insomnia patients; 35 trials in older adults) show positive effects on insomnia severity and sleep quality, but the evidence certainty is mostly low (few low-risk-of-bias trials, small samples), effects vary widely by exercise type, and day-level data show no reliable same-night benefit. Positive but modest/mixed with low-to-moderate certainty fits moderate, not strong.

For gut & digestion

Moving your body regularly - walking, cycling, Pilates, general aerobic exercise - appears to take the edge off IBS symptoms for many people, and it is cheap, available and good for you in a dozen other ways. But the studies behind this are small and imperfect, and when researchers pooled them, exercise helped symptom scores while quality-of-life and anxiety scores did not budge significantly. Start gently, since hard or high-intensity exercise can itself stir up cramping, urgency and loose stools.

Safety

Moderate regular activity is broadly safe and carries wide health benefits beyond the gut. The relevant IBS-specific caution runs the other way: strenuous exercise is itself a common trigger of gastrointestinal symptoms. In a survey of 471 marathon runners, 83% reported occasionally or frequently suffering one or more gastrointestinal disturbances during or immediately after running, most commonly the urge to have a bowel movement (53%) and diarrhoea (38%), with markedly higher rates in women (74% and 68%); both upper and lower tract symptoms were significantly more common during a hard run than an easy one (p<0.01) (Riddoch & Trinick, Br J Sports Med 1988, PMID 3167507). In a separate survey of 109 distance runners, 62% had stopped to have a bowel movement while training and 47% had experienced diarrhoea after racing or hard runs; runners with symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome were particularly likely to have to stop for a bowel movement during training (Sullivan & Wong, J Clin Gastroenterol 1992, PMID 1556421). Practically, that means building intensity gradually, keeping hydration and pre-exercise meals in mind, and treating symptom flares during hard sessions as a signal to reduce intensity rather than a sign the approach has failed. Effects on quality of life and anxiety were not statistically significant in the pooled analysis, so exercise should be framed as symptom support and general health, not as an emotional cure.

Who should be careful: People with known or suspected cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, significant respiratory disease, or who are markedly deconditioned should get medical clearance before starting a vigorous programme. Anyone with alarm features (unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, nocturnal symptoms, new onset after age 50, or a family history of bowel cancer, coeliac disease or IBD) needs medical assessment before self-managing with lifestyle change. Exercise is not a treatment for inflammatory bowel disease flares, and should not be scaled up during acute illness, significant dehydration or an unexplained change in bowel habit. In diarrhoea-predominant IBS, high-intensity and long-duration endurance exercise may provoke urgency and loose stools; lower-intensity options are a reasonable starting point, though we found no trial testing this head to head.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Exercise interacts with diet more than with drugs: high-FODMAP or high-fibre pre-exercise meals, caffeine and large fluid loads are common symptom triggers around training. Anyone using antidiarrhoeals, laxatives or antispasmodics can generally exercise normally but should discuss any change in requirement with their clinician.

What the evidence says

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis screened 2,142 citations and included 10 studies totalling 437 IBS patients; improvement in the IBS Symptom Severity Score was greater with exercise than control, but effects on IBS quality of life and on anxiety were NOT statistically significant, and the authors conclude that no evidence-based consensus exists on a standardised exercise prescription. A separate 2026 network meta-analysis of 16 studies found moderate-intensity aerobic exercise and Pilates significantly improved IBS-SSS, IBS-QOL, depression and anxiety scores (P<0.05), with running and aerobic exercise ranking best overall, though its proposed brain-gut-immune mechanisms are hypothesised rather than demonstrated. The anchoring trial randomised 102 patients to a physiotherapist-guided increase in physical activity or lifestyle maintenance; among the 75 who completed, the IBS-SSS change was -51 (range -130 to 49) with exercise versus -5 (range -101 to 118) in controls (P=0.003), and significantly fewer exercisers deteriorated. A long-term follow-up of that cohort found IBS-SSS improved from 276 to 218 (P=0.001) at a median 5.2 years with self-reported activity rising from 3.2 to 5.2 hours per week (P=0.019), but only 39 of 76 patients attended, there was no concurrent control comparison, and measured oxygen uptake did not change significantly. Against this, a pilot RCT of 27 completers comparing Iyengar yoga with walking found NO significant group-by-time effect on IBS severity, with walking better for negative affect and yoga symptoms rebounding toward baseline by 6 months. Subtype evidence is thin: the only clearly subtype-specific trial randomised 60 women with constipation-predominant IBS to 8 weeks of Pilates plus dietary advice versus dietary advice alone and found a moderate advantage for Pilates (Cohen d 0.73 for IBS-SSS, p<0.001), though dietary advice alone also improved every outcome except body weight. Importantly, one frequently cited positive trial (Hajizadeh Maleki et al., Cytokine 2018;102:18-25, a 60-woman RCT reporting symptom and inflammatory benefits) has been RETRACTED and is flagged in PubMed as a Retracted Publication, so it must not be counted as evidence. We found no trial adequately testing whether vigorous exercise worsens diarrhoea-predominant IBS.

Doses studied: Protocols vary widely and no standard prescription is established; the 2026 pooled analysis states explicitly that no evidence-based consensus exists on a standardised exercise prescription for IBS, and notes this absence may itself confound efficacy estimates. Verified examples from the trial base: a physiotherapist-guided programme to increase physical activity versus instructions to maintain usual lifestyle; 16 biweekly Iyengar yoga sessions or an equivalent walking programme; and an 8-week Pilates programme of two sessions per week alongside dietary advice. A 2026 network meta-analysis covered running, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, Pilates, yoga and Baguazhang, ranking running and aerobic exercise best overall.

Why this grade: Meta-analytic evidence exists and points in a favourable direction, but the underlying trials are small, few and mostly unblinded: the 2026 pooled analysis included only 10 studies totalling 437 patients, and its authors state there is no evidence-based consensus on a standardised exercise prescription for IBS. Crucially, that same meta-analysis found the effects on IBS quality of life and on anxiety were NOT statistically significant; a pilot RCT found no significant group-by-time effect on IBS severity; the anchoring RCT had only 75 completers; its long-term follow-up was uncontrolled with 39 of 76 patients attending and showed no change in measured oxygen uptake; and a frequently cited positive exercise-and-IBS trial has since been formally retracted, removing it from the evidence base. Graded conservatively as early rather than moderate on that basis.
