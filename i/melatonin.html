Melatonin

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For migraine & headache

Taking melatonin at bedtime appears to cut the number of migraine days per month, and in one head-to-head study it worked about as well as a standard prescription preventive with fewer side effects. What matters is the form: the plain immediate-release 3 mg version is what worked, while a slow-release version failed to beat placebo. Melatonin is a hormone rather than a vitamin, so it deserves a bit more caution than its supermarket-shelf status suggests.

Safety

Melatonin is a hormone, not a vitamin, and deserves more caution than its over-the-counter status implies. In trials it was well tolerated — side effects occurred in 6.7% on melatonin versus 3.3% on placebo in one study, and it produced less sleepiness than amitriptyline (RR 0.49) — with weight loss rather than the weight gain seen on amitriptyline. Common complaints are next-day grogginess, vivid dreams or nightmares, dizziness, and paradoxically headache. Formulation appears to matter: immediate-release 3 mg beat placebo while 2 mg prolonged-release did not. Do not drive or operate machinery after a dose.

Who should be careful: Avoid or use only under clinician guidance in pregnancy and breastfeeding, in autoimmune disease, in seizure disorders, and in significant liver disease. Use in children and adolescents should be clinician-supervised — the pediatric migraine trial was null and underpowered. People with daytime sleepiness, sleep apnea, or occupations requiring night alertness should discuss timing with a clinician.

Interactions: Sedation is additive with benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids, alcohol, antihistamines and other sedating medications. Fluvoxamine and other CYP1A2 inhibitors, including ciprofloxacin, can raise melatonin blood levels substantially. Melatonin may affect anticoagulants including warfarin, and may alter blood pressure and blood glucose control, so people on antihypertensives or diabetes medication should monitor. Caffeine and smoking change how melatonin is metabolized.

What the evidence says

PREVENTIVE only — melatonin has not been established as an abortive treatment during an attack. Gonçalves et al. randomized 196 adults (178 treated) to melatonin 3 mg, amitriptyline 25 mg or placebo for 12 weeks and found mean reductions of 2.7, 2.2 and 1.1 migraine headache days per month respectively; melatonin beat placebo (p = 0.009) but did not differ from amitriptyline (p = 0.19), and was better tolerated, with weight loss rather than the weight gain seen on amitriptyline. A 2026 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (n = 788) found melatonin reduced headache days by 1.54 (95% CI -2.50 to -0.58), attack duration by 4.98 hours (95% CI -9.30 to -0.67) and severity by 2.08, and increased the 50% response rate (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.70), while remaining less effective than amitriptyline on several endpoints but with lower sleepiness risk (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.87). The strongest contrary evidence is a two-center Class I crossover trial: 2 mg prolonged-release melatonin taken an hour before bed for 8 weeks reduced attacks from 4.2 to 2.8 per month, but the placebo reduction was almost identical (p = 0.497), absolute risk reduction 3% (95% CI -15 to 21), NNT 33, with no improvement in sleep quality (p = 0.09). A 2020 systematic review of 7 RCTs and 9 non-randomized studies could not pool the placebo comparisons because of clinical and methodological heterogeneity, and a randomized pediatric trial that closed early (42 of a planned 210 randomized) found a median difference of 0 migraine days (95% CI -9 to 3) between melatonin and placebo. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 44 adult prevention trials (4,612 participants) confirmed efficacy for topiramate, valproate and propranolol and listed melatonin among agents showing potential preventive effects rather than established ones.

Doses studied: 3 mg immediate-release taken at bedtime for 8-12 weeks is the regimen with positive trial support, including as an add-on to propranolol 20 mg twice daily. A 2 mg prolonged-release formulation taken one hour before bedtime for 8 weeks did not beat placebo. A pediatric trial tested 3 mg and 6 mg nightly and was null and underpowered.

Why this grade: A 2016 randomized trial of 178 treated adults found immediate-release melatonin 3 mg superior to placebo for migraine days and better tolerated than amitriptyline, and a 2026 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (n = 788) reported benefit on headache days, response rate and disability. Set against that, a Class I crossover trial of 2 mg prolonged-release melatonin found no effect over placebo (p = 0.497, NNT 33), a 2020 systematic review could not pool the trials due to heterogeneity, a pediatric trial showed no suggestion of superiority, and a 2025 network meta-analysis of 44 adult prevention trials placed melatonin among agents with 'potential' effects rather than in the confirmed-efficacy tier occupied by topiramate, valproate and propranolol. There is no guideline first-line status. Positive but formulation-dependent and mixed places this at 'moderate', not 'strong'.

For insomnia

Melatonin is a hormone your brain makes at night, and the supplement is one of the most-studied sleep aids there is. Careful reviews find it helps people fall asleep only a few minutes faster on average, which is why sleep-medicine doctors don't recommend it for ongoing insomnia — though it seems more useful when your body clock is shifted, like night-owl sleep schedules. The good news is that short-term use appears quite safe for most adults.

Safety

Short-term use appears well tolerated: the Buscemi meta-analyses (2005, 2006; 17 controlled trials, n=651) found no evidence of adverse effects with use of 3 months or less. Commonly reported effects include daytime drowsiness, headache, dizziness, and vivid dreams. Avoid driving or operating machinery in the hours after taking it. In the United States melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, so actual content can vary between products; long-term safety data are limited.

Who should be careful: Caution in pregnancy and breastfeeding (insufficient safety data), in autoimmune conditions, and in children/adolescents outside clinician guidance. People with epilepsy or on multiple sedating medicines should consult a clinician first.

Interactions: May add to the effect of sedatives, alcohol, and other sleep medicines. Fluvoxamine markedly raises melatonin levels (CYP1A2 inhibition). Use caution with anticoagulants/antiplatelets, immunosuppressants, antihypertensives, and diabetes medications; discuss with a pharmacist or clinician if taking any of these.

What the evidence says

The evidence base for melatonin in insomnia is large but the effects are small. A 2013 meta-analysis of 19 randomized placebo-controlled trials (n=1,683) found melatonin reduced sleep-onset latency by 7.06 minutes (95% CI 4.37-9.75), increased total sleep time by 8.25 minutes (95% CI 1.74-14.75), and improved sleep quality (SMD 0.22). An earlier meta-analysis (Buscemi 2005) found a pooled sleep-latency reduction of only 7.2 minutes (95% CI 2.4-12.0) in insomnia versus 38.8 minutes in delayed sleep phase syndrome, concluding melatonin is not effective for most primary sleep disorders; the companion 2006 meta-analysis found no significant effect in secondary sleep disorders (-13.2 min, 95% CI -27.3 to 0.9). The 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 154 trials (44,089 participants) concluded melatonin did not show overall material benefits for insomnia disorder, and the 2017 AASM clinical practice guideline suggests clinicians not use melatonin for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance chronic insomnia (weak recommendation). Doses in trials ranged from about 0.5 to 10 mg taken before bedtime; higher doses and longer trials showed somewhat larger latency effects in meta-regression.

Doses studied: Approximately 0.5-10 mg taken 30-120 minutes before bedtime; 2-5 mg most common in trials (e.g., 3 mg in a recent comparative RCT). Meta-regression suggested larger latency effects at higher doses and longer durations.

Why this grade: Graded per the rubric's guideline criterion: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline (Sateia 2017) suggests clinicians NOT use melatonin for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance chronic insomnia (weak recommendation), and the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis found no overall material benefit. Meta-analyses do show statistically significant effects, but they are small (roughly 7-12 minutes faster sleep onset) and judged below clinical significance; torn between moderate and not_supported, the lower grade was taken. Evidence is stronger for circadian problems such as delayed sleep phase, which is a distinct condition from insomnia.

Sources (5)
  1. Meta-analysis: melatonin for the treatment of primary sleep disorders.
    Meta-analysisFerracioli-Oda, Qawasmi & Bloch, 2013 · PMID 23691095 · positive

    Across 19 RCTs (n=1,683) in primary sleep disorders, melatonin reduced sleep-onset latency by 7.06 min (95% CI 4.37-9.75), increased total sleep time by 8.25 min (95% CI 1.74-14.75), and modestly improved sleep quality (SMD 0.22, 95% CI 0.12-0.32); the authors describe the effects as modest but persistent with continued use.

  2. The efficacy and safety of exogenous melatonin for primary sleep disorders. A meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisBuscemi et al., 2005 · PMID 16423108 · mixed

    Melatonin shortened sleep-onset latency by 11.7 min overall, but by only 7.2 min (95% CI 2.4-12.0) in insomnia versus 38.8 min in delayed sleep phase syndrome; authors concluded melatonin is not effective for treating most primary sleep disorders with short-term use, while noting it appeared safe up to 3 months.

  3. Efficacy and safety of exogenous melatonin for secondary sleep disorders and sleep disorders accompanying sleep restriction: meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisBuscemi et al., 2006 · PMID 16473858 · null

    In 6 RCTs (n=97) of secondary sleep disorders, melatonin had no significant effect on sleep-onset latency (-13.2 min, 95% CI -27.3 to 0.9), and no effect in sleep-restriction conditions (-1.0 min, 95% CI -2.3 to 0.3); 17 RCTs (n=651) showed no evidence of adverse effects with use of 3 months or less.

  4. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisDe Crescenzo et al., 2022 · PMID 35843245 · negative

    In a network meta-analysis of 154 double-blind RCTs (44,089 participants) of insomnia disorder in adults, melatonin (with ramelteon and other non-licensed drugs) 'did not show overall material benefits,' while benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, and eszopiclone outperformed melatonin on sleep-quality outcomes (SMD 0.27-0.71).

  5. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    GuidelineSateia et al., 2017 · PMID 27998379 · negative

    The AASM guideline states: 'We suggest that clinicians not use melatonin as a treatment for sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults' (WEAK recommendation), reflecting low certainty evidence rather than proof of ineffectiveness.
