Herbs

Hops (Humulus lupulus)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Hops has been used to help people sleep for centuries and is the main ingredient in many 'natural sleep' products on the shelf. But nobody has ever run a proper placebo-controlled trial of hops on its own for insomnia - researchers have said so in print. The two small human sleep studies used non-alcoholic beer with no placebo group, and both real randomised trials of products containing hops found no sleep benefit over a dummy pill. People have used this for a very long time and nobody has properly tested it by itself.

Safety

Hops was well tolerated in the human studies retrieved, and a pharmacokinetic study in menopausal women found no acute toxicity, no change in sex hormones and no change in prothrombin time. However, hops contains 8-prenylnaringenin, one of the most potent known plant estrogens, with a half-life over 20 hours and enterohepatic recirculation; a 2018 review of this compound concluded that the safety risks of long-term phytoestrogen exposure still need to be defined. Safety in pregnancy, breastfeeding and children has not been studied. Note that the two human sleep studies used non-alcoholic beer; alcoholic beer is not an equivalent exposure and alcohol degrades sleep architecture rather than improving it.

Who should be careful: Because of the potent phytoestrogen content, avoid in hormone-sensitive conditions (breast, uterine or ovarian cancer, endometriosis, uterine fibroids) unless a clinician advises otherwise. Not established as safe in pregnancy or lactation.

Interactions: Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids, sedating antihistamines and other CNS depressants; do not combine before driving or operating machinery. Theoretical interaction with hormone therapy, oral contraceptives and anti-estrogens such as tamoxifen given the 8-prenylnaringenin content, though this has not been tested clinically.

What the evidence says

No placebo-controlled randomised trial of hops alone with a sleep endpoint in adults with insomnia was retrieved, and Scholey and colleagues state this explicitly in the discussion of their own 2017 trial. Franco et al. (2012) studied 17 female rotating/night-shift nurses who drank 333 mL of hopped non-alcoholic beer with supper for 14 days, each acting as her own control: actigraphic sleep latency fell from 20.50 +/- 4.21 to 12.01 +/- 1.19 min and total nocturnal activity from 7258.78 +/- 898.89 to 5284.78 +/- 836.99 pulses (both p <= 0.05), with no randomisation, no placebo beverage and no blinding; Franco et al. (2014) repeated the uncontrolled design in 30 university students and reported improved PSQI sleep latency and global score (p < 0.05). Both randomised placebo-controlled trials of hops-containing products were null: Cornu et al. (2010), in adults with chronic primary insomnia, found mean Leeds sleep scores equivalent between arms (p = 0.95), marked improvement in 65% on active versus 62% on placebo (p = 0.52), actigraphic sleep efficiency 72% to 76% versus 75% (p = 0.91) and unchanged urinary melatonin/6-sulfatoxymelatonin; Scholey et al. (2017), in 171 randomised adults with mild insomnia taking a lactium-Ziziphus-hops-magnesium-B6 complex, found PSQI improved in both arms with no treatment effect (F = 0.14, p = 0.713). A randomised crossover trial of hops dry extract (0.4 g/day, 4 weeks) in 36 young adults reduced DASS-21 anxiety (9.2 +/- 7.3 to 5.1 +/- 5.9), depression (11.9 +/- 7.9 to 9.2 +/- 7.4) and stress (19.1 +/- 8.1 to 11.6 +/- 8.1) significantly more than placebo, but measured no sleep outcome. A 2010 review of 16 valerian and hops studies for primary insomnia found the 12 positive studies concerned valerian alone or valerian combined with hops, and concluded designs differed too much to recommend these herbs without further placebo-controlled trials.

Doses studied: No established sleep dose for hops alone. Human sleep studies used 333 mL hopped non-alcoholic beer with the evening meal (Franco 2012/2014). The one randomised hops-extract trial with any endpoint used 0.4 g/day dry extract (two 0.2 g capsules daily) for 4 weeks and measured mood, not sleep (Kyrou 2017). Controlled sleep dosing exists only for valerian-hops combinations (120 mg hops extract alongside 500 mg valerian).

Why this grade: Hops is a formally recognised traditional sedative - listed as an approved herb for mood and sleep disturbances in the German Commission E monographs - but a fresh PubMed search found no placebo-controlled randomised trial of hops alone with a sleep endpoint in adults with insomnia. This absence is not an inference: an independent trial team stated in print in 2017 that 'there are no randomized controlled trials investigating the efficacy of hops alone in the treatment of insomnia. Thus it remains unclear whether hops has independent sedative effects, works as a synergist, or lacks sedative activity.' The two human sleep studies are small, non-randomised, within-subject before/after designs using hopped non-alcoholic beer, and both randomised placebo-controlled trials of hops-containing products with sleep endpoints were null. Nearly all controlled hops sleep evidence comes from valerian-hops combination products, graded separately. One adequate placebo-controlled trial of hops alone would move this grade.

Sources (7)
  1. Exploring the Effect of Lactium™ and Zizyphus Complex on Sleep Quality: A Double-Blind, Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Scholey A, Benson S, Gibbs A, Perry N, Sarris J, Murray G, 2017 · PMID 28218661 · null

    Double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 171 randomised adults with mild insomnia (160 in the modified intention-to-treat analysis) of LZComplex3, which the full text states contains lactium, sour date and hops plus magnesium and vitamin B6. PSQI improved in both arms with a significant effect of day (F = 30.40, p < 0.001) but no effect of treatment (F = 0.14, p = 0.713) and no treatment-by-day interaction (F = 1.13, p = 0.340). Most secondary outcomes improved similarly in both groups. The same paper states that no randomised controlled trials of hops alone for insomnia exist, so it is unclear whether hops has independent sedative effects, acts as a synergist, or lacks sedative activity.

  2. A dietary supplement to improve the quality of sleep: a randomized placebo controlled trial.
    Cornu C, Remontet L, Noel-Baron F, et al., 2010 · PMID 20569455 · null

    Randomised placebo-controlled trial of a supplement containing polyunsaturated fatty acids plus Humulus lupulus extract versus olive oil for one month in adults aged 25-65 with chronic primary insomnia: mean Leeds sleep score was equivalent between groups (p = 0.95), marked improvement was reported by 65% on active versus 62% on placebo (p = 0.52), actigraphic sleep efficiency went from 72% to 76% (active) versus 75% (placebo) (p = 0.91), and night melatonin and 6-sulfatoxymelatonin were unchanged. The authors concluded the supplement had no effect on perceived sleep quality, melatonin metabolism or the sleep-wake cycle.

  3. The sedative effect of non-alcoholic beer in healthy female nurses.
    Franco L, Sánchez C, Bravo R, et al., 2012 · PMID 22815680 · positive

    In 17 healthy female nurses on rotating/night shifts acting as their own controls, 14 days of 333 mL hopped non-alcoholic beer with supper shortened actigraphic sleep latency from 20.50 +/- 4.21 to 12.01 +/- 1.19 min and reduced total nocturnal activity from 7258.78 +/- 898.89 to 5284.78 +/- 836.99 pulses (both p <= 0.05). Not randomised, not blinded, no placebo beverage.

  4. Effect of non-alcoholic beer on Subjective Sleep Quality in a university stressed population.
    Franco L, Bravo R, Galán C, Rodríguez AB, Barriga C, Cubero J, 2014 · PMID 25183509 · positive

    In 30 university students given one non-alcoholic beer at dinner for 14 days after a 7-day control week, PSQI sleep latency decreased and the global sleep-quality score improved significantly (p < 0.05). Uncontrolled before-and-after design with no placebo arm and no randomisation.

  5. Effects of a hops (Humulus lupulus L.) dry extract supplement on self-reported depression, anxiety and stress levels in apparently healthy young adults: a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, crossover pilot study.
    Kyrou I, Christou A, Panagiotakos D, et al., 2017 · PMID 28742505 · positive

    In 36 young adults, 0.4 g/day hops dry extract for 4 weeks reduced DASS-21 anxiety (9.2 +/- 7.3 to 5.1 +/- 5.9), depression (11.9 +/- 7.9 to 9.2 +/- 7.4) and stress (19.1 +/- 8.1 to 11.6 +/- 8.1), reductions significantly greater than placebo (all p < 0.05), with no change in body composition or morning cortisol. No sleep outcome was measured. The authors note the German Commission E approves hops for anxiety/mood disorders and restlessness.

  6. Treating primary insomnia - the efficacy of valerian and hops.
    Salter S, Brownie S, 2010 · PMID 20628685 · mixed

    Review of 1950-2009 literature identified 16 studies meeting inclusion criteria; 12 found that valerian, on its own or combined with hops, was associated with improvements in some sleep parameters such as sleep latency and sleep quality. The authors cautioned that designs differed significantly between studies and concluded further randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trials are needed before such herbal treatments can be confidently recommended.

  7. Pharmacokinetics of prenylated hop phenols in women following oral administration of a standardized extract of hops.
    van Breemen RB, Yuan Y, Banuvar S, et al., 2014 · PMID 25045111 · null

    Escalating-dose study of a standardised spent-hops extract in menopausal women found no effect on sex hormones or prothrombin time and no acute toxicity, but showed slow absorption, enterohepatic recirculation and half-lives exceeding 20 hours for the estrogenic and proestrogenic prenylated phenols 8-prenylnaringenin, isoxanthohumol and xanthohumol.
