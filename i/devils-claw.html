Herbs

Devil's claw (Harpagophytum procumbens)

Early evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Devil's claw is a traditional southern African root studied mainly for flare-ups of long-standing back pain, where a few small studies suggest it may help somewhat over about a month. Reviewers keep landing in the same place: the studies are too small and too varied in what they actually gave people to say for sure, and one expert panel reviewing supplements for long-term muscle and joint pain recommended against it. For arthritis, the placebo-controlled studies that exist were judged poor quality, so honestly we do not know. It does seem to be gentler on the stomach than the drugs it has been compared with.

Safety

Devil's claw was generally well tolerated across trials lasting 4 weeks to 4 months. In the head-to-head osteoarthritis trial, adverse events were significantly less frequent with Harpagophytum than with diacerhein, with diarrhoea in 8.1% versus 26.7% (PMID 11185727). The largest placebo-controlled low back pain trial reported no Harpagophytum-related side effects apart from possibly mild and infrequent gastrointestinal symptoms (PMID 10101629). An 8-week open-label study of 259 UK patients with rheumatic complaints reported good tolerance assessed by self-reporting, blood analysis and liver function tests (PMID 17886223); being uncontrolled and single-group, it speaks to tolerability rather than efficacy. A 2026 review concludes devil's claw is generally well tolerated but that long-term safety data and product standardization remain variable across jurisdictions (PMID 42394971), and a 2006 review noted that safety assessment is limited by the small populations studied and that long-term assessment is still required (PMID 17212570). Gastrointestinal upset is the adverse effect most consistently reported.

Who should be careful: Gastrointestinal upset, including diarrhoea, is the most frequently reported adverse effect across trials, so caution is warranted with active peptic ulcer disease, gastritis or significant reflux. Safety has not been studied in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children, so it is not established as safe in these groups. Because trial products range from standardized harpagoside extracts to whole cryoground root powder at very different doses, and because product standardization is described as variable across jurisdictions, check exactly what a product contains rather than assuming equivalence to the tested preparations. Tell your surgical team about any devil's claw supplement before a planned operation.

Interactions: Formal interaction studies are absent. Devil's claw is flagged in anticoagulation practice as a complementary medicine with potential to interact with warfarin: a review of 631 pharmaceutical care plans in a UK anticoagulant outpatient clinic found 170 patients (26.9%) starting warfarin were taking some form of complementary medicine, and 99 of those (58% of CAM users) were taking a product with potential to interact - the commonest named being cod-liver oil and garlic capsules, with Harpagophytum among the agents indexed for that report (PMID 16115136). That is a usage survey rather than an interaction study, so the risk is a plausible and monitored one rather than a demonstrated one. Anyone on warfarin, a direct oral anticoagulant, or an antiplatelet drug should not start devil's claw without telling their anticoagulation clinic or pharmacist. Devil's claw is frequently sold inside multi-herb formulas, in which case the interaction profile of every component applies.

What the evidence says

The 2014 Cochrane review rates Harpagophytum standardized to 50 mg or 100 mg harpagoside daily as low-quality evidence that it 'may be better than placebo for short-term improvements in pain' and may reduce rescue medication use in non-specific low back pain, from two trials totalling 315 participants; the 2006 version of the same review had called those identical trials 'strong evidence', and the downgrade reflects stricter GRADE assessment rather than new negative trials. The largest placebo-controlled trial (197 patients, 4 weeks) found 10 pain-free patients on 1,200 mg extract versus 6 on 600 mg and 3 on placebo (p=0.027, one-tailed), but its own subsidiary analyses of the Arhus pain component pointed the other way, with benefit appearing greatest in the 600 mg group and in patients without severe or radiating pain. In osteoarthritis, four double-blind placebo-controlled RCTs were identified by a 2006 review, but that review judged the methodological quality of the existing trials generally poor and concluded the evidence 'cannot provide a definitive answer' to whether devil's claw works or whether it is safe; the two comparative trials with usable reporting lack placebo arms entirely - a 122-patient, 4-month comparison against diacerhein 100 mg/day found no efficacy difference between arms, and a 60-patient comparison against meloxicam showed improvement in both arms with no significant superiority. A 2019 GRADE-based evidence review of 19 dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain made an explicit recommendation against devil's claw. A 2026 structured review concludes the clinical evidence 'remains limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous formulations, variable dosages, short follow-up durations, and a scarcity of high-quality head-to-head randomized trials against NSAIDs,' and that current evidence does not support devil's claw as a general replacement for NSAIDs.

Doses studied: For low back pain, extract WS 1531 at 600 mg/day (50 mg harpagoside) or 1,200 mg/day (100 mg harpagoside) for 4 weeks (PMID 10101629), and preparations standardized to 50 mg or 100 mg harpagoside daily in the trials pooled by Cochrane (PMID 25536022). For osteoarthritis, Harpadol at 6 capsules/day of 435 mg cryoground powdered root, that is 2,610 mg/day of whole root powder, for 4 months (PMID 11185727), and Teltonal at 2,480 mg/day for one month (PMID 34712343). Standardized-harpagoside extracts and whole powdered root are not interchangeable, and the 2026 review names formulation and dosage heterogeneity as a central limitation of the evidence base.

Why this grade: Placebo-controlled randomized evidence exists, so this is not merely traditional, but it does not reach 'moderate'. The 2014 Cochrane review rates devil's claw for non-specific low back pain as low-quality evidence from two trials totalling 315 participants - a downgrade from the 'strong evidence' the 2006 version of the same Cochrane review reported for the same trials, reflecting stricter GRADE assessment rather than new negative data. In osteoarthritis, placebo-controlled trials do exist (a 2006 review identified four double-blind placebo-controlled RCTs among 14 studies) but their methodological quality was judged generally poor, and the two comparative trials with usable modern reporting both lack a placebo arm, so neither can distinguish two effective treatments from two ineffective ones. A 2019 GRADE-based evidence review of dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain recommended against devil's claw. Independent reviews in 2006 and again in 2026 reach the same conclusion, that the trials are too small, too heterogeneous in formulation and dose, and too short to answer whether it works. Small, few, methodologically limited human trials with a formal recommendation against is 'early', and the lower grade is taken deliberately.

Sources (8)
  1. Herbal medicine for low-back pain.
    Oltean H, Robbins C, van Tulder MW, Berman BM, Bombardier C, Gagnier JJ, 2014 · PMID 25536022 · mixed

    Cochrane review of 14 RCTs (2,050 participants) in non-specific low back pain. Daily doses of Harpagophytum procumbens standardized to 50 mg or 100 mg harpagoside 'may be better than placebo for short-term improvements in pain and may reduce use of rescue medication' - low quality evidence from two trials, 315 participants. A separate Harpagophytum trial demonstrating relative equivalence to rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day (88 participants) was graded very low quality. No significant adverse events were noted within the included trials, and the completeness of reporting across the herbal trials was described as generally poor.

  2. Herbal medicine for low back pain.
    Gagnier JJ, van Tulder M, Berman B, Bombardier C, 2006 · PMID 16625605 · positive

    The 2006 version of the same Cochrane review, including ten trials. It reported that 'Two high quality trials examining the effects of Harpagophytum Procumbens (Devil's Claw) found strong evidence that daily doses standardized to 50 mg or 100 mg harpagoside were better than placebo for short-term improvements in pain and rescue medication,' with another high quality trial demonstrating relative equivalence to rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day. Cited here to document that the 2014 GRADE reassessment downgraded this same evidence from 'strong' to 'low quality' without any new negative trial - a change in appraisal standards, not in the data.

  3. Effectiveness of Harpagophytum extract WS 1531 in the treatment of exacerbation of low back pain: a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind study.
    Chrubasik S, Junck H, Breitschwerdt H, Conradt C, Zappe H, 1999 · PMID 10101629 · mixed

    197 patients with chronic susceptibility to back pain and current exacerbation worse than 5 on a 0-10 scale were randomized to Harpagophytum WS 1531 600 mg/day (50 mg harpagoside), 1,200 mg/day (100 mg harpagoside) or placebo for 4 weeks with tramadol rescue; 183 completed. Patients pain-free without rescue medication for 5 of the last 7 days were 3 on placebo, 6 on 600 mg and 10 on 1,200 mg (p=0.027, one-tailed Cochrane-Armitage test). Subgroup analyses suggested the effect was confined to patients with more severe radiating pain and neurological deficit, but subsidiary analyses of the Arhus current-pain component 'painted a slightly different picture,' with benefits seeming greatest in the 600 mg group and in patients without severe pain, radiation or neurological deficit. No Harpagophytum-related side effects except possibly mild, infrequent gastrointestinal symptoms.

  4. Dietary Ingredients as an Alternative Approach for Mitigating Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: Evidence-Based Recommendations for Practice and Research in the Military.
    Crawford C, Boyd C, Paat CF, Meissner K, Lentino C, Teo L, Berry K, Deuster P, 2019 · PMID 30986309 · negative

    A steering committee assessed 19 dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain using systematic review, meta-analysis where feasible, and GRADE. Recommendations were made AGAINST the use of devil's claw, alongside collagen, creatine, l-carnitine, methylsulfonylmethane, pycnogenol, willow bark extract and vitamin E. Eight other ingredients including curcuma were conditionally recommended, and no recommendation was made for boswellia. The panel concluded the scientific evidence overall is 'insufficiently robust to establish definitive clinical practice guidelines.'

  5. Efficacy and tolerance of Harpagophytum procumbens versus diacerhein in treatment of osteoarthritis.
    Chantre P, Cappelaere A, Leblan D, Guedon D, Vandermander J, Fournie B, 2000 · PMID 11185727 · mixed

    Double-blind, randomized, multicentre trial in 122 patients with knee or hip osteoarthritis over 4 months: Harpadol at 6 capsules/day, each containing 435 mg cryoground powdered Harpagophytum root, versus diacerhein 100 mg/day. Spontaneous pain and the Lequesne functional index improved significantly over the study with no difference in efficacy between the two treatments. Harpadol patients used significantly less NSAID and analgesic rescue medication and had significantly fewer adverse events, with diarrhoea in 8.1% versus 26.7%. There was no placebo arm, so the trial cannot distinguish two effective treatments from two ineffective ones. The first author's affiliation is Laboratoires Arkopharma, which markets the test product.

  6. The Efficacy of Harpagophytum procumbens (Teltonal) in Patients with Knee Osteoarthritis: A Randomized Active-Controlled Clinical Trial.
    Farpour HR, Rajabi N, Ebrahimi B, 2021 · PMID 34712343 · null

    60 patients aged 40-60 with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 1-2 painful knee osteoarthritis were randomized to Harpagophytum (Teltonal, two tablets daily totalling 2,480 mg) for one month, or meloxicam 15 mg/day for ten days. VAS, Oxford Knee Score and WOMAC improved in both groups over 8 weeks (p<0.001), but 'no significant superiority was shown' for either arm; at 8 weeks VAS was 4.80 (SD 1.80) with Teltonal versus 5.06 (SD 1.43) with meloxicam, and WOMAC 25.73 (SD 10.11) versus 26.20 (SD 13.94). With no placebo arm and unequal treatment durations, within-group improvement cannot be separated from natural course, regression to the mean and placebo response.

  7. Harpagophytum procumbens in musculoskeletal disorders: current evidence and comparison with NSAIDs.
    Hong JY, Lee J, Kim H, Kim H, Jeon WJ, Yeo C, Lee YJ, Go HY, Ha IH, 2026 · PMID 42394971 · mixed

    Structured narrative review of mechanism, clinical efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics and regulatory status. Clinical studies, mainly in osteoarthritis and low back pain, suggest selected preparations may provide symptomatic benefit in some patients, but the clinical evidence base 'remains limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous formulations, variable dosages, short follow-up durations, and a scarcity of high-quality head-to-head randomized trials against NSAIDs.' Preclinical mechanistic findings are described as indirect evidence to be interpreted cautiously. NSAIDs are supported by a substantially broader and higher-certainty evidence base. Safety data suggest devil's claw is generally well tolerated, though product quality, standardization and long-term safety data remain variable across jurisdictions. The authors state that current evidence does not support positioning it as a general replacement for NSAIDs.

  8. Devil's Claw (Harpagophytum procumbens) as a treatment for osteoarthritis: a review of efficacy and safety.
    Brien S, Lewith GT, McGregor G, 2006 · PMID 17212570 · mixed

    Review of 14 clinical studies in osteoarthritis identified from 1966 to 2006: eight observational studies, two comparator trials (one open, one randomized) and four double-blind placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials, assessed for internal and external validity. Data from the higher-quality studies suggested devil's claw reduced pain, but 'many of the published trials lacked certain important methodological quality criteria' and safety assessment was limited by the small populations studied. Devil's claw appeared associated with minor risk relative to NSAIDs, but long-term assessment was lacking. The authors conclude the methodological quality of existing trials is generally poor and the clinical evidence 'cannot provide a definitive answer' to whether it works and whether it is safe, and that a definitive high-quality trial is needed.
