Fermented foods and probiotics (gut-brain axis)

Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Early evidence · for gut & digestion

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

The idea that gut bacteria talk to the brain is real science, but the human evidence for anxiety is genuinely mixed — some reviews of probiotic supplements find a modest benefit and others find essentially none. For fermented foods themselves, like yogurt, kefir, kimchi and sauerkraut, the research is younger and thinner: the one careful diet trial found people felt less stressed, but not clearly more than the comparison group did. They are a healthy, low-risk addition to most people's meals rather than a proven way to ease anxiety.

Safety

For most healthy adults, fermented foods and probiotics are well tolerated; the most common effects are temporary gas and bloating when intake increases, and the 4-week fermented and prebiotic diet was well tolerated in healthy adults. One practical caveat: many supermarket products that read as fermented are pasteurized after fermentation or are simply vinegar-brined and contain no live cultures, so the product in the shop often does not match what the trials tested. Probiotic supplements are also regulated as foods or dietary supplements rather than reviewed before sale, so strain identity and viable counts vary between products.

Who should be careful: People who are severely immunocompromised, critically ill, or have central venous catheters should not take probiotic supplements without medical supervision, because of rare reports of bloodstream infections. People taking MAOI antidepressants must avoid aged and fermented tyramine-rich foods. Those with histamine intolerance may react to aged fermented products, and many fermented foods (kimchi, miso, sauerkraut, some pickles) are high in sodium, which matters for people managing blood pressure.

Interactions: The clinically important interaction is tyramine in aged and fermented foods with MAOI antidepressants, which can cause dangerous blood-pressure spikes. Probiotic supplements are generally not recommended alongside profound immunosuppression without clinician input.

What the evidence says

Most controlled evidence concerns probiotic supplements rather than fermented foods as such, and it is inconsistent across meta-analyses. A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 clinical studies (1,527 individuals) found that probiotics did not significantly reduce anxiety symptoms in humans (Hedges' g -0.12, 95% CI -0.29 to 0.05, p=0.151) and found no differential effect between clinical and healthy human samples, even though the same review found a significant anxiolytic effect across 22 animal studies (Reis 2018). A 2019 meta-analysis of 34 controlled clinical trials found a small but statistically significant effect of probiotics on anxiety (d=-0.10, p=.03) and no effect of prebiotics (d=.12, p=.11), with the clinical-versus-community moderation reported for depression rather than anxiety (Liu 2019); a 2025 meta-analysis restricted to clinically diagnosed samples (23 RCTs, 1,401 patients, of which 9 assessed anxiety) reported a moderate reduction in anxiety with probiotics (SMD -0.59, 95% CI -0.98 to -0.19) but noted high heterogeneity across studies (Asad 2025). In healthy adults the signal is weaker still: an 8-week randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial of Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1 in 29 healthy men found no effect over placebo on mood, anxiety, stress or sleep measures, the cortisol response to a socially evaluated cold pressor test, cognitive performance, or inflammatory markers, despite strong preclinical findings (Kelly 2017). Evidence for whole foods is thinner: a 4-week RCT of a diet high in fermented and prebiotic foods in 45 healthy adults reduced perceived stress within the diet group (32% versus 17% in controls) but the between-group difference was not statistically significant and biological stress markers were unchanged, with higher dietary adherence associated with larger stress reductions (Berding 2023). Cross-sectional data from 710 young adults found fermented food consumption independently predicted fewer social anxiety symptoms, particularly among those high in neuroticism, but this design cannot establish causation (Hilimire 2015).

Doses studied: No standard food dose is established. Probiotic supplement trials typically used daily Lactobacillus and/or Bifidobacterium preparations for about 4-8 weeks; the whole-diet RCT used a diet high in fermented and prebiotic foods for 4 weeks.

Why this grade: Downgraded from 'moderate' on adversarial review. Almost all controlled evidence here tests probiotic SUPPLEMENTS, not fermented foods, and even that proxy evidence is inconsistent rather than overall positive: a 2018 meta-analysis of 14 clinical studies (1,527 people) found no significant reduction in human anxiety (g=-0.12, p=0.151) and no difference between clinical and healthy samples, a 2019 meta-analysis of 34 trials found a statistically significant but trivially small effect (d=-0.10), and only a 2025 meta-analysis restricted to clinically diagnosed samples found a moderate effect (SMD -0.59), with high heterogeneity acknowledged by its authors. The evidence for fermented foods themselves is a single 4-week RCT in 45 healthy adults whose between-group comparison was not statistically significant, plus one cross-sectional survey. Small, few and preliminary human trials on the food itself is the definition of 'early', and the rule to take the lower grade when torn applies here.

Sources (6)
  1. The anxiolytic effect of probiotics: A systematic review and meta-analysis of the clinical and preclinical literature.
    Reis, Ilardi & Punt, 2018 · PMID 29924822 · null

    Meta-analysis of 22 preclinical studies (743 animals) and 14 clinical studies (1,527 individuals): probiotics reduced anxiety-like behavior in animals (Hedges' g -0.47, 95% CI -0.77 to -0.16, p=0.004) but did NOT significantly reduce symptoms of anxiety in humans (Hedges' g -0.12, 95% CI -0.29 to 0.05, p=0.151), and did not differentially affect clinical versus healthy human samples.

  2. Prebiotics and probiotics for depression and anxiety: A systematic review and meta-analysis of controlled clinical trials.
    Liu, Walsh & Sheehan, 2019 · PMID 31004628 · mixed

    Random-effects meta-analysis of 34 controlled clinical trials: probiotics produced a small but statistically significant effect on anxiety (d=-0.10, p=.03), while prebiotics did not differ from placebo for anxiety (d=.12, p=.11). The larger effect in clinical/medical versus community samples (d=-0.45, p<.001) was reported for depression, not for anxiety.

  3. Effects of Prebiotics and Probiotics on Symptoms of Depression and Anxiety in Clinically Diagnosed Samples: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Asad et al., 2025 · PMID 39731509 · positive

    Across 23 RCTs involving 1,401 patients with clinically diagnosed conditions (9 trials assessing probiotics for anxiety), probiotics moderately reduced anxiety symptoms (SMD -0.59, 95% CI -0.98 to -0.19); high heterogeneity was observed across studies, with study duration and probiotic formulation contributing to variation in effect sizes.

  4. Lost in translation? The potential psychobiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus (JB-1) fails to modulate stress or cognitive performance in healthy male subjects.
    Kelly et al., 2017 · PMID 27865949 · null

    In an 8-week randomized placebo-controlled crossover trial in 29 healthy male volunteers, L. rhamnosus JB-1 showed no effect over placebo on mood, anxiety, stress or sleep quality measures, the HPA/cortisol response to a socially evaluated cold pressor test, cognitive performance and associated EEG measures, or basal and stimulated cytokine levels, despite strong preclinical findings in an anxious mouse strain.

  5. Feed your microbes to deal with stress: a psychobiotic diet impacts microbial stability and perceived stress in a healthy adult population.
    Berding et al., 2023 · PMID 36289300 · mixed

    In a 4-week RCT randomizing 45 healthy adults to a psychobiotic diet high in prebiotic and fermented foods (n=24) or control (n=21), perceived stress fell within the diet group (32% versus 17% in controls) but not significantly between groups; biological markers of stress were not affected, and microbial composition and function changed only subtly. Higher adherence to the diet was associated with larger decreases in perceived stress.

  6. Fermented foods, neuroticism, and social anxiety: An interaction model.
    Hilimire, DeVylder & Forestell, 2015 · PMID 25998000 · positive

    Cross-sectional study of 710 young adults: after controlling for demographics, general healthful food consumption and exercise frequency, fermented food consumption independently predicted fewer social anxiety symptoms and interacted with neuroticism, such that among those high in neuroticism more frequent fermented food consumption was associated with fewer social anxiety symptoms. The authors state the direction of causality cannot be determined from this design.

For gut & digestion

Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi and kombucha are popular for gut health, and pooled research does show a small overall benefit for IBS symptoms. The honest catch is that almost all of that benefit came from specific commercial cultured milk drinks with named bacterial strains - several of them studied by the company that makes them - and even so, the studies found no clear improvement in the two things that bother people most, pain and bloating. These are nourishing foods that are reasonable to try, but the evidence for them as a treatment for IBS is still early and thin, and some people find fermented foods make their symptoms worse rather than better.

Safety

Fermented foods are ordinary foods with a long record of safe consumption, and no serious adverse events attributable to them were reported in the IBS trials retrieved here. The realistic downside in IBS is symptom provocation rather than toxicity: the sauerkraut trial's authors concluded the effect of their product was largely attributable to its prebiotic (fermentable carbohydrate) content, and fermentable carbohydrate is exactly what provokes symptoms in a subset of people with IBS - so fermented vegetables and cultured dairy will suit some people and aggravate others. Fermented milk drinks still contain lactose and can provoke symptoms in people who do not tolerate it. Introduce one fermented food at a time in a small amount and judge it on your own response rather than assuming benefit; if it makes you worse, that is a meaningful result, not a failure of adherence. Salt-preserved ferments such as kimchi, sauerkraut and miso are high in sodium, which matters for anyone advised to limit salt.

Who should be careful: People who are significantly immunocompromised - including those on immunosuppressive drugs, after transplant, during chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, or with central venous catheters - should not start live-culture foods or probiotic products without their clinician's agreement. Home-brewed kombucha and home-fermented products carry contamination risk and are best avoided in pregnancy and in anyone immunocompromised; kombucha and kefir can also contain small amounts of alcohol, which is relevant in pregnancy, in liver disease and for anyone in recovery. Anyone with a confirmed lactose intolerance or milk allergy should avoid the dairy-based versions, which are also the only versions with meaningful trial support. As with any dietary change for gut symptoms, get a proper diagnosis first - unexplained weight loss, bleeding, anaemia or fever needs medical assessment, not a food experiment.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions were identified in the retrieved trials. Two practical cautions. Live-culture foods overlap with probiotic supplements, so taking both adds dose without added evidence of benefit. And fermented foods sit in direct tension with the restriction phase of a low-FODMAP diet, since several are sources of fermentable carbohydrate or lactose; if you are running a structured elimination diet, reintroduce fermented foods deliberately during the reintroduction phase rather than alongside restriction, so you can tell what is actually affecting you.

What the evidence says

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (1264 IBS patients) found fermented foods associated with symptom relief in the 12 trials reporting it (975 patients; RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.04-1.42, p=0.01, I-squared 0%) and a small improvement in global symptom scores (SMD -0.15, 95% CI -0.29 to -0.02, p=0.02, I-squared 46%), but NO significant improvement in abdominal pain scores or bloating scores; on subgroup analysis only fermented milk retained benefit (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.01-1.39). The authors state the conclusion should be interpreted with caution. The evidence base is dominated by strain-defined commercial dairy studied by its manufacturer. A 4-week trial in 34 patients with IBS-C found a fermented dairy product containing B. lactis DN-173 010 reduced maximal abdominal distension (median difference -39%, 95% CI -78 to -5, P=0.02), accelerated colonic transit (-12.2 h, 95% CI -22.8 to -1.6, P=0.026) and improved overall symptom severity (-0.5, 95% CI -1.0 to -0.05, P=0.032), though mean daytime distension showed only a non-significant trend (-1.52 cm, 95% CI -3.33 to 0.39, P=0.096); four co-authors were Danone-affiliated and the abstract itself refers to the product as 'this probiotic'. Against that, a trial of fermented milk containing B. lactis CNCM I-2494 in 106 randomised IBS patients, run entirely by Danone Nutricia Research staff, found no difference from control on gastrointestinal symptoms or breath hydrogen and methane across the whole cohort, with benefit appearing only in a post-hoc subgroup of 35 high hydrogen producers. Evidence for non-dairy fermented foods in IBS is minimal: a 6-week pilot in 34 IBS patients found sauerkraut improved IBS-SSS in both arms (-38.57 +/- 17.08 pasteurised vs -56.99 +/- 16.92 unpasteurised, both P<0.04) with no difference between them, and because both arms received sauerkraut there was no non-sauerkraut comparator; the authors concluded the effect was more likely attributable to prebiotic content than to viable lactic acid bacteria, and that the study was underpowered. Mechanistic support comes from outside IBS: a 17-week randomised trial in healthy adults (n=18/arm) found a high-fermented-food diet steadily increased microbiota diversity and decreased inflammatory markers, while a crossover of 250 g/day yogurt in 119 randomised healthy adults (110 completed) found only transient enrichment of yogurt-derived bacteria, with Streptococcus thermophilus rising from absent to 0.97% (95% CI 0.71-1.26), and no effect on faecal short-chain fatty acids or on blood markers of metabolic control, inflammation, immunity, oxidative stress or gut permeability.

Doses studied: IBS trials used specific commercial products rather than a general 'eat more fermented food' prescription. A fermented dairy product containing Bifidobacterium lactis DN-173 010 was consumed for 4 weeks; a fermented milk containing B. lactis CNCM I-2494 for 14 days; and a daily lacto-fermented sauerkraut supplement, pasteurised or unpasteurised, for 6 weeks. Non-IBS trials used a high-fermented-food diet over 17 weeks in healthy adults, and 250 g/day of yogurt (alone or with 50 g rolled oats) over a two-period crossover in healthy adults. No trial retrieved here established a dose for kimchi, miso or kefir in IBS.

Why this grade: One 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in IBS (16 RCTs, 1264 patients) shows a modest benefit for global symptom relief (RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.04-1.42), which by itself could read as moderate. Graded down to early, and the tension is stated rather than hidden, for three reasons. First, that same meta-analysis found NO significant improvement in abdominal pain or bloating - the two symptoms people most want help with - and its authors explicitly say the conclusion should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of current evidence. Second, the positive signal survives subgroup analysis only for fermented milk (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.01-1.39), meaning the evidence is for commercial cultured-milk products carrying defined probiotic strains rather than for fermented foods as a food category; there is essentially no adequately powered IBS trial evidence for kimchi, kombucha, miso or kefir as such, and the two fermented-milk trials that anchor the field were run by the manufacturer. Third, key individual trials are null: the larger fermented-milk RCT found no difference from control across the whole randomised cohort, and the one sauerkraut trial in IBS was underpowered and found no difference between live and pasteurised product. Taking the lower of two candidate grades, this is early.

Sources (6)
  1. Efficacy of fermented foods in irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Ding L, Duan J, Yang T, et al., 2025 (Front Nutr) · PMID 39839290 · mixed

    16 RCTs, 1264 IBS patients. Symptom relief favoured fermented foods in the 12 trials reporting it (975 patients; RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.04-1.42, p=0.01, I-squared 0%) and global symptom scores improved slightly (SMD -0.15, 95% CI -0.29 to -0.02, p=0.02, I-squared 46%). However there was NO significant improvement in abdominal pain scores or bloating scores, and only the fermented milk subgroup retained benefit (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.01-1.39). Authors state the conclusion should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of current evidence.

  2. Fasting breath H2 and gut microbiota metabolic potential are associated with the response to a fermented milk product in irritable bowel syndrome.
    Le Nevé B, Derrien M, Tap J, et al., 2019 (PLoS One) · PMID 30946757 · null

    Double-blind randomised parallel trial; 125 IBS patients included, 106 randomised, 14 days of fermented milk containing Bifidobacterium lactis CNCM I-2494 vs control. NO difference between groups on gastrointestinal symptoms or breath H2/CH4 in the whole cohort. Benefit appeared only in a post-hoc subgroup of 35 high hydrogen producers (fasting H2 of 10 ppm or more), where gastrointestinal discomfort only tended to decrease (p=0.05). All authors were affiliated with Danone Nutricia Research or Diana Nova.

  3. Lacto-fermented sauerkraut improves symptoms in IBS patients independent of product pasteurisation - a pilot study.
    Nielsen ES, Garnås E, Jensen KJ, et al., 2018 (Food Funct) · PMID 30256365 · mixed

    34 IBS patients, 6 weeks, randomised double-blind, comparing pasteurised (n=15) with unpasteurised (n=19) sauerkraut. Mean IBS-SSS change -38.57 +/- 17.08 vs -56.99 +/- 16.92; both improved significantly (P<0.04) but with NO difference between groups. Both arms received sauerkraut, so there was no non-sauerkraut comparator. Authors concluded the effect can to a large extent be attributed to potential prebiotics rather than viable lactic acid bacteria, and that the study was underpowered.

  4. Clinical trial: the effects of a fermented milk product containing Bifidobacterium lactis DN-173 010 on abdominal distension and gastrointestinal transit in irritable bowel syndrome with constipation.
    Agrawal A, Houghton LA, Morris J, et al., 2009 (Aliment Pharmacol Ther) · PMID 18801055 · positive

    Randomised double-blind parallel trial, 34 patients with IBS-C, 4 weeks. Maximal distension fell (median difference -39%, 95% CI -78 to -5, P=0.02); orocaecal (-1.2 h, P=0.049) and colonic (-12.2 h, 95% CI -22.8 to -1.6, P=0.026) transit accelerated; overall symptom severity improved (-0.5, 95% CI -1.0 to -0.05, P=0.032). Mean daytime distension showed only a non-significant trend (-1.52 cm, 95% CI -3.33 to 0.39, P=0.096). The abstract describes the intervention as 'this probiotic'; four co-authors were Danone-affiliated.

  5. Gut-microbiota-targeted diets modulate human immune status.
    Wastyk HC, Fragiadakis GK, Perelman D, et al., 2021 (Cell) · PMID 34256014 · positive

    17-week randomised prospective trial in healthy adults (n=18/arm), NOT an IBS population. A high-fermented-food diet steadily increased microbiota diversity and decreased inflammatory markers; in the comparator high-fibre arm the primary outcome (cytokine response score) was unchanged. Provides mechanistic plausibility only, not efficacy evidence in IBS.

  6. Impact of Yogurt and Rolled Oats Consumption on the Gut Microbiome: A Randomized Crossover Study Displaying Individual Responses and General Resilience.
    Thriene K, Stanislas V, Huang KD, Strowig T, Michels KB, 2026 (J Nutr) · PMID 41687784 · null

    Randomised open-label 2-period crossover, 119 healthy adults randomised and 110 completed, 250 g/day yogurt. Streptococcus thermophilus rose transiently from absent to 0.97% (95% CI 0.71-1.26), but there were NO additional effects on faecal short-chain fatty acid concentrations or on blood markers of metabolic control, inflammation, immunity, oxidative stress or gut permeability. Authors concluded a healthy gut microbiota is largely stable and resilient to short-term diet change.
