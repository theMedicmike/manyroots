Iberogast / STW 5 herbal combination

Early evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Iberogast is a liquid blend of nine plant extracts — a newer version has six — sold in Europe for gut symptoms. One good four-week trial found it eased abdominal pain and overall IBS symptoms better than a dummy liquid, which is more than most herbal blends can show. But no independent group has repeated that result in IBS, most of the wider research is for indigestion rather than IBS and is itself mixed, and the original formula contains an ingredient linked to rare but serious liver injury. This is one to talk through with a clinician rather than pick off a shelf.

Safety

The efficacy trials reported good tolerability: in the functional dyspepsia meta-analysis adverse events were similar with the herbal preparation and placebo, no serious adverse events occurred, and the safety analysis was supported by a reference-controlled study of 199 versus 198 patients. The important safety issue is compositional rather than symptomatic. Preclinical characterisation of the two formulations identifies STW 5 as extracts of Iberis amara (bitter candytuft), Melissa officinalis, Matricaria recutita (chamomile), Carum carvi (caraway), Mentha x piperita (peppermint), Glycyrrhiza glabra (licorice), Angelica archangelica, Silybum marianum (milk thistle) and Chelidonium majus (greater celandine), and STW 5-II as the same combination without the last three. Greater celandine is an established cause of idiosyncratic herb-induced liver injury, and a review of the causality-assessed case literature concluded the risk-to-benefit ratio of products containing it can be considered negative. Check the label of the specific product for greater celandine (Chelidonium majus) before use, and stop immediately and seek medical attention for jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, right upper abdominal pain or unexplained fatigue. Note also that the preparation contains a licorice root (Glycyrrhiza glabra) extract, which is relevant to blood pressure and potassium.

Who should be careful: Do not use a celandine-containing formulation if you have any liver disease, abnormal liver blood tests, or a history of drug- or herb-induced liver injury, and avoid heavy alcohol use while taking it. Because of the licorice component, anyone with high blood pressure, heart failure, kidney disease or low potassium, or taking diuretics, should not use it without clinician oversight. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Check the label for alcohol content, which matters for children, people in alcohol recovery, and anyone taking metronidazole or disulfiram. Alarm features — weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, onset after 50, family history of bowel cancer or coeliac disease — require medical assessment first.

Interactions: Reviews of the herbal IBS literature explicitly flag that evidence on herb-drug interactions for these preparations is limited, so absence of documented interactions is not evidence of safety. Practical concerns follow from the licorice constituent, which warrants caution alongside thiazide or loop diuretics, digoxin and corticosteroids. Anyone on hepatically metabolised drugs or with a complex medication list should have a pharmacist review the specific label. Tell any clinician ordering liver function tests that you are taking it.

What the evidence says

The anchor trial is a double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre study of 208 IBS patients (203 completers) randomised to commercial STW 5 (n=51), the research preparation STW 5-II (n=52), a bitter candytuft mono-extract (n=53) or placebo (n=52) for 4 weeks: both STW 5 (P=0.0009 for total abdominal pain, P=0.001 for IBS symptom score) and STW 5-II (P=0.0005, P=0.0003) beat placebo, while bitter candytuft alone did not (P=0.1473, P=0.1207), an internal contrast arguing the effect is not simply placebo response. That symptom-efficacy result has not been independently replicated in IBS; the only other placebo-controlled IBS study is a 10-patient crossover gas-challenge study in which STW5 reduced gas-related symptom perception (score increment 3.2 plus or minus 0.4 versus 4.0 plus or minus 0.3 on placebo, P=0.035) but did not change gas retention, evacuation or abdominal distension. A 2020 systematic review of 33 double-blind placebo-controlled herbal IBS trials credits STW 5 and STW 5-II with efficacy in rigorously designed trials but states that, other than peppermint oil, aloe vera and asafoetida, none of the positive trials have been replicated, which limits definitive statements. The wider functional dyspepsia evidence — a separate condition that should not be read across to IBS — is mixed rather than uniform: a 2004 meta-analysis pooled three placebo-controlled trials (STW 5 n=138 versus placebo n=135) and found an odds ratio of 0.22 (95% CI 0.11-0.47, P=0.001) for severity of the most bothersome gastrointestinal symptom, but a subsequent 103-patient multicentre placebo-controlled trial in functional dyspepsia with gastroparesis found the improvement in its primary gastrointestinal symptom score did not reach significance (P=0.08, with responder proportion P=0.03) and showed no acceleration of gastric emptying. A 2026 guideline-focused review notes Iberogast has shown clinical improvements across multiple trials but that peppermint oil remains the only plant-based therapy consistently acknowledged in adult and paediatric international IBS guidelines, and a 2025 review of herbal IBS therapies flags small samples, short durations, methodological weaknesses and limited long-term safety and herb-drug interaction data across this literature.

Doses studied: The pivotal IBS trial used the commercial STW 5 liquid preparation (nine plant extracts) and the research preparation STW 5-II (six extracts) over 4 weeks; the published abstract does not state the drop dose. Later placebo-controlled studies used 2 weeks of treatment before gas-challenge testing. Functional dyspepsia trials pooled in the 2004 meta-analysis, and a separate 103-patient trial, used the commercial STW 5 preparation over 4 weeks.

Why this grade: In IBS specifically, symptom-efficacy evidence rests on a single 4-week multicentre placebo-controlled trial (n=208) with clearly positive results that no independent group has repeated. The only other placebo-controlled IBS study is a 10-patient mechanistic crossover that improved gas-related symptom perception without changing gas retention, evacuation or distension — informative, but not a replication. A 2020 systematic review of 33 double-blind placebo-controlled herbal IBS trials states explicitly that, apart from peppermint oil, aloe vera and asafoetida, none of the positive herbal trials have been replicated, which limits definitive efficacy statements for STW 5. The larger evidence base is in functional dyspepsia, a different condition, and it is not uniform: a pooled meta-analysis of three placebo-controlled trials was clearly positive, but a later 103-patient placebo-controlled trial missed statistical significance on its primary symptom endpoint. Multiple reviews flag small samples, short durations and methodological weakness, and manufacturer involvement is common across the trial programme. Torn between 'moderate' and 'early', the lower grade applies. No trial cited here reported IBS-C versus IBS-D subtype results.

Sources (9)
  1. Treatment of irritable bowel syndrome with herbal preparations: results of a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, multi-centre trial.
    Madisch A, Holtmann G, Plein K, Hotz J, 2004 · PMID 14984373 · positive

    208 IBS patients randomised across four arms for 4 weeks: STW 5 (n=51), STW 5-II (n=52), bitter candytuft mono-extract (n=53), placebo (n=52); 203 completed. Intention-to-treat analysis: STW 5 and STW 5-II both beat placebo on total abdominal pain (P=0.0009 and P=0.0005) and IBS symptom score (P=0.001 and P=0.0003). The bitter candytuft mono-extract did not differ from placebo (P=0.1473 and P=0.1207). This remains the only placebo-controlled IBS trial of the combination reporting symptom efficacy as its main outcome.

  2. Clinical effects of STW 5 (Iberogast) are not based on acceleration of gastric emptying in patients with functional dyspepsia and gastroparesis.
    Braden B, Caspary W, Borner N, Vinson B, Schneider ARJ, 2009 · PMID 19220753 · mixed

    103 patients with functional dyspepsia randomised to STW 5 or liquid placebo for 28 days in a multicentre double-blind study. The primary endpoint — change in a validated gastrointestinal symptom (GIS) score — did NOT reach statistical significance (P=0.08), although the proportion of patients with a treatment response did (P=0.03). Gastric half-emptying time was prolonged in 48.6% of the STW 5 group and 43.8% of placebo at baseline, and during treatment increased non-significantly on STW 5 (+23 plus or minus 109 min, P=0.51) while slightly accelerating on placebo (-26 plus or minus 51 min, P=0.77), between-group P=0.49. The authors conclude any clinical effect is not mediated by accelerated gastric emptying and that a clear-cut correlation with symptom improvement is still lacking.

  3. Effect of Iberogast (STW5) on tolerance to colonic gas in patients with irritable bowel syndrome: A randomized, double-blind, placebo control clinical trial.
    Aguilar A, Benslaiman B, Serra J, 2024 · PMID 38361151 · mixed

    Independent placebo-controlled crossover study in only 10 IBS patients with bloating, each tested after 2 weeks of STW5 and 2 weeks of placebo. Colonic gas filling raised abdominal symptom perception significantly less on STW5 (score increment 3.2 plus or minus 0.4) than on placebo (4.0 plus or minus 0.3, P=0.035), and perception and distension returned to baseline after evacuation on STW5 but remained elevated on placebo. However, abdominal distension during filling, gas retention and gas evacuation were similar on both treatments. A mechanistic gas-tolerance study, not a replication of the symptom-efficacy trial, and far too small to establish efficacy.

  4. Western herbal medicines in the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Hawrelak JA, Wohlmuth H, Pattinson M, Myers SP, Goldenberg JZ, Harnett J, et al., 2020 · PMID 31987249 · mixed

    33 double-blind placebo-controlled trials of 18 herbal preparations in IBS. STW 5 and STW 5-II are listed among preparations demonstrating efficacy in rigorously designed trials. The authors' central caveat: with the exception of peppermint essential oil, Aloe vera and asafoetida, none of the positive trials have been replicated, and this lack of replication limits the capacity to make definitive statements of efficacy for these herbal medicines, including STW 5.

  5. Meta-analysis: phytotherapy of functional dyspepsia with the herbal drug preparation STW 5 (Iberogast).
    Melzer J, Rosch W, Reichling J, Brignoli R, Saller R, 2004 · PMID 15606389 · positive

    Meta-analysis in FUNCTIONAL DYSPEPSIA, not IBS. Three placebo-controlled trials were reanalysed and pooled (STW 5 n=138 versus placebo n=135): STW 5 was more effective for severity of the most bothersome gastrointestinal symptom, odds ratio 0.22 (95% CI 0.11-0.47), P=0.001. A fourth randomised trial showed no significant difference between STW 5 and cisapride. The safety analysis was supported by one reference-controlled study (STW 5 n=199, control n=198); adverse events were similar with verum and placebo and no serious adverse events occurred. Included because it is the strongest STW 5 efficacy evidence, but it is in a different condition and does not transfer to IBS.

  6. Plant-Derived Treatments for IBS: Clinical Outcomes, Mechanistic Insights, and Their Position in International Guidelines.
    Pastras P, Aggeletopoulou I, Bali M, Triantos C, 2026 · PMID 41599795 · mixed

    Review mapping plant extracts studied in IBS against international guidelines. Iberogast (STW-5 and STW-5 II) is credited with clinical improvements across multiple trials, but the review's guideline analysis concludes peppermint oil is the only plant-based therapy consistently acknowledged across adult and paediatric recommendations. Iberogast, despite its positive trial, has not been taken up in major IBS guidelines.

  7. Natural Remedies for Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Comprehensive Review of Herbal-Based Therapies.
    Abalo R, Gallego-Barcelo P, Gabbia D, 2025 · PMID 41096612 · mixed

    Comprehensive review of herbal therapies in IBS. Peppermint oil and Iberogast are named as the two with demonstrated efficacy in randomised controlled trials, but the authors state that many studies suffer from small sample sizes, short durations and methodological weaknesses, that large-scale long-term randomised trials are lacking for most herbal remedies, and that evidence on herb-drug interactions and long-term safety profiles is limited.

  8. Antiulcerogenic effect of some gastrointestinally acting plant extracts and their combination.
    Khayyal MT, el-Ghazaly MA, Kenawy SA, Seif-el-Nasr M, Mahran LG, Kafafi YA, Okpanyi SN, 2001 · PMID 11505785 · mixed

    Animal (rat) study, cited here to document formulation composition rather than as evidence of clinical benefit. It identifies the commercial preparation STW 5 (Iberogast) as the combination of extracts from Iberis amara, Melissa officinalis, Matricaria recutita, Carum carvi, Mentha x piperita, Glycyrrhiza glabra, Angelica archangelica, Silybum marianum and Chelidonium majus, and the modified formulation STW 5-II as the same combination lacking the last three constituents. In indometacin-induced rat gastric ulcers the combined formulations produced anti-ulcerogenic activity comparable to cimetidine, with reduced acid output and increased mucin secretion.

  9. Hepatotoxicity induced by greater celandine (Chelidonium majus L.): a review of the literature.
    Pantano F, Mannocchi G, Marinelli E, Gentili S, Graziano S, Busardo FP, di Luca NM, 2017 · PMID 28379595 · negative

    Literature review of Chelidonium majus (greater celandine), one of the nine extracts in the original STW 5 formulation. Multiple hepatotoxicity cases were judged probably or highly probably connected to greater celandine exposure using liver-targeted causality assessment methods, and the injury is characterised as a distinct idiosyncratic, metabolic-type herb-induced liver injury. Given the absence of demonstrated benefit from greater celandine itself, the authors conclude the risk-to-benefit ratio of herbal products containing greater celandine can be considered negative.
