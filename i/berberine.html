Berberine

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Berberine is a yellow compound from plants like goldenseal and barberry, and unlike most supplements it does measurably lower blood sugar in people with type 2 diabetes over a few months. That also means it behaves like a drug: it commonly causes constipation or stomach upset, it can raise blood levels of other medicines you take, and it should be avoided in pregnancy, breastfeeding and newborns. Because it does something measurable, it gets sold as "natural metformin" or "nature's Ozempic." It is not a substitute for either, and the evidence says so directly rather than by disclaimer: the largest pooled analysis, 50 studies and 4,150 people, found berberine lowered the three-month average blood sugar only when it was taken alongside prescribed glucose-lowering drugs, not when it was taken on its own. Nobody has tested whether it prevents any of the things diabetes actually does to people, so treat it as something to raise with your prescriber, and never as a reason to stop or cut back medication you have been prescribed.

Safety

Berberine is not a replacement for prescribed glucose-lowering medication and nothing here is a reason to stop or reduce a prescription; changes to diabetes medication belong to the prescriber. Gastrointestinal effects are the rule rather than the exception: 34.5% of patients had transient GI adverse effects in an early trial (Yin et al. 2008, PMID 18442638); across 12 placebo-controlled lipid trials GI adverse events occurred in 2-23% on berberine versus 2-15% on placebo (Blais et al. 2023, PMID 36941490); and in colorectal adenoma trials adverse events were nearly three times more frequent than placebo (RR 2.91), constipation most often, resolving on stopping (Fang et al. 2021, PMID 34509605). No serious adverse events appear in the diabetes meta-analyses, but trials rarely exceeded 3 months, so long-term safety is unknown. Product quality varies and berberine is often sold in combination products.

Who should be careful: Pregnancy, breastfeeding and infants: berberine displaces bilirubin from albumin in vitro about tenfold more potently than phenylbutazone and reduced bilirubin protein binding in rats, and the authors advise avoiding berberine-rich herbs in jaundiced neonates and pregnant women (Chan 1993, PMID 8513024); berberine-containing Rhizoma coptidis was implicated in neonatal jaundice and kernicterus in G6PD-deficient newborns and was banned in Singapore from 1978, a ban later lifted after review (Ho et al. 2014, PMID 25610485). People taking cyclosporine or tacrolimus, or other narrow-therapeutic-index drugs cleared by CYP3A4, CYP2D6 or CYP2C9, should not add berberine without prescriber supervision. The same applies to warfarin, cleared by CYP2C9, and to statins such as simvastatin and atorvastatin, cleared by CYP3A4. Type 1 diabetes: not studied, and never a substitute for insulin.

Interactions: Berberine has genuine drug-like pharmacology. It inhibits CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 in humans after two weeks of use (Guo et al. 2012, PMID 21870106), so blood levels of many prescription drugs can rise; cyclosporine trough concentrations were 29.3% higher and AUC 34.5% higher with co-administration (Wu et al. 2005, PMID 16133554). Two everyday co-medications deserve naming because they sit in the same cabinet as a blood-sugar supplement. Statins: simvastatin and atorvastatin are cleared by CYP3A4, the enzyme berberine inhibits (midazolam AUC +40%, Guo et al. 2012, PMID 21870106; a goldenseal extract standardised to berberine raised midazolam AUC 1.43-fold, 90% CI 1.35-1.53, Nguyen et al. 2021, PMID 33174626), so berberine can raise statin blood levels, and higher statin levels are what drives muscle pain and, rarely, rhabdomyolysis; in laboratory work berberine combined with simvastatin or atorvastatin inhibited CYP3A4 more than berberine alone and further reduced the hERG cardiac potassium current, which the authors link to QT prolongation and cardiotoxicity (Feng et al. 2018, PMID 30086269 - cell and rat data, not a human trial). Set against that mechanism, the human lipid trials of the combination have not shown more muscle problems: a meta-analysis of 11 RCTs in 1,386 patients found transaminase elevation and muscle aches less frequent on berberine alone or berberine plus simvastatin than on control, with constipation more frequent, though the authors judged the included studies to be of unsatisfactory quality (Zhang et al. 2019, PMID 31094214). The interaction is real and its clinical size in people is unmeasured, so a statin is a reason to involve your prescriber, not a reason to assume you are safe either way. Warfarin: berberine reduced CYP2C9 activity in healthy volunteers (the losartan metabolic ratio doubled, Guo et al. 2012, PMID 21870106), and CYP2C9 clears the active S-isomer of warfarin, so a rise in INR and bleeding risk is pharmacologically predictable even though no clinical study has measured it. Digoxin and other P-glycoprotein substrates may likewise accumulate: in rats, oral digoxin AUC rose to 123-170% of control after berberine while intravenous digoxin was unchanged, consistent with inhibition of intestinal P-glycoprotein (Qiu et al. 2009, PMID 19370549); this has not been tested in people. If you take a statin, warfarin, digoxin, cyclosporine or tacrolimus, do not start berberine without telling your prescriber and pharmacist first. Berberine also inhibits P-glycoprotein-type efflux and intestinal uptake transport in preclinical work, and a goldenseal (berberine-containing) extract reduced metformin exposure by 23% in healthy volunteers (Nguyen et al. 2021, PMID 33174626), though a mouse study indicated berberine alone did not precipitate that interaction (Oyanna et al. 2023, PMID 37562957). Additive glucose lowering with insulin or sulfonylureas is pharmacologically plausible and warrants closer glucose monitoring, although pooled trial data did not show an increase in hypoglycaemia (Xie et al. 2022, PMID 36467075).

What the evidence says

Every result below is a surrogate marker (HbA1c, fasting glucose, post-meal glucose), not a prevented heart attack, stroke, amputation, kidney failure or death; no berberine trial has measured a hard clinical outcome or progression from prediabetes to diabetes. In PREMOTE (409 newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients, 20 centres, 12 weeks, double-blind, all arms preceded by a one-week gentamycin run-in), berberine alone lowered HbA1c by 0.99% (95% CI -1.16 to -0.83) versus 0.59% with placebo, a placebo-adjusted difference of about 0.4 percentage points, with more gastrointestinal side effects on berberine. An earlier placebo-controlled trial (116 patients, 1.0 g/day, 3 months) found HbA1c falling from 7.5% to 6.6% and LDL cholesterol from 3.23 to 2.55 mmol/L. Meta-analyses of mostly Chinese RCTs agree in direction: Xie et al. (37 RCTs, 3,048 patients) report fasting glucose -0.82 mmol/L and HbA1c -0.63%; Guo et al. (46 trials, PMID 34956436) HbA1c -0.73% (95% CI -0.97 to -0.51) for berberine alone or added to standard therapy. The largest, Wang et al. (50 studies, 4,150 participants), is more revealing: berberine alone significantly reduced fasting glucose (-0.59 mmol/L, P=0.048) and 2-hour glucose (-1.57 mmol/L), but a significant HbA1c reduction (-0.69%) was reported only for berberine combined with glucose-lowering drugs. Signals of weakness: Liang et al. found the effect became unremarkable when treatment ran beyond 90 days, when the dose exceeded 2 g/day, or in people over 60; and a 2026 meta-analysis that pooled probiotics, synbiotics and berberine together across more than 30 trials found a combined HbA1c reduction of only 0.19% and concluded these belong as adjunctive rather than primary therapy. Prediabetes evidence is one small manufacturer-product pilot (34 people, 12 weeks, HbA1c 6.40% to 5.43%; PMID 37679692). Pharmacologically berberine is not inert: 300 mg three times daily for two weeks inhibited CYP2D6 (urinary dextromethorphan/dextrorphan ratio up ninefold), CYP2C9 (losartan ratio doubled) and CYP3A4 (midazolam AUC +40%, oral clearance -27%) in healthy volunteers, and in renal transplant recipients it left cyclosporine trough concentrations 29.3% higher, with cyclosporine AUC up 34.5% in a companion pharmacokinetic substudy.

Doses studied: Most trials used 0.9-1.5 g/day (commonly 500 mg two or three times daily with meals) for 1-3 months in type 2 diabetes; 1.0 g/day for 3 months in the key placebo-controlled RCT; 500 mg three times daily for 12 weeks in the 34-person prediabetes pilot.

Why this grade: Consistent glucose-lowering across more than 30 randomized trials and several meta-analyses, anchored by one large multicentre double-blind RCT (PREMOTE, n=409) in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Held at moderate rather than strong because every endpoint measured is a surrogate: no berberine trial has measured cardiovascular events, kidney disease, amputation, death, or progression from prediabetes to diabetes, and no clinical guideline endorses it. Additional reasons to stay at moderate: almost all trials are short (1-3 months), of low-to-moderate quality and overwhelmingly from one country; the placebo-adjusted HbA1c difference in the best trial is about 0.4 percentage points; the largest meta-analysis (50 studies, 4,150 participants) reported a significant HbA1c reduction only for berberine combined with glucose-lowering drugs, not for berberine alone; one meta-analysis found the effect became unremarkable beyond 90 days; and berberine carries real drug-interaction and tolerability costs.

Sources (13)
  1. Gut microbiome-related effects of berberine and probiotics on type 2 diabetes (the PREMOTE study).
    Zhang Y, Gu Y, Ren H, et al., 2020 · PMID 33024120 · positive

    Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 409 newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients, 20 centres in China, 12 weeks after a one-week gentamycin run-in: HbA1c fell 0.99% (95% CI -1.16 to -0.83) with berberine alone and 1.04% with probiotics plus berberine, versus 0.59% with placebo (P<0.001); berberine induced more gastrointestinal side effects.

  2. Treatment of type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia with the natural plant alkaloid berberine.
    Zhang Y, Li X, Zou D, et al., 2008 · PMID 18397984 · positive

    116 patients with type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia randomized to berberine 1.0 g/day or placebo for 3 months: HbA1c fell from 7.5% to 6.6%, fasting glucose from 7.0 to 5.6 mmol/L and LDL cholesterol from 3.23 to 2.55 mmol/L, all differing significantly from placebo (P<0.0001); glucose disposal rate rose within the berberine group (P=0.037) but did not differ significantly between groups (P=0.063); mild-to-moderate constipation occurred in 5 berberine participants.

  3. Glucose-lowering effect of berberine on type 2 diabetes: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Xie W, Su F, Wang G, et al., 2022 · PMID 36467075 · positive

    37 RCTs, 3,048 patients with type 2 diabetes: berberine reduced fasting glucose by 0.82 mmol/L (95% CI -0.95 to -0.70), HbA1c by 0.63% (95% CI -0.72 to -0.53) and 2-h postprandial glucose by 1.16 mmol/L; it did not increase total adverse events (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.55-0.97) or hypoglycaemia (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.21-1.08).

  4. Effects of administering berberine alone or in combination on type 2 diabetes mellitus: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Wang J, Bi C, Xi H, Wei F, 2024 · PMID 39640489 · mixed

    Largest pooled analysis to date, 50 studies and 4,150 participants: berberine ALONE significantly reduced fasting glucose (MD -0.59 mmol/L, P=0.048), 2-h postprandial glucose (-1.57 mmol/L), LDL-C, total cholesterol and triglycerides, but a significant HbA1c reduction (MD -0.69%) was reported only when berberine was combined with glucose-lowering drugs; most common dose 0.9-1.5 g/day for 1-3 months.

  5. Effects of berberine on blood glucose in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a systematic literature review and a meta-analysis.
    Liang Y, Xu X, Yin M, et al., 2019 · PMID 30393248 · mixed

    28 RCTs, 2,313 patients: berberine lowered fasting glucose by 0.54 mmol/L (95% CI -0.77 to -0.30), postprandial glucose by 0.94 mmol/L and HbA1c by 0.54 (95% CI -0.93 to -0.15), but subgroup analyses showed the glucose-lowering effect became unremarkable when treatment lasted more than 90 days, when the daily dose exceeded 2 g, or in patients older than 60.

  6. Probiotics, synbiotics and berberine in Type 2 diabetes mellitus: A systematic review, meta-analysis, and molecular dynamics simulation study.
    Shadin M, Bhuia MS, Alfaifi M, et al., 2026 · PMID 42213651 · mixed

    More than 30 trials and over 2,000 participants pooled ACROSS probiotics, synbiotics and berberine together (not berberine separately): combined reductions were statistically significant but clinically modest, fasting glucose -0.71 mmol/L and HbA1c -0.19%, with substantial between-study variability; the authors conclude these support use as adjunctive rather than primary therapeutic options and call for larger, longer, standardized trials.

  7. Repeated administration of berberine inhibits cytochromes P450 in humans.
    Guo Y, Chen Y, Tan ZR, Klaassen CD, Zhou HH, 2012 · PMID 21870106 · negative

    Randomized crossover study in healthy men: after 2 weeks of berberine 300 mg three times daily, CYP2D6 activity fell (urinary dextromethorphan/dextrorphan ratio increased ninefold, P<0.01), CYP2C9 activity fell (losartan/E-3174 ratio doubled, P<0.01) and CYP3A4 was inhibited (midazolam Cmax +38%, AUC0-inf +40%, oral clearance -27%); the authors conclude drug-drug interactions must be considered when berberine is administered.

  8. Effects of berberine on the blood concentration of cyclosporin A in renal transplanted recipients: clinical and pharmacokinetic study.
    Wu X, Li Q, Xin H, Yu A, Zhong M, 2005 · PMID 16133554 · negative

    Randomized controlled trial in 104 renal transplant recipients (52 per arm): berberine 0.2 g three times daily for 3 months left final cyclosporine trough concentrations 29.3% higher than in berberine-free patients (P<0.05); in a companion 6-patient pharmacokinetic study cyclosporine AUC rose 34.5% and apparent clearance fell 40.4%, consistent with CYP3A4 inhibition.

  9. Efficacy and safety of berberine in preventing recurrence of colorectal adenomas: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Fang S, Guo S, Du S, et al., 2021 · PMID 34509605 · negative

    3 RCTs, 1,076 patients: berberine produced more adverse events than placebo (RR 2.91, 95% CI 1.24 to 6.85, P=0.01), with constipation the most common; no serious adverse events were observed on full-text reading and events resolved on discontinuation.

  10. The enhancement of cardiotoxicity that results from inhibiton of CYP 3A4 activity and hERG channel by berberine in combination with statins.
    Feng P, Zhao L, Guo F, et al., 2018 · PMID 30086269 · negative

    Laboratory study (human cell lines and rats, no human participants): berberine combined with simvastatin or atorvastatin inhibited CYP3A4 activity and CYP3A4 protein and mRNA expression more than berberine alone; simvastatin and atorvastatin reduced hERG potassium current by accelerating channel inactivation, and the berberine-statin combination inhibited hERG current more than either drug alone; the authors conclude the combination can increase cardiotoxicity by inhibiting CYP3A4 and the hERG channel, the channel whose blockade underlies long QT syndrome. Mechanistic signal only, not a measured clinical event rate.

  11. Assessing Transporter-Mediated Natural Product-Drug Interactions Via In vitro-In Vivo Extrapolation: Clinical Evaluation With a Probe Cocktail.
    Nguyen JT, Tian DD, Tanna RS, et al., 2021 · PMID 33174626 · negative

    Probe-cocktail study in 16 healthy volunteers of a goldenseal extract standardised to its major alkaloid berberine: midazolam AUC rose, geometric mean ratio 1.43 (90% CI 1.35-1.53), confirming CYP3A inhibition; metformin AUC fell, GMR 0.77 (90% CI 0.71-0.83), with no change in metformin half-life or renal clearance, which the authors note may have unfavourable effects on glucose control; no effect on rosuvastatin (BCRP, OATP1B1/3) or furosemide (OAT1/3).

  12. Effect of berberine on the pharmacokinetics of substrates of CYP3A and P-gp.
    Qiu W, Jiang XH, Liu CX, Ju Y, Jin JX, 2009 · PMID 19370549 · negative

    Rat study: after berberine pretreatment, the AUC of ORAL digoxin rose to 133% and 170% of control (single dose, berberine 30 and 100 mg/kg) and 123% and 169% (2-week dosing), while intravenous digoxin pharmacokinetics were unchanged, indicating inhibition of intestinal P-glycoprotein; oral cyclosporine A AUC rose 62-96%. Animal data only; not tested in people.

  13. Efficacy and Safety of Berberine Alone or Combined with Statins for the Treatment of Hyperlipidemia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Clinical Trials.
    Zhang LS, Zhang JH, Feng R, et al., 2019 · PMID 31094214 · mixed

    11 RCTs, 1,386 patients with hyperlipidaemia: the incidence of adverse reactions including transaminase elevation and muscle aches was LOWER with berberine alone or berberine plus simvastatin than with control, while constipation was more frequent; berberine plus simvastatin reduced triglyceride and total cholesterol more than simvastatin alone. The authors state the quantity and quality of included studies were unsatisfactory, which may reduce the reliability of the results. Relevant here as the only human counterweight to the laboratory berberine-statin cardiotoxicity signal.
