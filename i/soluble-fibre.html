Soluble fibre (psyllium/ispaghula)

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Not all fibre behaves the same way in IBS, and this is one of the few places where the distinction really changes what happens to you. Soluble fibre - psyllium, also sold as ispaghula husk - has decent trial evidence for easing overall IBS symptoms, with the clearest signal for symptoms overall and for constipation. Coarse insoluble fibre like wheat bran is a different story: it has failed to help in the trials and it made a substantial share of people worse, so 'just eat more bran' is advice worth ignoring.

Safety

Psyllium is generally well tolerated but commonly causes gas, bloating and abdominal distension in the first one to two weeks, which is the main reason people abandon it; building the dose up over several weeks reduces this. It must be taken with a full glass of liquid and adequate fluid through the day - taken dry or with too little water it can swell and cause choking or oesophageal blockage. Coarse insoluble fibre (wheat bran) is a different intervention with a different risk profile in IBS: it failed to improve any outcome in pooled analyses, drove the highest early dropout in the largest randomised trial because symptoms worsened, and 55% of surveyed IBS patients reported it made them worse. Fibre is a symptom-management step, not a diagnostic one: new or changing bowel symptoms with rectal bleeding, unintended weight loss, anaemia, a family history of bowel cancer or coeliac disease, or onset after age 50 need medical assessment before self-treating.

Who should be careful: Avoid in suspected or known bowel obstruction, intestinal strictures, faecal impaction, and in anyone with swallowing difficulty or who cannot reliably drink enough fluid with the dose. People with a history of gastrointestinal surgery causing narrowing, or with severe gastroparesis, should take medical advice first. Psyllium allergy is rare but real - IgE-mediated sensitisation and anaphylaxis are documented in consumers of psyllium laxatives and, occupationally, in healthcare and manufacturing workers who handle the powder; stop immediately for rash, wheeze or throat tightness.

Interactions: Bulk-forming fibre can slow or reduce absorption of other oral medicines; take other drugs at least 1-2 hours before or 2-4 hours after psyllium. Extra caution with narrow-margin drugs (levothyroxine, lithium, carbamazepine, digoxin, warfarin) and with diabetes medicines, since fibre can blunt post-meal glucose rises and doses may need review.

What the evidence says

Reviews that separate fibre by type consistently split the two. A 17-trial systematic review found soluble fibre improved global IBS symptoms (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.35-1.78) while insoluble fibre did not (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.72-1.11), with the authors noting insoluble fibre may in some cases worsen the clinical outcome and no evidence that fibre relieves abdominal pain; a later 22-trial review reported the same split, RR 1.49 (95% CI 1.09-2.03) for soluble fibre with abdominal pain mean difference -1.84 (95% CI -2.72 to -0.97), and no outcome improved by insoluble fibre. In the largest primary-care randomised trial (n = 275; 10 g psyllium, 10 g bran or placebo for 12 weeks), adequate relief was reported by 57% on psyllium vs 35% on placebo in month 1 (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.13-2.26) and 59% vs 41% in month 2 (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.02-2.06), with symptom severity falling 90 points on psyllium vs 49 on placebo at 3 months (p = 0.03); in that same trial early dropout was most frequent in the bran arm, and worsening IBS symptoms was the main reason. Contrary evidence is real and substantial. A 2011 Cochrane review of 12 bulking-agent trials (621 patients) found no benefit for global assessment (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.91-1.33; 11 studies, 565 patients), abdominal pain (SMD 0.03) or symptom score (SMD -0.00), with soluble and insoluble subgroups both non-significant. A 2019 network meta-analysis of 51 trials in 4,644 patients placed soluble fibre alongside the other traditional first-line options and found no significant difference between any active treatments, with peppermint oil ranked first and tricyclic antidepressants second, and only 13 of the 51 trials at low risk of bias. On subtype, the trial evidence is thin: the 2004 review found benefit in IBS-related constipation (RR 1.56, 95% CI 1.21-2.02), which fits the proposed mechanism of holding water and giving stool more form, but the trials were not designed to compare IBS-C with IBS-D, and fermentation of any added fibre can transiently increase gas and bloating.

Doses studied: 10 g/day psyllium (ispaghula husk) for 12 weeks in the largest primary-care trial, in split doses; other trials used ispaghula, calcium polycarbophil and comparable soluble fibres, with treatment periods generally of 4-12 weeks. No optimal starting dose has been established in trials. Gas and bloating in the first weeks are the usual reason people stop, so building up gradually rather than starting at the full trial dose is the practical approach.

Why this grade: Two systematic reviews that analysed fibre types separately both found benefit for soluble fibre (RR 1.55 and RR 1.49 for global symptom improvement) and none for insoluble fibre, and the largest primary-care randomised trial found psyllium superior to placebo in months 1 and 2. It stops short of strong on three counts: a Cochrane review of bulking agents found no significant benefit overall or in the soluble subgroup; a 2019 network meta-analysis of 51 trials ranked soluble fibre below peppermint oil and tricyclic antidepressants with no active treatment significantly better than another, and judged only 13 of those 51 trials to be at low risk of bias; and benefit on abdominal pain specifically was not demonstrated in the earlier review. Conservatively graded moderate rather than strong on the strength of that contrary evidence.

Sources (6)
  1. Systematic review: the role of different types of fibre in the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome.
    Bijkerk, Muris, Knottnerus, Hoes & de Wit, 2004 · PMID 14984370 · mixed

    17 randomised trials. Soluble fibre (psyllium, ispaghula, calcium polycarbophil) improved global IBS symptoms, RR 1.55 (95% CI 1.35-1.78); insoluble fibre (corn, wheat bran) did not, RR 0.89 (95% CI 0.72-1.11), and the authors state insoluble fibre may in some cases worsen the clinical outcome. Fibre overall gave RR 1.33 (1.19-1.50) for global symptoms and RR 1.56 (1.21-2.02) in constipation, but there was no evidence of benefit for abdominal pain.

  2. Soluble or insoluble fibre in irritable bowel syndrome in primary care? Randomised placebo controlled trial.
    Bijkerk, de Wit, Muris, Whorwell, Knottnerus & Hoes, 2009 · PMID 19713235 · positive

    275 primary-care patients randomised to 10 g psyllium, 10 g bran or placebo for 12 weeks. Adequate relief: psyllium 57% vs placebo 35% in month 1 (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.13-2.26) and 59% vs 41% in month 2 (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.02-2.06). Symptom severity fell 90 points on psyllium vs 49 on placebo (p = 0.03) and 58 on bran (p = 0.61 vs placebo). Bran's month-3 result was not significant in worst-case analysis (RR 1.45, 0.97-2.16), and early dropout was most common in the bran group, mainly because IBS symptoms worsened.

  3. The role of fiber supplementation in the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Nagarajan, Morden, Bischof, King, Kosztowski, Wick & Stein, 2015 · PMID 26148247 · positive

    22 randomised/crossover trials. Fibre overall improved global symptom assessment (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.05-1.54). Soluble fibre improved symptom assessment (RR 1.49, 95% CI 1.09-2.03) and abdominal pain score (mean difference -1.84, 95% CI -2.72 to -0.97); insoluble fibre showed no improvement in any outcome. The authors note moderate clinical, methodological and statistical heterogeneity and moderate risk of bias, and conclude there is no evidence for recommending insoluble fibre in IBS.

  4. Bulking agents, antispasmodics and antidepressants for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome.
    Ruepert, Quartero, de Wit, van der Heijden, Rubin & Muris, 2011 (Cochrane) · PMID 21833945 · null

    12 bulking-agent trials (621 patients) found no benefit over placebo for abdominal pain (4 studies, 186 patients; SMD 0.03, 95% CI -0.34 to 0.40; p = 0.87), global assessment (11 studies, 565 patients; RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.91-1.33; p = 0.32) or symptom score (3 studies, 126 patients; SMD -0.00, 95% CI -0.43 to 0.43). Subgroup analyses for soluble and insoluble fibre were both non-significant, and restricting to trials with adequate allocation concealment did not change the result. Conclusion: no evidence that bulking agents are effective for IBS.

  5. Efficacy of soluble fibre, antispasmodic drugs, and gut-brain neuromodulators in irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Black, Yuan, Selinger, Camilleri, Quigley, Moayyedi & Ford, 2019 · PMID 31859183 · mixed

    51 RCTs, 4,644 patients, comparing soluble fibre (ispaghula husk), antispasmodics, peppermint oil and gut-brain neuromodulators; only 13 trials were at low risk of bias. For failure to improve global IBS symptoms at 4-12 weeks, peppermint oil ranked first (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.48-0.83) and tricyclic antidepressants second (RR 0.66, 0.53-0.83), and there were no significant differences between active treatments on direct or indirect comparison. The authors state there is likely to be considerable uncertainty around the rankings because of the methodological quality of the trials, and that long-term relative efficacy is unknown.

  6. Bran and irritable bowel syndrome: time for reappraisal.
    Francis & Whorwell, 1994 · PMID 7912305 · negative

    Uncontrolled questionnaire survey of 100 consecutive IBS referrals who had all tried bran: 55% reported bran made them worse and only 10% found it helpful, with bowel disturbance most often adversely affected, followed by abdominal distension and pain. With the exception of fruit, other forms of dietary fibre were not as detrimental and proprietary supplements were reported as beneficial. Observational and subject to recall bias, but the earliest clear signal that insoluble fibre is not a neutral intervention in IBS.
