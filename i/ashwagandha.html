Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera)

Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy Early evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Ashwagandha is an herb from Ayurvedic tradition, and quite a few small studies report that people taking it feel less stressed and less anxious, with lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol. The studies are small, mostly funded by the companies selling the extracts, and reviewers grading the evidence rated their confidence in it as low, so the real benefit is probably much smaller than the headlines suggest. It is usually well tolerated, but rare cases of liver injury and thyroid problems have been reported, so it is not for everyone.

Safety

Generally well tolerated in trials up to 8 weeks: a 1,002-participant randomized safety study found fewer adverse events than placebo and no liver-test changes. However, post-marketing evidence documents rare cholestatic or mixed liver injury with jaundice attributed to ashwagandha supplements, typically self-limited over 1-5 months, and separate case reports document thyrotoxicosis and biopsy-consistent painless thyroiditis resolving after discontinuation; a placebo-controlled trial in bipolar disorder found T4 rose 7-24% in all three ashwagandha-treated patients while 6 of 7 placebo patients showed decreases. Anyone developing jaundice, dark urine, nausea, itching, unexplained weight loss, or heat intolerance while taking it should stop and seek medical evaluation. Long-term safety beyond a few months is not established, and most of the trial evidence comes from a small number of commercially sponsored research groups.

Who should be careful: Avoid in pregnancy (traditional and regulatory sources advise against use) and in pre-existing liver disease. Use caution in thyroid disorders, given documented case reports of thyrotoxicosis and trial data suggesting ashwagandha can raise thyroxine, and in autoimmune conditions given possible immune-stimulating activity. Supplement quality varies; several documented liver-injury cases involved multi-ingredient products.

Interactions: Possible additive effects with sedatives, thyroid hormone, and medications that lower blood pressure or blood sugar; caution with immunosuppressants and with other supplements carrying hepatotoxic potential.

What the evidence says

Evidence for ashwagandha root extract in chronic stress and anxiety comes from multiple small RCTs: a 60-day trial in 60 stressed adults found 240 mg/day of a standardized extract reduced HAM-A versus placebo (p=0.040) with only a near-significant DASS-21 change (p=0.096), alongside greater reductions in morning cortisol (p<0.001) and DHEA-S (p=0.004). A 2022 meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (n=1,002) reported large pooled reductions in anxiety (SMD -1.55, 95% CI -2.37 to -0.74) and stress (SMD -1.75, 95% CI -2.29 to -1.22), but with I-squared of 93.8% and 83.1% respectively and certainty of evidence rated low for both outcomes; a 2024 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (n=558) similarly found improvements on the Perceived Stress Scale (MD -4.72), Hamilton Anxiety Scale (MD -2.19), and serum cortisol (MD -2.58). An independent 2022 network meta-analysis placed Withania somnifera at HAM-A MD -4.90 (95% CrI -9.70 to -0.17) but noted its trials were limited by small sample sizes and that placebo effects may account for part of the apparent herbal benefit. A 2026 three-arm trial in 141 adults is the clearest caution: all three groups including placebo improved significantly on cortisol, PSS, HAM-A and mood scores, and only the proprietary extract — not the standard root extract — separated from placebo. On safety, an 8-week randomized study in 1,002 adults found 600 mg/day well tolerated, with fewer adverse events than placebo (5.6% vs 9.2%), no serious adverse events, and no liver, renal, or hematological changes. Against that, a case series from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network documented five cases of cholestatic or mixed liver injury with jaundice at 2-12 weeks' latency, resolving over 1-5 months, and separate case reports document thyrotoxicosis and painless thyroiditis resolving on discontinuation.

Doses studied: 240-600 mg/day of standardized root extract (commonly 300 mg twice daily), typically for 8 weeks; no efficacy trials extend meaningfully beyond that.

Why this grade: Downgraded from moderate. Although a dozen RCTs and several meta-analyses favor ashwagandha, the underlying trials are small, largely conducted in India with commercial extract sponsorship, and the pooled effects are implausibly large with heterogeneity of I-squared 83-94%; the 2022 meta-analysis rated certainty of evidence LOW for both anxiety and stress, and an independent network meta-analysis flagged small sample sizes and possible placebo effects. Decisively, a 2026 three-arm trial found placebo improved significantly on every measure and standard root extract did not separate from it. A documented hepatotoxicity signal and thyroid case reports further warrant caution. Torn between moderate and early, graded down.

Laboratory evaluation showed no significant changes in liver function tests (AST, ALT), renal markers, or hematological parameters, and tolerability ratings did not differ (p=0.487).

For fatigue & low energy

Ashwagandha is widely sold for energy and stress, and some small studies in healthy or athletic people report less mental fatigue and better exercise tolerance. But the one trial that specifically enrolled middle-aged men who were already tired found no real difference in fatigue between ashwagandha and a dummy capsule. It also carries a real, documented risk of liver injury and can shift thyroid hormone levels, so this is not a harmless first thing to try.

Safety

Ashwagandha has a documented and reproducible pattern of idiosyncratic drug-induced liver injury. A case series from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network describes jaundice, pruritus, nausea and lethargy after 2-12 weeks of use, with a cholestatic or mixed pattern (R ratios 1.4-3.3) and prolonged recovery (liver tests normalizing over 1-5 months). A 2026 scoping review of 25 published patients found the same syndrome and reported one case of acute liver failure requiring transplantation and three deaths among patients with pre-existing cirrhosis. Stop immediately and seek medical care for yellowing of the eyes or skin, dark urine, persistent itching, unusual fatigue with nausea, or right-upper-abdominal pain. Ashwagandha also measurably alters thyroid hormones: a randomized placebo-controlled trial in 50 people with subclinical hypothyroidism found 600 mg/day for 8 weeks significantly changed serum TSH (p < 0.001), T3 (p = 0.0031) and T4 (p = 0.0096) versus placebo, and a published case describes painless thyroiditis with thyrotoxicosis — presenting with fatigue, night fever and weight loss — that resolved after the supplement was stopped. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established in these trials.

Who should be careful: Do not use if you have cirrhosis or advanced chronic liver disease — the published deaths occurred in exactly that group, and the 2026 review explicitly advises those patients to avoid it. Avoid or use only under medical supervision if you have any liver disease, hepatitis, or unexplained abnormal liver enzymes; if you have thyroid disease or take thyroid medication; or if you are pregnant or breastfeeding. Persistent fatigue should be worked up for treatable causes such as anemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnea and depression rather than treated with a supplement that carries a documented liver-injury signal.

Interactions: Because ashwagandha can raise T3 and T4 and lower TSH, it can confound thyroid monitoring and may compound the effect of levothyroxine or other thyroid medication. Avoid stacking it with other supplements or medications known to stress the liver — in the published liver-injury series, four of five patients were taking additional supplements, and in one case rhodiola was named as a possible co-agent. Disclose it to any prescriber before surgery or before starting a new drug. Formal pharmacokinetic interaction data are limited.

What the evidence says

Human trials of ashwagandha rarely enrol people with persistent clinical fatigue, and the closest one was null on that outcome. In a 16-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in overweight men aged 40-70 with mild fatigue (57 enrolled, 50 completing the first 8-week period, 43 completing all 16 weeks) taking a standardized extract delivering 21 mg/day of withanolide glycosides, fatigue, vigor and sexual and psychological well-being improved over time but with NO statistically significant between-group differences; the only significant findings were hormonal (DHEA-S 18% greater increase, p = .005; testosterone 14.7% greater increase, p = .010). Supportive data come from adjacent outcomes and healthier populations: a randomized placebo-controlled trial in 120 adults aged 30-75 with self-reported cognitive and energy problems found 600 mg/day of root extract for 8 weeks produced greater improvement than placebo on COMPASS episodic memory, working memory and accuracy of attention (p < .05), with Mental Fatigue Scale, POMS-A vigor and BRIEF-A scores improving from baseline; a double-blind trial in 50 healthy athletic adults using 300 mg twice daily for 8 weeks reported greater VO2max improvement than placebo (P = 0.0074) and better RESTQ scores for fatigue recovery, lack of energy and fitness (all P < 0.0001); and a Bayesian meta-analysis of 13 physical-performance studies in healthy men and women found ashwagandha more efficacious than placebo across strength/power, cardiorespiratory-fitness and fatigue/recovery variables, which is performance and recovery rather than persistent clinical fatigue. In oncology the main citation is weaker than usually presented: the breast cancer study of 2 g root extract every 8 hours during chemotherapy was an OPEN-LABEL, NON-RANDOMIZED comparative trial in 100 patients that found lower Piper (P < .001) and Schwartz (P < .003) fatigue scores in the ashwagandha arm, but its design cannot exclude expectation and selection effects and the 24-month survival difference (72% vs 56%) was not significant (P = .176). Against this sits a well-documented harm signal: a case series from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network and a 2026 scoping review of 25 published patients describe a reproducible cholestatic or mixed liver injury, including one acute liver failure requiring transplantation and three deaths in patients with pre-existing cirrhosis, and a randomized trial in subclinical hypothyroidism shows the herb measurably moves TSH, T3 and T4. No adequate randomized evidence exists for ashwagandha in ME/CFS, post-viral fatigue, iron-deficiency fatigue or thyroid-related fatigue, and no meta-analysis has pooled trials in people with clinical fatigue.

Doses studied: Most commonly root extract 600 mg/day for 8 weeks, or 300 mg twice daily for 8 weeks in athletic adults. Other regimens tested include a concentrated preparation delivering 21 mg/day of withanolide glycosides for 8 weeks in the trial that was null for fatigue, a low-dose 30 mg/day bioactive-optimized extract for 8 weeks, 600 mg/day for 8 weeks in the subclinical hypothyroidism trial, and 2 g of root extract every 8 hours through chemotherapy in the non-randomized breast cancer study. Withanolide content differs greatly between products, so milligram doses are not directly comparable.

Why this grade: Graded early. Ashwagandha has many small human trials but almost none in adults with persistent clinical fatigue as the enrolling condition, and the trial that came closest — overweight, mildly fatigued men aged 40-70 — found NO significant between-group difference in fatigue or vigor. The supportive findings come from healthy or athletic volunteers, from mental-fatigue and cognition scales rather than persistent physical fatigue, or from an open-label non-randomized oncology study whose design cannot exclude expectation and selection effects. No meta-analysis exists in people with clinical fatigue; the only pooled analysis is a Bayesian meta-analysis of physical-performance trials in healthy men and women, which is a different question. Several trials are single-site and industry-linked. A documented, reproducible and sometimes severe hepatotoxicity signal, plus measurable effects on thyroid hormones, further argue for a cautious grade.

For insomnia

Ashwagandha is a traditional Ayurvedic herb that early studies suggest may help people fall asleep faster and feel their sleep is better, especially at higher doses taken for two months or more. The catch is that the studies so far are few, small, and mostly from the same research groups, so this is promising rather than proven. There are also rare but real reports of liver injury linked to ashwagandha supplements, so people with liver problems should be cautious.

Safety

Short-term tolerability in randomized trials has been good, with no serious side effects reported across the 5 trials in the 2021 meta-analysis; however, that meta-analysis explicitly states that data on serious adverse effects are limited and long-term safety is not established. The liver-injury case series (5 cases) showed a cholestatic or mixed pattern that was self-limited in the documented cases.

Who should be careful: People with existing liver disease should be cautious given documented case reports of supplement-associated liver injury, and anyone developing jaundice, dark urine, itching, or unusual fatigue while taking ashwagandha should stop and seek medical evaluation. It is generally advised to avoid ashwagandha in pregnancy.

Interactions: As a herb used for its calming properties, it may add to sedative medication effects; people taking thyroid, immunosuppressant, or sedative medicines should check with a clinician before use.

What the evidence says

Evidence for ashwagandha in sleep is preliminary but consistently positive in the small trials published to date. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (400 participants) found a small but significant effect on overall sleep (SMD -0.59, 95% CI -0.75 to -0.42, I2 = 62%), with larger effects in participants diagnosed with insomnia, at doses of 600 mg/day or more, and with treatment of 8 weeks or longer (Cheah 2021). In a double-blind RCT of 80 participants (40 healthy, 40 with insomnia), 8 weeks of root extract significantly improved sleep-onset latency (p = 0.013) and sleep efficiency (p < 0.0001), with greater improvement in the insomnia arm (Langade 2020). A 2025 double-blind RCT of 90 adults with mild-moderate stress found significant improvements in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores at days 28, 56, and 84 versus placebo (p < 0.05) using a 125 mg/day formulation (Mahadevan 2025). Against this, a case series from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network documented 5 cases of cholestatic or mixed liver injury attributed to ashwagandha-containing supplements, with jaundice and pruritus after 2-12 weeks of use and normalization of liver tests within 1-5 months (Bjornsson 2020). Trials to date report good tolerability, but the meta-analysis explicitly notes long-term safety data are insufficient.

Doses studied: 125-600 mg/day of root extract for 4-12 weeks; the meta-analysis found effects most prominent at >=600 mg/day for >=8 weeks.

Why this grade: A meta-analysis exists and is positive (SMD -0.59 for overall sleep), but it pools only 5 small RCTs totaling 400 participants, largely from a single country and with industry involvement common in this literature; the meta-analysis itself notes that serious-adverse-effect data are limited. With documented liver-injury case reports adding a safety caveat, the conservative call between moderate and early is early.
