Sleep restriction therapy

Strong evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Sleep restriction sounds backwards, because it means temporarily spending less time in bed rather than more, but it is one of the best-tested things a person can do for long-running insomnia, and it involves no medication. A large UK trial found that four short sessions with a practice nurse left people meaningfully less troubled by insomnia six months later. The honest catch is the first two or three weeks, when you will probably feel sleepier and slower during the day before things improve, which is why this is best done with a clinician guiding it rather than alone.

Safety

The best-documented harm is transient, real, and front-loaded. In a laboratory study of 16 patients, acute sleep restriction cut polysomnographic total sleep time by 69-91 minutes across the first three weeks, raised Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores at weeks 1-3, and significantly worsened psychomotor vigilance lapses and reaction times, with all measures returning to baseline by 3 months; a separate 147-participant randomised trial independently found a temporary increase in daytime interference at weeks 1-3. In the 642-participant HABIT trial, 8 participants in each arm experienced serious adverse events, none judged related to the intervention. Because the impairment is concentrated in the first weeks, sleep restriction should be started under clinician guidance and not immediately before a stretch of heavy driving, shift work, or other safety-critical activity. Prescribed time-in-bed windows in trials were standardised and titrated rather than cut to an arbitrary minimum, and the single-component meta-analysis notes that SRT procedures are inconsistently standardised across trials — another reason not to improvise this alone.

Who should be careful: The following are standard clinical cautions rather than findings from the trials retrieved here: bipolar disorder, in which sleep loss can precipitate mania; seizure disorders, in which sleep deprivation lowers seizure threshold; untreated obstructive sleep apnea; parasomnias; and occupations or life circumstances requiring sustained vigilance, such as commercial driving or operating machinery. Sleep restriction is not an appropriate unsupervised self-help protocol for these groups.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Alcohol and sedating medications compound the daytime sleepiness that sleep restriction transiently produces during titration. Anyone currently taking a prescription hypnotic should discuss timing and any tapering plan with the prescribing clinician before starting, rather than adjusting medication independently.

What the evidence says

Sleep restriction therapy (SRT) is the behavioural core of CBT-I and, unusually for a single component, has its own standalone trial literature. In the HABIT pragmatic RCT (n=642 adults with insomnia disorder recruited from 35 general practices in England), four brief nurse-delivered SRT sessions produced a mean Insomnia Severity Index of 10.9 (SD 5.5) versus 13.9 (SD 5.2) for sleep hygiene at 6 months, an adjusted mean difference of -3.05 (95% CI -3.83 to -2.28), p<0.0001, Cohen's d -0.74, with an incremental cost per QALY of £2,076 and outcomes also assessed at 3 and 12 months. An earlier general-practice RCT of simplified sleep restriction (n=97) reported 6-month PSQI 6.2 versus 8.4 (p<0.001), ISI 8.6 versus 11.1 (p=0.001), actigraphic sleep efficiency +2.2% (p=0.006), and insomnia remission in 67% versus 41% (NNT 4; adjusted OR 2.7, 95% CI 1.1-6.5). A component network meta-analysis of 241 trials and 31,452 participants identified sleep restriction as one of four critical components (remission incremental OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.04-2.13) while sleep hygiene education was not essential (iOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77-1.32), and the AASM 2021 guideline recommends multicomponent CBT-I STRONGLY but suggests SRT as a single-component therapy only CONDITIONALLY. The standalone base is thinner than those headline figures imply: a meta-analysis restricted to single-component SRT found only 7 RCTs (N=1102), 5 of them at high risk of bias, with the authors calling for more rigorous control arms. The documented cost is real and front-loaded: in a laboratory study of 16 patients, acute SRT reduced polysomnographic total sleep time by 69-91 minutes, raised Epworth scores at weeks 1-3 and increased psychomotor vigilance lapses at three of five assessment points, all returning to baseline by 3 months, and a separate 147-participant RCT found a temporary increase in daytime interference at weeks 1-3. Mechanistic claims should be held loosely: a randomised secondary analysis (n=56) found no clear evidence that SRT reduces REM sleep fragmentation on the primary index at week 1 (p=0.097) or week 4 (p=0.741).

Doses studied: HABIT trial: four brief nurse-delivered sleep restriction sessions plus a sleep hygiene booklet, with participants following a prescribed, standardised time-in-bed window derived from a sleep diary; outcomes were assessed at 3, 6 and 12 months. The retrieved abstract does not specify session format or total contact time, so no figure is given here. Falloon 2015 used a further-simplified sleep restriction protocol delivered alongside sleep hygiene advice in general practice. The single-component meta-analysis explicitly flags that SRT procedures are inconsistently standardised and reported across trials, so 'the dose' is not settled.

Why this grade: Multiple randomised trials of single-component sleep restriction therapy show consistent benefit, anchored by a 642-participant pragmatic RCT in NHS primary care with a large effect size (Cohen's d -0.74 at the 6-month primary endpoint), supported by a second general-practice RCT (n=97), a 241-trial component network meta-analysis identifying sleep restriction as one of four critical CBT-I components, and clinical practice guideline endorsement of the multicomponent therapy it anchors. Graded strong on that trial evidence, with three caveats stated plainly rather than buried: the AASM recommendation for sleep restriction AS A STANDALONE therapy is CONDITIONAL, not strong; the standalone literature is only 7 RCTs (N=1102), of which 5 were judged at high risk of bias; and transient daytime sleepiness with objectively measured vigilance impairment during the first weeks of titration is documented in two separate studies, not hypothetical.

Sources (8)
  1. Clinical and cost-effectiveness of nurse-delivered sleep restriction therapy for insomnia in primary care (HABIT): a pragmatic, superiority, open-label, randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialKyle SD, Siriwardena AN, Espie CA, et al., 2023 (Lancet) · PMID 37573859 · positive

    642 adults with insomnia disorder from 35 English general practices randomised 1:1 to four sessions of nurse-delivered sleep restriction therapy plus a sleep hygiene booklet versus the booklet alone; mean age 55.4 years, 76.2% female; 580 (90.3%) provided at least one outcome. At the 6-month primary endpoint mean ISI was 10.9 (SD 5.5) versus 13.9 (SD 5.2); adjusted mean difference -3.05 (95% CI -3.83 to -2.28), p<0.0001, Cohen's d -0.74. Incremental cost per QALY gained was £2,076, with a 95.3% probability of cost-effectiveness at a £20,000 threshold. Eight participants in each group had serious adverse events, none judged related to the intervention.

  2. The effect of single-component sleep restriction therapy on depressive symptoms: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisTse KYK, Maurer LF, Espie CA, Kyle SD, 2024 (J Sleep Res) · PMID 38419123 · mixed

    Searched to 19 August 2023 for trials delivering sleep restriction therapy to adults with insomnia; the entire standalone literature amounted to 7 randomised controlled trials (N=1102) plus 2 uncontrolled trials (N=22). SRT was associated with a medium effect on depressive symptoms post-treatment (N=6, g=-0.45, 95% CI -0.70 to -0.21, p<0.001) and a small effect at follow-up (N=4, g=-0.31, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.16, p<0.001). Critically, 5 of the 7 RCTs were judged to have a HIGH risk of bias, no trial was performed in a population with clinically defined depression, and the authors call for improved standardisation and reporting of SRT procedures and more rigorous control arms.

  3. Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline.
    GuidelineEdinger JD, Arnedt JT, Bertisch SM, et al., 2021 (J Clin Sleep Med) · PMID 33164742 · positive

    GRADE-based guideline. Recommendation 1: multicomponent cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia for chronic insomnia disorder in adults (STRONG). Recommendation 4: 'We suggest that clinicians use sleep restriction therapy as a single-component therapy for the treatment of chronic insomnia disorder in adults' (CONDITIONAL). Recommendation 6 advises clinicians NOT to use sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy (CONDITIONAL).

  4. Components and Delivery Formats of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia in Adults: A Systematic Review and Component Network Meta-Analysis.
    Network meta-analysisFurukawa Y, Sakata M, Yamamoto R, et al., 2024 (JAMA Psychiatry) · PMID 38231522 · positive

    241 randomised trials, 31,452 participants (mean age 45.4 years, 67% women). Sleep restriction was one of four critical components for post-treatment remission (incremental OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.04-2.13), alongside cognitive restructuring (1.68, 1.28-2.20), third-wave components (1.49, 1.10-2.03) and stimulus control (1.43, 1.00-2.05). Sleep hygiene education was not essential (iOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77-1.32) and relaxation procedures were found to be potentially counterproductive (iOR 0.81, 95% CI 0.64-1.02). Sleep restriction was associated with improved subjective sleep quality, sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset. Authors caution that potential undetected interactions could undermine the conclusions.

  5. Sleep restriction therapy for insomnia is associated with reduced objective total sleep time, increased daytime somnolence, and objectively impaired vigilance: implications for the clinical management of insomnia disorder.
    Clinical trialKyle SD, Miller CB, Rogers Z, et al., 2014 (Sleep) · PMID 24497651 · negative

    Within-subject, noncontrolled study of 16 patients with psychophysiological insomnia undergoing 4 weeks of single-component SRT. Polysomnographic total sleep time fell by 91 minutes on night 1, 78 minutes on night 8 and 69 minutes on night 22 versus baseline (p<0.001; effect sizes 1.60-1.80). Psychomotor vigilance lapses increased significantly at three of five assessment points and reaction times slowed at four of five (all p<0.05); Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores were elevated at weeks 1, 2 and 3. All measures returned to baseline by 3 months.

  6. Simplified sleep restriction for insomnia in general practice: a randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialFalloon K, Elley CR, Fernando A, Lee AC, Arroll B, 2015 (Br J Gen Pract) · PMID 26212846 · positive

    97 primary-care patients with persistent primary insomnia and no mental health or significant comorbidity randomised to simplified sleep restriction plus sleep hygiene versus sleep hygiene alone; 94 (97%) completed. At 6 months: PSQI 6.2 versus 8.4 (p<0.001), ISI 8.6 versus 11.1 (p=0.001), actigraphy-assessed sleep efficiency difference 2.2% (p=0.006), reduced fatigue (difference -2.3 units, p=0.04). Treatment response 67% (28/42) versus 41% (20/49); NNT 4 (95% CI 2.0 to 19.0); adjusted OR for remission 2.7 (95% CI 1.1 to 6.5). There were no significant differences in other outcomes or adverse effects.

  7. Understanding sleep restriction therapy: a network intervention analysis of symptom- and process-level change.
    Randomised trialLooman MI, Schoenmakers TM, Kamphuis JH, Blanken TF, Lancee J, 2026 (Sleep) · PMID 41263549 · mixed

    Secondary network analysis of a randomised controlled trial (N=147) in which adults with insomnia received telephone-guided SRT or a sleep diary control. SRT was associated with reduced difficulty initiating and maintaining sleep (weeks 1-6), reduced early morning awakenings (weeks 1-2) and reduced sleep dissatisfaction (weeks 2-4, 6), but ALSO with a temporary increase in daytime interference at weeks 1-3 — independent confirmation, in a second and larger sample, of the transient daytime cost seen in the laboratory study.

  8. The effect of sleep restriction therapy for insomnia on REM sleep fragmentation: A secondary analysis of a randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialMaurer LF, Sharman R, Espie CA, Kyle SD, 2023 (J Sleep Res) · PMID 37730206 · null

    56 participants (39 female, mean age 40.78) randomised to 4 weeks of sleep restriction therapy or time-in-bed regularisation, with ambulatory polysomnography at baseline, week 1 and week 4. No significant between-group difference on the primary REM sleep fragmentation index at week 1 (p=0.097, d=-0.31) or week 4 (p=0.741, d=-0.06), and no group effect on non-REM arousals. Secondary indices did decrease more with SRT at week 1 (index 2: p=0.023, d=-0.46; index 3: p=0.051, d=-0.39) but not at week 4 (d<=0.13). The authors conclude they did not find clear evidence that SRT modifies REM sleep fragmentation, leaving a proposed mechanism unconfirmed.
