Herbs

Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata)

Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Early evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Passionflower is a traditional calming herb, and a handful of small studies point in a hopeful direction, including one where people reported less anxiety before surgery. Other studies of the same kind found no difference from a dummy pill, and reviewers agree there simply are not enough good trials yet to say whether it works. It appears gentle and non-drowsy for most people over the short term.

Safety

Short-term use in trials was well tolerated, with no adverse effects observed across the nine trials in the 2020 systematic review and no sedation or psychomotor impairment at 500 mg in the presurgical study. One 2025 randomized trial found significantly higher diastolic blood pressure in the passionflower group than in the midazolam or placebo groups. Drowsiness and dizziness are possible at higher doses, and there are no long-term safety data.

Who should be careful: Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding due to lack of safety data and traditional cautions about uterine-stimulant constituents. Use caution before driving until individual response is known. Given the single trial signal on diastolic blood pressure, people with poorly controlled hypertension should discuss it with a clinician first.

Interactions: Additive sedation is plausible with benzodiazepines, sleep medications, other sedating herbs, and alcohol; caution when combined with any CNS depressant.

What the evidence says

Evidence for passionflower in anxiety rests on a few small randomized trials. A 4-week pilot double-blind RCT in 36 outpatients with GAD found Passiflora extract (45 drops/day) comparable to oxazepam 30 mg/day, with no significant between-group difference at trial end and significantly less job-performance impairment on passionflower, though oxazepam acted faster and the trial had no placebo arm. A double-blind placebo-controlled study in 60 ambulatory surgery patients found a single 500 mg oral dose 90 minutes preoperatively significantly lowered numerical anxiety scores versus placebo (p<0.001) without differences in sedation, psychomotor recovery, or discharge time. The Cochrane systematic review identified only two eligible trials (198 participants) and concluded that RCTs examining passionflower for anxiety are too few in number to permit any conclusions, while a 2020 systematic review of nine clinical trials lasting one day to 30 days reported that most found reduced anxiety, with the effect less evident in mild anxiety and no adverse effects observed. Against this, an independent 2022 Bayesian network meta-analysis found passionflower did not significantly reduce HAM-A scores in the summary network (MD -4.20, 95% CrI -8.82 to 0.16), and a 2025 randomized three-arm trial in 30 third-molar-extraction patients found no significant difference in anxiety scores between passionflower, midazolam and placebo (p>0.05), with higher diastolic blood pressure in the passionflower group, although salivary cortisol was significantly lower than placebo. No adequately powered placebo-controlled trial in generalized or chronic anxiety has been published.

Doses studied: 45 drops/day of liquid extract for 4 weeks (GAD trial); single 500 mg oral dose 30-90 minutes pre-procedure; commercial teas and tinctures vary widely and are not standardized to the trial preparations.

Why this grade: Small positive trials exist, including a 36-patient GAD trial against oxazepam and a 60-patient presurgical trial against placebo, and a 2020 systematic review of nine trials found most reported reduced anxiety. But the Cochrane review concluded the trials are too few to permit any conclusions, an independent 2022 network meta-analysis found no significant HAM-A reduction (MD -4.20, 95% CrI -8.82 to 0.16), a 2025 three-arm RCT found no difference from placebo on any anxiety score, no adequately powered placebo-controlled trial in generalized or chronic anxiety exists, and study durations run from a single dose to 30 days. This is the definition of early evidence.

Sources (6)
  1. Passionflower in the treatment of generalized anxiety: a pilot double-blind randomized controlled trial with oxazepam.
    Akhondzadeh S et al., 2001 · PMID 11679026 · positive

    In 36 outpatients with DSM-IV GAD treated 4 weeks (18 per arm), Passiflora extract 45 drops/day was comparable to oxazepam 30 mg/day with no significant difference at trial end; oxazepam showed a more rapid onset of action, while significantly more problems relating to impairment of job performance occurred on oxazepam. Pilot-sized with no placebo arm, so it cannot establish efficacy over placebo.

  2. Preoperative oral Passiflora incarnata reduces anxiety in ambulatory surgery patients: a double-blind, placebo-controlled study.
    Movafegh A et al., 2008 · PMID 18499602 · positive

    In 60 outpatients randomized to oral Passiflora 500 mg or placebo 90 minutes before surgery, numerical rating scale anxiety scores were significantly lower with passionflower (p<0.001), with no differences in sedation at any measured interval, psychomotor recovery, or discharge time.

  3. Passiflora for anxiety disorder.
    Miyasaka LS et al., 2007 · PMID 17253512 · null

    Cochrane review: only two RCTs (198 participants) were eligible. One indicated a lack of difference in efficacy between benzodiazepines and passiflora with similar dropout rates; suggested advantages (better job performance, less drowsiness than mexazolam) did not reach statistical significance. The reviewers concluded that trials are too few in number to permit any conclusions and that larger placebo-controlled trials are needed.

  4. Medicinal herbs for the treatment of anxiety: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Zhang W et al., 2022 · PMID 35378276 · null

    Independent Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 RCTs comparing 12 medicinal herbs: passionflower did not significantly reduce HAM-A scores in the summary network (MD -4.20, 95% CrI -8.82 to 0.16), with the credible interval crossing zero. The authors note it remains worthy of further study on the basis of separate networks, and that all results should be considered preliminary.

  5. Effects of Passiflora incarnata on salivary biomarkers and anxiety in patients undergoing third molar extraction surgery.
    Lima Neto TJ et al., 2025 · PMID 41381570 · null

    In 30 patients randomized to Passiflora incarnata, midazolam, or placebo 30 minutes before impacted third molar extraction, no significant differences were observed between groups on any anxiety scale or on most physiological measures (p>0.05); diastolic blood pressure was significantly higher in the Passiflora group (p<0.05). Salivary cortisol was significantly lower than placebo in both the Passiflora and midazolam groups (p<0.05).

  6. Passiflora incarnata in Neuropsychiatric Disorders - A Systematic Review.
    Janda K et al., 2020 · PMID 33352740 · mixed

    Systematic review of nine clinical trials in adults, durations ranging from one day to 30 days: the majority reported reduced anxiety after passionflower administration, with the effect less evident in people with mild anxiety symptoms. No adverse effects, including memory loss or collapse of psychometric function, were observed across the reviewed trials.

For insomnia

Passionflower is a traditional calming herb, usually drunk as tea, and the little research we have is mildly encouraging: in the best study, people with insomnia who took it for two weeks slept about 23 minutes longer on lab measurement than they did before, while the placebo group did not change. But the studies are small and short, and most of what they measured did not clearly improve. It appears very gentle and well tolerated, so it is a reasonable low-stakes option while the science catches up.

Safety

Tolerability in the small trial literature is good: the 30-day RCT reported no adverse effects, and the polysomnography and tea studies reported no notable safety signals. Drowsiness and dizziness are the plausible side effects given the herb's calming use; long-term safety has not been formally studied.

Who should be careful: Because of its traditional sedative use, passionflower is best avoided before driving or operating machinery, and there is insufficient safety data to recommend it in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Interactions: May add to the drowsiness of sedative medications, sleep aids, or alcohol; people on such medicines should consult a clinician before use.

What the evidence says

Evidence for passionflower in sleep is limited to a few small trials. In the largest, a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled study of 110 adults with DSM-5 insomnia disorder, 2 weeks of passionflower extract increased polysomnographic total sleep time versus placebo (change +23.05 +/- 54.26 vs -0.16 +/- 53.12 minutes, P = 0.049); sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset improved within the passionflower group but did not differ from placebo between groups (Lee 2020). A double-blind placebo-controlled crossover study in 41 healthy young adults found passionflower tea for 1 week improved subjective sleep-quality ratings versus placebo tea (t(40) = 2.70, p < 0.01), but this was the only significant result among six sleep-diary measures (Ngan & Conduit 2011). A 30-day randomized placebo-controlled trial in 65 participants with stress and insomnia reported significant reductions in Perceived Stress Scale scores and increased total sleep time versus placebo, with no adverse effects, though it was industry-affiliated (Harit 2024). As indirect contrary evidence on the herb's presumed calming mechanism, a network meta-analysis found passionflower did not significantly reduce Hamilton Anxiety scores in the summary network (MD -4.20, 95% CrI -8.82 to 0.16) (Zhang 2022). Larger and longer trials in insomnia are needed before firmer conclusions.

Doses studied: One cup of passionflower herbal tea nightly for 1 week (crossover study); proprietary standardized extracts taken at bedtime for 2-4 weeks in the RCTs (extract doses were not specified in the trial abstracts).

Why this grade: Human evidence consists of a handful of small short trials: one 2-week polysomnography RCT (n=110) with a barely significant benefit on one objective outcome (total sleep time, P = 0.049) and no between-group difference on sleep efficiency or WASO, one crossover tea study (n=41) positive on 1 of 6 diary measures, and one small industry-affiliated trial. This is exactly the small, few, preliminary trial base that defines early.

Sources (4)
  1. Effects of Passiflora incarnata Linnaeus on polysomnographic sleep parameters in subjects with insomnia disorder: a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled study.
    Lee J et al., 2020 (Int Clin Psychopharmacol) · PMID 31714321 · mixed

    In 110 adults with DSM-5 insomnia disorder treated for 2 weeks, polysomnographic total sleep time increased with passionflower versus placebo (+23.05 +/- 54.26 vs -0.16 +/- 53.12 minutes, P = 0.049); sleep efficiency and WASO improved within the passionflower group but showed no significant between-group difference.

  2. A double-blind, placebo-controlled investigation of the effects of Passiflora incarnata (passionflower) herbal tea on subjective sleep quality.
    Ngan A & Conduit R, 2011 (Phytother Res) · PMID 21294203 · positive

    In a crossover study of 41 healthy adults (18-35 years) drinking passionflower or placebo tea for a week each, sleep quality was rated significantly better with passionflower (t(40) = 2.70, p < 0.01), but this was the only significant finding among six sleep-diary measures analysed; authors describe short-term subjective benefit at low (tea) dose.

  3. Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled, Clinical Study of Passiflora incarnata in Participants With Stress and Sleep Problems.
    Harit MK et al., 2024 (Cureus) · PMID 38646244 · positive

    In 65 participants with stress and insomnia randomized to a standardized Passiflora extract or placebo at bedtime for 30 days, the extract group showed statistically significant reduction in Perceived Stress Scale scores and significantly increased total sleep time versus placebo, with no adverse effects reported; the study involved a manufacturer's research department.

  4. Medicinal herbs for the treatment of anxiety: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Zhang W et al., 2022 (Pharmacol Res) · PMID 35378276 · null

    In the summary network of this Bayesian network meta-analysis, passionflower did not significantly reduce Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores (MD -4.20, 95% CrI -8.82 to 0.16), a null result on the anxiolytic mechanism often invoked for its use in sleep, though the authors deemed it worthy of further study based on separate networks.
