Coenzyme Q10

Moderate evidence · for fatigue & low energy Early evidence · for high blood pressure Early evidence · for migraine & headache

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For fatigue & low energy

CoQ10 is a substance your cells use to make energy, and when many small studies are pooled it looks like it may take some edge off fatigue. The effect is modest, and the two most carefully run single trials - one in women going through chemotherapy, one in polio survivors where tiredness was the main thing being measured - both found no difference at all. It is well tolerated, so it is a reasonable thing to try once a cause has been looked for, but it is not a substitute for looking.

Safety

CoQ10 is well tolerated in trials: the 2022 fatigue meta-analysis recorded a single gastrointestinal adverse event across 602 participants taking it, and the post-polio trial reported it as safe and well tolerated. Reported effects are mild and mostly digestive - nausea, loose stools, upper abdominal discomfort - and some people find it disrupts sleep if taken late in the day. Long-term safety data in pregnancy and breastfeeding are lacking.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications have been established. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding because data are absent rather than reassuring. Anyone taking warfarin should tell the clinician managing their anticoagulation before starting.

Interactions: CoQ10 resembles vitamin K structurally and case reports describe a reduced warfarin effect; a randomised double-blind crossover in 24 patients on stable long-term warfarin found no change in INR or weekly warfarin dose (36.5 mg/week on CoQ10 versus 36.0 mg/week on placebo, PMID 12772396). The practical answer is to monitor INR rather than assume in either direction. Statins lower circulating CoQ10, which is the usual rationale for taking it, but that does not by itself establish that supplementing relieves statin-related symptoms. May add to the effect of blood-pressure-lowering drugs.

What the evidence says

A 2022 meta-analysis of 13 randomised trials (1,126 participants) found CoQ10 reduced fatigue scores versus placebo (Hedges' g -0.398, 95% CI -0.641 to -0.155, P=0.001), with consistent direction in both healthy and diseased participants; the effect was statistically significant for CoQ10-only formulations but not for combination products, and meta-regression showed larger effects with higher daily dose (coefficient -0.0017 per mg, P<0.001) and longer treatment (coefficient -0.0042 per day, P=0.007). The two best single trials point the other way. In the largest, 236 women with newly diagnosed breast cancer randomised to 300 mg/day CoQ10 with vitamin E or placebo for 24 weeks showed no difference on the Profile of Mood States-Fatigue scale (7.08 versus 8.24, P=0.257) or the FACIT-Fatigue scale (37.6 versus 37.6, P=0.965), despite plasma CoQ10 rising from 0.70 to 1.83 ug/mL. In the trial designed specifically around fatigue, 103 polio survivors with moderate-to-high fatigue took 100 mg/day CoQ10 or placebo for 60 days and the between-group difference was not statistically significant on either the Multidimensional Assessment of Fatigue (the primary outcome) or the Fatigue Severity Scale. A 2025 meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (474 participants) in depression found CoQ10 improved depressive symptoms (SMD -0.68, 95% CI -1.02 to -0.33) but no significant benefit for fatigue (SMD -0.33, 95% CI -1.38 to 0.72, P=0.54, I-squared 89%, only 2 trials). A 2025 systematic review of 24 trials in healthy adults concluded CoQ10 reliably raises blood levels but gives at most modest, context-dependent effects on exercise performance, with evidence for reduced subjective fatigue described as suggestive and becoming more consistent only after outliers were excluded, and overall certainty rated very low to low. In ME/CFS specifically, a 12-week trial randomised 207 patients to 200 mg CoQ10 plus 20 mg NADH or placebo and reported reduced cognitive fatigue perception (P<0.001) and total FIS-40 score (P=0.022) - but the product was a combination, so the CoQ10 contribution cannot be isolated, and one co-author was employed by the manufacturer's clinical research department.

Doses studied: Trials in the fatigue meta-analysis used CoQ10-only or combination products across a range of daily doses, with higher daily dose and longer duration associated with greater effect; the null breast-cancer trial used 300 mg/day with 300 IU vitamin E for 24 weeks; the null post-polio trial used 100 mg/day for 60 days; the ME/CFS trial used 200 mg/day CoQ10 with 20 mg/day NADH for 12 weeks.

Why this grade: A meta-analysis of 13 RCTs in over 1,100 people found a real but modest reduction in fatigue scores, and the body of trials is far too large to call this preliminary. It is held below 'strong' because the nulls are substantial and independent: the largest and best-conducted single trial found nothing, a dedicated 103-person trial with fatigue as its primary outcome found nothing, a 2025 meta-analysis found no significant fatigue effect when pooling the depression literature, and the most recent systematic review in healthy adults rated overall certainty very low to low. Positive and null results coexist across populations and formulations.

For high blood pressure

Coenzyme Q10 is often marketed for blood pressure, but the trials that actually tested it in people with high blood pressure are few and very small, and the most careful review of them found no meaningful effect. Some larger analyses in people with diabetes or high cholesterol report a drop of about 4 mmHg, though those results vary enormously from study to study. It appears well tolerated in the short studies that have been done, but nobody has shown it prevents a heart attack or stroke.

Safety

CoQ10 appears well tolerated at the doses studied. In the crossover trial using 200 mg/day for 12 weeks it was well tolerated with no clinically relevant changes in safety parameters, and the Cochrane hypertension review noted that in one of three trials reporting adverse effects CoQ10 was well tolerated. That said, safety reporting is genuinely sparse: the Cochrane primary-prevention review found that none of its six included trials provided adverse-event data, so the absence of reported harm reflects limited measurement as much as demonstrated safety. Long-term safety beyond a few months has not been characterised in hypertension trials.

Who should be careful: No established contraindications emerged from these trials. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established in this literature. Because the best-controlled hypertension evidence is null, CoQ10 should not replace treatment shown to lower blood pressure or prevent cardiovascular events.

Interactions: A reduced anticoagulant effect of warfarin has been reported with CoQ10, so anyone on warfarin should not start it without INR monitoring and medical advice. It is frequently taken alongside statins, and several trials studied it in statin-treated patients; any additive blood-pressure effect alongside antihypertensive medication should be monitored.

What the evidence says

Evidence for coenzyme Q10 in hypertension is small, inconsistent and confined to blood pressure as an endpoint. The Cochrane review of double-blind placebo-controlled trials lasting at least three weeks in primary hypertension could pool only two RCTs (50 participants) after excluding a third for unacceptably high risk of bias, and found no significant change in clinic systolic pressure (-3.68 mmHg, 95% CI -8.86 to 1.49) or diastolic pressure (-2.03 mmHg, 95% CI -4.86 to 0.81), concluding on moderate-quality evidence that CoQ10 does not have a clinically significant effect. A 12-week randomised double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial in 30 hypertensive patients with metabolic syndrome and inadequate blood pressure control found no significant reduction in 24-hour ambulatory systolic (p=0.60) or diastolic pressure (p=0.12), although daytime diastolic blood pressure loads were significantly lower on CoQ10; the authors concluded any effect is smaller than earlier meta-analyses suggested and that CoQ10 is not indicated as adjunctive antihypertensive treatment in this group. Larger positive syntheses exist outside primary hypertension: a GRADE-assessed dose-response meta-analysis of 26 studies (1831 subjects with cardiometabolic disorders) reported a 4.77 mmHg systolic reduction (95% CI -6.57 to -2.97) with a U-shaped curve favouring 100-200 mg/day, rated moderate quality for systolic and low for diastolic pressure; and 16 RCTs in type 2 diabetes reported reductions of 3.86 mmHg systolic and 2.70 mmHg diastolic, though with extreme heterogeneity (I-squared 83.7% and 92.1%) and a stated conclusion that contradicts the paper's own lipid result, which limits the weight it can carry. For hard outcomes there is nothing: the Cochrane primary-prevention review identified only 6 small RCTs (218 participants randomised, 20-52 each) and reported that no study assessed mortality or non-fatal cardiovascular events, and none provided adverse-event data.

Doses studied: Trials generally used 100-200 mg/day for at least three weeks and typically 8-12 weeks or longer; the dose-response analysis in cardiometabolic populations found the greatest systolic effect at approximately 100-200 mg/day with a U-shaped curve, and effects more pronounced in studies running beyond 12 weeks. The null ambulatory-blood-pressure crossover trial used 100 mg twice daily for 12 weeks.

Why this grade: The direct evidence in primary hypertension is very thin: the Cochrane review could pool only two acceptable double-blind RCTs totalling 50 participants, and found no significant change in systolic (-3.68 mmHg, 95% CI -8.86 to 1.49) or diastolic pressure (-2.03 mmHg, 95% CI -4.86 to 0.81), concluding on moderate-quality evidence that CoQ10 has no clinically significant effect while explicitly calling for more well-conducted trials. Larger positive meta-analyses exist but are in cardiometabolic populations (diabetes, dyslipidaemia) rather than primary hypertension, and carry very high heterogeneity (I-squared 84-92%). A well-conducted ambulatory-blood-pressure crossover trial in hypertensive patients with metabolic syndrome was null on both primary endpoints, though one prespecified secondary measure favoured CoQ10. No trial in this evidence base has tested CoQ10 against cardiovascular events, and the Cochrane primary-prevention review found no included study reported mortality, cardiovascular events or adverse events. Graded early because the hypertension-specific trial base is small and preliminary and the best-controlled results are null - not 'not_supported', because a two-trial fifty-person pool is too thin to settle the question either way.

For migraine & headache

CoQ10 taken daily may reduce how many migraines you get each month, but it does not appear to make the attacks you do get shorter or less painful, and the evidence is thinner than it looks. Every study is small, they do not all agree, and the review that stuck strictly to placebo-controlled trials found no clear effect at all. Headache guidelines rate it a step below magnesium and vitamin B2, and it costs considerably more than either.

Safety

CoQ10 was described as well tolerated in the pivotal 300 mg/day trial, no side effects were reported by participants in a 400 mg/day trial, and adverse events were not a limiting factor in the trials reviewed. Commonly reported complaints in practice are mild gastrointestinal upset, nausea, and difficulty sleeping if it is taken late in the day. It is fat-soluble and absorbed better with a meal containing fat. Safety beyond roughly three months at 300-400 mg/day has not been formally characterized in these trials, and it is markedly more expensive than magnesium or riboflavin — which matters, because the evidence behind it is weaker than for either.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications are established. It has not been adequately studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so it should not be started in those situations without clinician input. The one randomized pediatric trial found no benefit at endpoint, so use in children should be clinician-directed. People scheduled for surgery should tell their surgical team, as with any supplement affecting bleeding or blood pressure.

Interactions: CoQ10 is structurally related to vitamin K and has been reported to reduce the anticoagulant effect of warfarin — do not start it while on warfarin without INR monitoring. It may add to the effect of blood-pressure-lowering medications. Statins reduce the body's own CoQ10 production, which is often the rationale offered for supplementing, but that is a mechanism argument, not proof of migraine benefit.

What the evidence says

PREVENTIVE only — CoQ10 has not been tested as an acute treatment during an attack. Sándor et al. randomized 42 adults to CoQ10 300 mg/day (100 mg three times daily) or placebo and found superiority for attack frequency, headache days and days with nausea in the third treatment month, with a 50% responder rate of 47.6% versus 14.4% (number-needed-to-treat 3). A 2019 dose-response meta-analysis of 4 trials (221 participants) found frequency reduced by 1.87 attacks/month (95% CI -2.69 to -1.05, p < 0.001) but no significant effect on severity (p = 0.105) or duration (p = 0.086), and a 2021 meta-analysis of 6 studies (371 participants) similarly found reduced frequency (MD -1.52, 95% CI -2.40 to -0.65) and duration (MD -0.19, 95% CI -0.27 to -0.11) with no significant reduction in severity. Against this, a 2019 systematic review restricted to placebo-controlled trials could pool only 2 trials (97 adults) and found no significant effect on frequency (MD -0.44, 95% CI -2.14 to 1.26), duration (MD -1.97, 95% CI -4.82 to 0.87) or severity (RoM -0.05, 95% CI -0.20 to 0.11), rating the frequency and duration nulls moderate strength of evidence; a 130-adult trial of a CoQ10-magnesium-riboflavin combination did not significantly reduce migraine days versus placebo (p = 0.23); and a pediatric add-on crossover trial randomizing 120 children found both groups improved with no between-group difference at day 224, though CoQ10 showed greater improvement in the first 4 weeks. The supportive placebo-controlled adult trials remain very small — 42 and 45 participants — and in the 45-woman 400 mg/day trial every participant was also taking a prophylactic medication. The AAN/AHS guideline classifies CoQ10 as Level C, possibly effective, below magnesium and riboflavin.

Doses studied: 300 mg/day split as 100 mg three times daily, with superiority demonstrated in the third treatment month, or 400 mg/day as a single dose for 3 months as an add-on to standard prophylaxis. A pediatric add-on trial used 100 mg/day and found no difference from placebo at day 224. It is fat-soluble and absorbed better with a meal containing fat.

Why this grade: Downgraded from 'moderate' on adversarial review. Every placebo-controlled adult trial is small — 42 participants in the pivotal trial, 45 in the 400 mg/day trial, and even the largest meta-analysis pools only 371 people across 6 studies, several of which used CoQ10 as an add-on to prophylactic drugs or in combination with other supplements. The three meta-analyses disagree: two found reduced attack frequency (-1.87 and -1.52 attacks/month) while the one restricted to placebo-controlled vitamin and mineral trials could pool just 2 trials (97 adults) and found no significant effect on frequency, duration or severity, rating the frequency and duration nulls moderate strength of evidence. A combination trial in 130 adults missed on migraine days, and the pediatric trial was null at endpoint. The AAN/AHS guideline rates CoQ10 Level C — possibly effective — the lowest efficacy tier, below magnesium and riboflavin. Small, few and preliminary human trials is the definition of 'early'.
