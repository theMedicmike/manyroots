Herbs

Feverfew (Tanacetum parthenium)

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

Feverfew is a daisy-family plant people have taken for headaches for a very long time. When it was put through proper trials the results were mixed: the best-run study found it prevented about half an attack more per month than a dummy pill, two other careful studies found no difference at all, and the largest pooled analysis found fewer attacks but no real change in how bad they were or in the nausea and light sensitivity that come with them. It is taken every day to head attacks off rather than during one, and the standardised extract used in the good study is not the same thing as most feverfew capsules on a shop shelf.

Safety

Across the randomized trials adverse events were mild and transient — most often gastrointestinal complaints and mouth ulcers (Cochrane 2015, PMID 25892430). In the 170-patient MIG-99 trial, possibly-related adverse events occurred in 8.4% on feverfew versus 10.2% on placebo (p=0.654; PMID 16232154), and in the dose-finding trial 35% of all participants (52/147) reported at least one adverse event with no dose-related pattern in any safety parameter (PMID 12230594). Cochrane's overall read is that feverfew is not associated with any major safety concerns at the doses studied. That said, a 2025 review makes the opposite emphasis worth stating plainly: clear safety studies are lacking, and dried-leaf products differ widely in dose and composition, so a good safety record in a standardised CO2 extract does not transfer automatically to a supermarket capsule (PMID 39853578). Trials ran up to 16 weeks (9 months in the null crossover trial); longer-term safety has not been characterised.

Who should be careful: Feverfew is a member of the Asteraceae (daisy/composite) family, so anyone with known allergy to that family — ragweed, chrysanthemum, daisy — should avoid it. It has not been evaluated in pregnancy or breastfeeding; the randomized trials enrolled non-pregnant adults, so it should not be used in those settings. For children and adolescents there are no official guidelines on nutraceuticals including feverfew for migraine prevention and only a few studies with limited efficacy evidence, so it should not be given to a child without clinician involvement (PMID 28527085).

Interactions: Interactions have not been systematically studied in migraine populations. Because feverfew is commonly taken alongside prescription preventives and acute analgesics, anyone on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, or on prescription migraine preventives, should clear it with a clinician first. The honest position is that the safety literature is too thin to rule interactions either in or out (PMID 39853578).

What the evidence says

Feverfew has been tested almost exclusively as a daily preventive rather than as an acute (abortive) treatment; the Cochrane review's scope covers preventive dosing only. That 2015 review identified six double-blind placebo-controlled trials (561 participants), could not pool them because of heterogeneity in preparations, participants and designs, graded the resulting evidence low quality, and set three small positive trials (17-60 participants) against two rigorous null trials (n=50 and n=147). The largest and most rigorous trial (MIG-99 CO2 extract 6.25 mg three times daily; 218 randomised, 170 in the intention-to-treat analysis) reduced attacks from a baseline of 4.76 per month by 1.9 versus 1.3 on placebo (p=0.0456), a between-group difference of about 0.6 attacks per month, with a responder odds ratio of 3.4 (p=0.0049), while the earlier dose-finding trial of the same extract (n=147) failed on its primary endpoint in the intention-to-treat analysis and reached significance only in a predefined subgroup of 49 patients with at least four attacks per month at baseline. A 2025 meta-analysis pooling nine double-masked placebo-controlled RCTs (899 participants) found a significant reduction in attack frequency (-1.11, 95% CI -1.23 to -0.99) but only a non-significant trend on pain severity (-0.63, 95% CI -1.48 to 0.21, p=0.14) and no significant effect on nausea, vomiting, photophobia or phonophobia, with selection and attrition bias limiting quality. Independent systematic reviews in 2019 and 2020 both characterise the feverfew evidence as mixed and note high risk of bias across the trial set. For acute use, feverfew has been studied mainly in combination rather than alone: a sublingual feverfew-plus-ginger preparation left 32% pain-free at two hours versus 16% on placebo (p=0.02) in a 60-patient pilot (Cady 2011, PMID 21631494), which cannot be attributed to feverfew by itself.

Doses studied: Preventive only. MIG-99 CO2-extracted feverfew 6.25 mg three times daily (18.75 mg/day) for 16 weeks in the positive trial; the dose-finding trial compared 2.08 mg, 6.25 mg and 18.75 mg three times daily; the null crossover trial used one daily capsule of an alcoholic extract standardised to 0.5 mg parthenolide. Cochrane specifically flags that dried-leaf preparations vary in dose and characteristics and are not interchangeable with these standardised extracts.

Why this grade: Nine double-blind placebo-controlled RCTs exist (899 participants in the 2025 pooled analysis; six trials and 561 participants in the 2015 Cochrane review), and the 2012 AAN/AHS guideline rated one standardised CO2 extract (MIG-99) Level B, probably effective, for prevention. Against that: Cochrane could not pool the trials and graded the body of evidence low quality, two rigorous trials were flatly null, the best positive result is a between-group difference of roughly 0.6 attacks per month, and the 2025 meta-analysis that did find a significant reduction in attack frequency found only a non-significant trend on pain severity and no effect at all on nausea, vomiting, photophobia or phonophobia. Independent reviews in 2019 and 2020 both describe the feverfew evidence as mixed. Real randomized evidence, positive but modest and internally inconsistent, is 'moderate' — not 'strong', and well beyond 'early'. The guideline rating attaches to a specific standardised extract, not to feverfew products in general.

Sources (8)
  1. Feverfew for preventing migraine.
    Journal articleWider, Pittler & Ernst, 2015 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 25892430 · mixed

    Six double-blind placebo-controlled trials, 561 patients; results could not be pooled due to heterogeneity in participants, interventions and designs. The most recent rigorous trial (n=218 as counted by Cochrane; 170 in the trial's own intention-to-treat analysis) reduced migraine frequency by 1.9 attacks (4.8 to 2.9) versus 1.3 on placebo (4.8 to 3.5), a difference of 0.6 attacks per month, with no statistically significant differences on intensity, duration, nausea and vomiting, or global assessment. Three small positive trials (17-60 participants) are set against two rigorous trials (n=50, n=147) that found no significant difference. Authors graded this low quality evidence needing confirmation before firm conclusions.

  2. Systematic review and meta-analysis of Tanacetum parthenium: evaluating its efficacy in migraine relief.
    Narrative reviewNelaturi, Viswanatha, Shenoy, Khanna & Krishnadas, 2025 (Nat Prod Res) · PMID 41422534 · mixed

    Nine double-masked placebo-controlled RCTs, 899 participants, searched to August 2025. Feverfew significantly reduced migraine attack frequency (IV -1.11, 95% CI -1.23 to -0.99, p<0.00001, I-squared=28%) and migraine duration (-4.43, 95% CI -7.63 to -1.23, p=0.007, I-squared=98%), but showed only a non-significant trend on pain severity (-0.63, 95% CI -1.48 to 0.21, p=0.14, I-squared=85%) and NO significant effect on migraine-associated symptoms including nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia. Authors note moderate heterogeneity plus selection and attrition bias limited overall quality, and conclude feverfew may offer modest benefits warranting further high-quality large-scale RCTs. The very high heterogeneity on the duration endpoint (I-squared=98%) makes that pooled estimate unreliable.

  3. Efficacy and safety of 6.25 mg t.i.d. feverfew CO2-extract (MIG-99) in migraine prevention--a randomized, double-blind, multicentre, placebo-controlled study.
    Randomised trialDiener, Pfaffenrath, Schnitker, Friede & Henneicke-von Zepelin, 2005 (Cephalalgia) · PMID 16232154 · positive

    170 patients in the intention-to-treat analysis (89 MIG-99, 81 placebo) treated 16 weeks after a 4-week baseline. Migraine frequency fell from 4.76 per month by 1.9 attacks on MIG-99 versus 1.3 on placebo (p=0.0456); responder odds ratio 3.4 in favour of MIG-99 (p=0.0049). Possibly drug-related adverse events: 8.4% (9/107) MIG-99 versus 10.2% (11/108) placebo (p=0.654).

  4. The efficacy and safety of Tanacetum parthenium (feverfew) in migraine prophylaxis--a double-blind, multicentre, randomized placebo-controlled dose-response study.
    Randomised trialPfaffenrath, Diener, Fischer, Friede & Henneicke-von Zepelin, 2002 (Cephalalgia) · PMID 12230594 · null

    147 patients randomised to MIG-99 2.08, 6.25 or 18.75 mg three times daily versus placebo for 12 weeks. No statistically significant difference on the primary or secondary efficacy parameters in the intention-to-treat sample, and no dose-response relationship observable in that analysis; authors state MIG-99 failed to show a significant migraine prophylactic effect in general. Benefit appeared only in a predefined subgroup of 49 patients with at least four attacks in the baseline month (6.25 mg t.i.d.: -1.8 +/- 1.5 attacks per 28 days versus -0.3 +/- 1.9 on placebo, p=0.02). Adverse events occurred in 35% (52/147) with no dose-related pattern in any safety parameter.

  5. Herbal medicines in migraine prevention Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled crossover trial of a feverfew preparation.
    Journal articleDe Weerdt, Bootsma & Hendriks, 1996 (Phytomedicine) · PMID 23195074 · null

    50 feverfew-naive patients randomised in a 9-month crossover trial of one daily capsule of dried alcoholic feverfew extract containing 0.5 mg parthenolide; 44 completed. Both treatment groups suffered the same number of migraine attacks and no prophylactic effect could be demonstrated. Authors explicitly noted the difference from two earlier positive trials may be explained by those trials having enrolled patients who already reported positive experiences with feverfew.

  6. Evidence-based guideline update: NSAIDs and other complementary treatments for episodic migraine prevention in adults: report of the Quality Standards Subcommittee of the American Academy of Neurology and the American Headache Society.
    GuidelineHolland, Silberstein, Freitag, Dodick, Argoff & Ashman, 2012 (Neurology) · PMID 22529203 · positive

    Structured review of 284 abstracts yielding 49 Class I or Class II articles. MIG-99 (feverfew) was rated Level B, probably effective, for episodic migraine prevention — a tier below the Level A given to Petasites in the same guideline. The rating is for the standardised MIG-99 extract specifically, not feverfew preparations generally, and reflects studies published through May 2009.

  7. Herbal treatments for migraine: A systematic review of randomised-controlled studies.
    Systematic reviewLopresti, Smith & Drummond, 2020 (Phytother Res) · PMID 32310327 · mixed

    Systematic review of 19 randomised controlled trials of single-ingredient herbal treatments for acute or prophylactic migraine treatment. Concluded that overall findings on the efficacy of feverfew were mixed, in contrast to positive albeit limited evidence for butterbur. Authors note the risk of bias was high for many of the included studies and that further high-quality research is essential to examine efficacy and safety.

  8. Nutraceuticals and Headache 2024: Riboflavin, Coenzyme Q10, Feverfew, Magnesium, Melatonin, and Butterbur.
    Narrative reviewTepper & Tepper, 2025 (Curr Pain Headache Rep) · PMID 39853578 · mixed

    Review concludes feverfew may be effective for migraine prevention, but explicitly counsels caution in recommending it because of the absence of clear safety studies, the differences in dose and characteristics across dried-leaf preparations, and the myriad of feverfew cellular effects. Notes nutraceuticals are not FDA-regulated, so product-level variability is a live clinical problem.
