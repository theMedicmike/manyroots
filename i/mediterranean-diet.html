Mediterranean dietary pattern

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

A Mediterranean way of eating - olive oil, vegetables, beans, nuts, fish and whole grains - is one of the few diets tested in long-term trials that counted real events, and heart patients following it had fewer heart attacks and strokes. Its two other headline results are not events: fewer high-risk people 'developed diabetes', and newly diagnosed people 'stayed off medication for longer' - each decided by a blood-test threshold, the second by study doctors who knew which diet a person was on, so read that one carefully. In that trial a doctor decided when each person started medication, against a set HbA1c threshold (above 7%), with checks along the way. It is not evidence that you can put off starting a drug your own clinician has recommended. Deferring treatment on your own is the misreading this finding invites, and it is the one thing this result does not support. The evidence is not airtight either - the biggest trial had to be re-analysed after the researchers found problems with how people were assigned to groups, a Cochrane review found no clear effect on dying from heart disease or from any cause, and the effect on the HbA1c test itself is modest, roughly a third of a percentage point, so this will not replace medication for most people with established diabetes - though it is a safe pattern that people find easy to keep up.

Safety

This is an ordinary eating pattern and has been used in multi-year trials of thousands of people without an excess of adverse events; in PREDIMED withdrawals were greater in the control group (PMID 24573661), CORDIOPREV ran 7 years (PMID 35525255), and the Cochrane review found adverse events absent or minor in the two trials reporting them (PMID 30864165). The pattern is relatively energy-dense from oil and nuts, and ad libitum versions produced little weight loss (0.3-2 kg, PMID 34796367); an energy-reduced version with physical activity prevented more diabetes (PMID 40854218), so portion awareness matters for people trying to lose weight. Important: the trials in which people 'stayed off' or 'delayed' glucose-lowering drugs did so under a study protocol in which physicians decided when to start treatment against a set HbA1c threshold (PMIDs 19721018, 24722497). That is not a reason to defer, stop or reduce prescribed medication yourself - and remember that the Cochrane review found no clear effect of this pattern on cardiovascular or total mortality.

Who should be careful: Nut allergy rules out the nut component (use the olive-oil version). The traditional pattern includes moderate wine with meals; people who do not drink should not start, and anyone with liver disease, pancreatitis, alcohol-use disorder or on medicines that interact with alcohol should omit it (general clinical guidance). If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, alcohol is a specific hazard here and not just a calorie question. Both of those drugs raise insulin whatever your glucose is doing, and alcohol is a recognised contributing cause of hypoglycaemia on them (PMID 23637538); alcohol is also named among the main external triggers of overnight lows in people on insulin, and overnight lows are routinely missed without continuous monitoring (PMID 39158988). The low can arrive long after the glass, and not only while you sleep: in a controlled crossover study, six men with type 1 diabetes who drank dry white wine (0.75 g/kg alcohol) at 9 pm had unchanged evening and overnight glucose but significantly lower fasting and post-breakfast glucose the next morning, and five of the six needed treatment for hypoglycaemia from 10 a.m. (nadir 1.9-2.9 mmol/L); none did after water (PMID 11679452). If you keep the wine, take it with food and never on an empty stomach, check your glucose before bed, keep checking into the next day, and tell your prescriber that you drink so your doses can account for it. Fish-heavy versions warrant mercury-aware choices in pregnancy (general guidance).

Interactions: No drug interactions specific to the pattern are documented in the cited trials. Because HbA1c and the need for glucose-lowering drugs fall (PMIDs 19721018, 24722497, 41470853), people on insulin or sulfonylureas should monitor for hypoglycaemia as the diet takes effect and have doses reviewed by their prescriber. If the pattern's wine component is kept, see contraindications - alcohol plus insulin or a sulfonylurea is its own hypoglycaemia hazard (PMIDs 11679452, 23637538, 39158988). High vitamin K intake from leafy greens can affect warfarin control if intake changes abruptly; keep intake consistent (general guidance).

What the evidence says

Unusually for a diet, some of this evidence is on real events rather than surrogates - but the surrogate and outcome layers must be read separately. Prevention of new-onset diabetes (a threshold-defined diagnosis, not a clinical event): in PREDIMED, 3,541 adults aged 55-80 at high cardiovascular risk without diabetes were randomised to a Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil, with nuts, or low-fat advice, with no weight-loss or exercise component; over a median 4.1 years diabetes incidence was 16.0, 18.7 and 23.6 cases per 1,000 person-years, multivariate HR 0.60 (95% CI 0.43-0.85) for olive oil and a non-significant 0.82 (0.61-1.10) for nuts, with the acknowledged limitations that randomisation was not stratified by diabetes status and withdrawals were greater in the control group (PMID 24573661); the single-centre PREDIMED-Reus report (n=418) found 4-year incidence 10.1% and 11.0% versus 17.9%, a pooled 52% reduction (PMID 20929998), and a published erratum was later issued for it (PMID 30104300). The trial-wide caveat is documented by the investigators themselves: PREDIMED's main cardiovascular report was withdrawn and republished after protocol deviations were identified, including enrolment of household members without randomisation, assignment to a study group without randomisation at 1 of 11 sites and apparent inconsistent use of randomisation tables at another; the revised intention-to-treat estimates were HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.53-0.91) for olive oil and 0.72 (0.54-0.95) for nuts, and were similar after omitting 1,588 participants whose assignment was known or suspected to have departed from protocol (PMID 29897866). Adding energy restriction and physical activity (PREDIMED-Plus, n=4,746 with metabolic syndrome; a prespecified SECONDARY outcome of a single-blinded trial in which the comparator arm also received Mediterranean-diet advice) lowered 6-year diabetes incidence to 9.5% versus 12.0%, a 31% relative reduction (95% CI 18-41%) (PMID 40854218). Established type 2 diabetes: in 215 overweight, newly diagnosed, drug-naive patients, a lower-carbohydrate Mediterranean diet versus a low-fat diet reduced the 4-year need for glucose-lowering drugs from 70% to 44% (absolute difference -26.0 percentage points; HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.51-0.86), in a single centre with unblinded drug-initiating investigators (PMID 19721018); in extended follow-up median time to needing a drug was 4.8 versus 2.8 years (HR 0.68, 0.50-0.89) with any remission in 14.7% versus 4.1% at year 1 and 5.0% versus 0% at year 6 (PMID 24722497). Surrogate meta-analyses of RCTs in type 2 diabetes give HbA1c -0.30 percentage points (95% CI -0.46 to -0.14; 9 RCTs, n=1,178) with fasting glucose -0.72 mmol/L (-1.24 to -0.21) (PMID 25369829) and HbA1c -0.307% (-0.451 to -0.163) with fasting glucose -0.845 mmol/L (-1.307 to -0.384) across 11 RCTs (PMID 41470853); a network meta-analysis of 10 RCTs versus low-fat diet gave HbA1c -0.45% (PMID 30070019). Cardiovascular and kidney outcomes: in 1,002 coronary patients followed 7 years, major cardiovascular events occurred in 87 versus 111 (28.1 vs 37.7 per 1,000 person-years; log-rank p=0.039; adjusted HR 0.719-0.753), with the benefit evident in men (HR 0.669) and no difference found among the 175 women (PMID 35525255), and eGFR declined less on the Mediterranean diet in the type 2 diabetes subgroup (p=0.040) (PMID 35030530). Null and cautionary evidence: the Cochrane review of 30 RCTs (12,461 participants) found low-quality evidence of little or no effect of the PREDIMED intervention on cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.50-1.32) or total mortality (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.81-1.24) over 4.8 years, moderate-quality evidence of fewer strokes (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.45-0.80, 24 to 14 per 1,000), only small blood-pressure and cholesterol effects, and concluded that uncertainty remains regarding clinical endpoints for both primary and secondary prevention (PMID 30864165). In 225 adults with prediabetes a 6-month Mediterranean diet lowered HbA1c by only 0.08 percentage points and time above 140 mg/dL by 0.3 h/day, significantly less than a personalised diet (PMID 34301736). Weight-loss meta-analyses find Mediterranean diets achieve only 0.3-2 kg more than control with 1-year remission around 15% on low-certainty evidence (PMID 34796367), and lifestyle interventions producing under 5% weight loss show non-significant HbA1c effects (PMID 25935570).

Doses studied: PREDIMED: ad libitum Mediterranean diet plus 1 litre/week of extra-virgin olive oil for the household or 30 g/day mixed nuts, with no calorie restriction and no exercise component (PMIDs 20929998, 24573661, 29897866). Esposito trials: Mediterranean-style diet with under 50% of energy from carbohydrate, rich in vegetables, whole grains and olive oil as the main fat, versus a low-fat diet with under 30% of energy from fat (PMIDs 19721018, 24722497). CORDIOPREV: 35% fat with 22% monounsaturated fat and under 50% carbohydrate versus 28% fat with 12% monounsaturated fat and over 55% carbohydrate (PMIDs 35525255, 35030530). PREDIMED-Plus added a planned 600 kcal/day energy reduction, increased physical activity and behavioural weight-loss support (PMID 40854218).

Why this grade: This pattern has an unusually good evidence base for a diet, but only one of its three pillars is a clinical outcome. Fewer cardiovascular events in coronary patients (CORDIOPREV, PMID 35525255) is an event count. The other two are glucose-threshold endpoints: new-onset diabetes in high-cardiovascular-risk adults (PREDIMED, HR 0.60 for the olive-oil arm, PMID 24573661) is a diagnosis defined by blood values, and the delay in starting glucose-lowering drugs in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes (44% vs 70% at 4 years, PMID 19721018) is a medication-initiation decision made against a set HbA1c threshold - protocol-defined as a follow-up HbA1c above 7% - by investigators the trial reports were not blinded, not something the patient experienced. Consistent meta-analyses of RCTs sit below all three, on HbA1c. It stops short of strong for five reasons. The glycaemic effect is modest (HbA1c about -0.30 percentage points; PMIDs 25369829, 41470853). The PREDIMED nut arm was non-significant, and the PREDIMED investigators themselves withdrew the original report after identifying protocol deviations including enrolment of household members without randomisation and inconsistent use of randomisation tables (Estruch 2018, PMID 29897866). The Cochrane review of Mediterranean-style diets found low-quality evidence of little or no effect on cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.50-1.32) or total mortality (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.81-1.24), and concluded that uncertainty remains for both primary and secondary prevention (Rees 2019, PMID 30864165). The drug-delay trial is single-centre (n=215) with unblinded drug-initiating investigators. And in prediabetes a 6-month Mediterranean diet lowered HbA1c by only 0.08 percentage points (PMID 34301736). When torn between moderate and strong, the lower grade was taken.

  1. Prevention of diabetes with Mediterranean diets: a subgroup analysis of a randomized trial.
    Randomised trialSalas-Salvado J et al., 2014 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 24573661 · positive

    3,541 high-cardiovascular-risk adults without diabetes, median 4.1 years: incidence 16.0 (olive oil), 18.7 (nuts) and 23.6 (control) per 1,000 person-years; HR 0.60 (95% CI 0.43-0.85) for Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil and a non-significant 0.82 (0.61-1.10) for nuts; no weight-loss or exercise intervention. Prespecified secondary outcome; randomisation was not stratified by diabetes status and withdrawals were greater in the control group. New-onset diabetes here is a diagnosis defined by blood values, not a clinical event.

  2. Primary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease with a Mediterranean Diet Supplemented with Extra-Virgin Olive Oil or Nuts.
    Journal articleEstruch R et al., 2018 (N Engl J Med, corrected and republished) · PMID 29897866 · mixed

    The PREDIMED investigators withdrew their 2013 report after identifying protocol deviations - enrolment of household members without randomisation, assignment to a study group without randomisation at 1 of 11 sites, and apparently inconsistent use of randomisation tables at another. In the republished analysis of 7,447 participants (288 primary events) the hazard ratios were 0.69 (95% CI 0.53-0.91) for olive oil and 0.72 (0.54-0.95) for nuts, similar after omitting 1,588 participants whose assignment was known or suspected to have departed from protocol.

  3. Mediterranean-style diet for the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease.
    Meta-analysisRees K et al., 2019 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 30864165 · mixed

    30 RCTs, 12,461 participants: LOW-quality evidence of little or no effect of the PREDIMED intervention on cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.50-1.32) or total mortality (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.81-1.24) over 4.8 years; moderate-quality evidence of fewer strokes (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.45-0.80; 24 to 14 per 1,000). Primary-prevention trials versus no/minimal intervention reported no clinical endpoints at all. Conclusion: uncertainty remains about effects on clinical endpoints and risk factors for both primary and secondary prevention.

  4. Effects of a Mediterranean-style diet on the need for antihyperglycemic drug therapy in patients with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes: a randomized trial.
    Randomised trialEsposito K et al., 2009 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 19721018 · positive

    215 overweight, newly diagnosed, drug-naive adults with type 2 diabetes: after 4 years 44% on the Mediterranean-style diet vs 70% on the low-fat diet required glucose-lowering drugs (absolute difference -26.0 percentage points, 95% CI -31.1 to -20.1; HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.51-0.86). The endpoint is a medication-initiation decision, defined by protocol as indicated for a follow-up HbA1c greater than 7%, not a clinical event. Single centre; investigators responsible for initiating drug therapy were not blinded; dietary intake self-reported.

  5. The effects of a Mediterranean diet on the need for diabetes drugs and remission of newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes: follow-up of a randomized trial.
    Randomised trialEsposito K et al., 2014 (Diabetes Care) · PMID 24722497 · positive

    Extended follow-up of the same 215-patient trial: median time to needing a diabetes drug 4.8 vs 2.8 years (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.50-0.89); any remission (partial or complete) 14.7% vs 4.1% at year 1 and 5.0% vs 0% at year 6. Same trial and same threshold-defined endpoint as PMID 19721018, not an independent replication.

  6. Impact of the Mediterranean Diet on Glycemic Control, Body Mass Index, Lipid Profile, and Blood Pressure in Type 2 Diabetes: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Meta-analysisWu MJ et al., 2025 (Nutrients) · PMID 41470853 · positive

    11 RCTs in established type 2 diabetes: HbA1c -0.307% (95% CI -0.451 to -0.163), fasting plasma glucose -0.845 mmol/L (-1.307 to -0.384), BMI -0.828 kg/m2 (-1.4 to -0.256), LDL-C -8.06 mg/dL (-14.213 to -1.907), diastolic BP -2.008 mmHg; systolic BP -5.130 mmHg (95% CI -10.877 to 0.617) was NOT significant. Surrogate endpoints only.

  7. Long-term secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease with a Mediterranean diet and a low-fat diet (CORDIOPREV): a randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialDelgado-Lista J et al., 2022 (Lancet) · PMID 35525255 · positive

    1,002 coronary heart disease patients, 7 years: major cardiovascular events 87 vs 111 (28.1 vs 37.7 per 1,000 person-years; log-rank p=0.039), multivariable-adjusted HR 0.719-0.753. The effect was evident in men (67/414 vs 94/413; HR 0.669, 95% CI 0.489-0.915) with no difference found among the 175 women. Single centre. This is the entry's only clinical-event outcome.

  8. Personalized Postprandial Glucose Response-Targeting Diet Versus Mediterranean Diet for Glycemic Control in Prediabetes.
    Randomised trialBen-Yacov O et al., 2021 (Diabetes Care) · PMID 34301736 · mixed

    225 adults with prediabetes (baseline HbA1c 5.9%): the 6-month Mediterranean diet changed HbA1c by only -0.08% and time above 140 mg/dL by -0.3 h/day, significantly less than a personalised postprandial-targeting diet (-0.16%, -1.3 h/day); between-group differences persisted at 12 months.

  9. The effect of evening alcohol consumption on next-morning glucose control in type 1 diabetes.
    Journal articleTurner BC et al., 2001 (Diabetes Care) · PMID 11679452 · negative

    Controlled crossover study, six men with type 1 diabetes: dry white wine (0.75 g/kg alcohol) drunk at 9 pm produced NO significant difference in evening or overnight glucose, but significantly lower fasting and post-breakfast glucose the next morning (postprandial peak 8.9 vs 15 mmol/L, P<0.01), and from 10:00 a.m. five of the six required treatment for hypoglycaemia (nadir 1.9-2.9 mmol/L); none did after water. Associated with reduced nocturnal growth hormone secretion. Directly relevant to the wine component of this pattern: the risk window extends beyond the night into the next day. Small (n=6), type 1 diabetes on insulin.

  10. Avoiding hypoglycemia: a key to success for glucose-lowering therapy in type 2 diabetes.
    Narrative reviewAhren B, 2013 (Vasc Health Risk Manag) · PMID 23637538 · negative

    Narrative review: glucose-lowering medications that raise circulating insulin independently of glucose - insulin and sulfonylurea therapy - are the most common cause of hypoglycaemia in type 2 diabetes, with alcohol consumption named among the other contributing factors alongside delayed or missed meals and exercise. Supports naming alcohol as a specific hazard for readers on these two drug classes. Review, not a trial of alcohol.

  11. Nocturnal Hypoglycemia in the Era of Continuous Glucose Monitoring.
    Narrative reviewKulzer B et al., 2024 (J Diabetes Sci Technol) · PMID 39158988 · negative

    Review of nocturnal hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes on insulin therapy: names external factors including exercise and alcohol among the main causes, and notes that continuous glucose monitoring showed nocturnal hypoglycaemic events were significantly underestimated by traditional blood glucose monitoring - i.e. these lows are routinely missed. Review, not a trial of alcohol.
