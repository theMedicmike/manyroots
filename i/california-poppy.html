Herbs

California poppy (Eschscholzia californica)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

California poppy has been used as a calming, sleep-promoting plant for well over a century, and lab studies in mice do show it makes them sleepy. But nobody has ever run a proper placebo-controlled sleep study on California poppy by itself in people. The human studies that exist all mixed it with other sedating ingredients and had no placebo group, so we honestly cannot say how much of anything came from the poppy.

Safety

Short-term tolerability looks acceptable in the trials retrieved. In the 264-patient placebo-controlled anxiety RCT, adverse events occurred in 15/130 (11.5%) on the poppy-containing product vs 13/134 (9.7%) on placebo, mainly mild-to-moderate digestive or psychopathological complaints; the eschscholtzia-valerian combination was reported as well tolerated over 4 weeks. Dedicated human toxicology is still thin, but the safety literature is not empty: an in vitro placental study designed to address the herb's use in pregnancy found California poppy extract did not alter placental protein expression, found its marker alkaloid protopine inconspicuous across cytotoxicity, apoptosis and metabolic assays, and found no genotoxicity for any tested phytochemical at 0.1 or 1 uM. That is cell-culture reassurance, not clinical pregnancy safety data. A practical caution is product variability: alkaloid content differs about 20-fold across commercial preparations, so dose is not comparable between brands. Despite the common name, it is not the opium poppy and its isoquinoline alkaloids are not morphine-type.

Who should be careful: No trial-based contraindications have been established. Given the benzodiazepine-receptor mechanism shown in animals and the absence of any clinical pregnancy, lactation or paediatric data (the available pregnancy evidence is in vitro only, and these herbal medicines are not officially approved for use in pregnancy), avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding, in children, and alongside prescribed sedatives or alcohol without clinician oversight. The controlled human data are in anxiety rather than insomnia, so no safe long-term sleep dose has been characterised.

Interactions: Not formally studied in humans. Mechanistically plausible additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, opioids, sedating antihistamines and other CNS depressants, because flumazenil (a benzodiazepine-receptor antagonist) abolished the extract's sedative and anxiolytic effects in mice, implying action at the benzodiazepine receptor. Two of its main alkaloids, escholtzine and protopine, were highly permeable in an intestinal cell model and were not degraded by gut microbiota, so systemic exposure after oral use is plausible.

What the evidence says

Human evidence for California poppy in insomnia is limited to combination products in uncontrolled designs, and a 2013 systematic review of 53 herbal anxiolytics classed Eschscholzia californica among 32 plants with preclinical evidence only and no human clinical trials. In a 4-week prospective observational study of adults with adjustment insomnia (Abdellah 2019), a fixed eschscholtzia-plus-valerian combination was associated with an Insomnia Severity Index fall of roughly 30% (16.09 +/- 3.67 to 11.32 +/- 4.78, p<0.0001) and sleep efficiency rising from 78.4% +/- 12.5 to 84.6% +/- 10.2 (p=0.002), with no improvement in sleep latency and no placebo arm. A 40-participant single-arm open-label study of melatonin, vitamin B6, California poppy, passionflower and lemon balm reported sleep quality rising 1.9 points (5.4 to 7.3, p<0.001), a result confounded by melatonin and by the absence of any control. The one double-blind placebo-controlled RCT containing the herb (n=264) tested a hawthorn-Eschscholtzia-magnesium product in mild-to-moderate anxiety, not insomnia, and found a modest Hamilton total-score advantage (-10.6 vs -8.9, p=0.005) with the somatic subscore not significant (-6.5 vs -5.7, p=0.054). Preclinical work in mice shows sleep induction and reduced locomotion at doses above 100 mg/kg and anxiolytic effects at 25 mg/kg, with flumazenil abolishing both sedative and anxiolytic effects, implying benzodiazepine-receptor affinity. A 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 RCTs in 1602 insomnia patients found RCT evidence for only four herbal monopreparations (valerian, chamomile, kava, wuling), and California poppy was not among them. Product content also varies widely: across eight commercial California poppy preparations, californidine ranged from 0.13 to 2.55 mg/g, escholtzine from 0.05 to 0.63 mg/g and protopine from 0.008 to 0.200 mg/g.

Doses studied: No monopreparation dose has been established for insomnia in humans. Human data come only from fixed combinations: up to four tablets nightly of a standardised eschscholtzia-plus-valerian product for 4 weeks, and two tablets twice daily of a hawthorn-Eschscholtzia-magnesium product for 3 months (anxiety indication). Commercial preparations are not interchangeable: alkaloid content varies roughly 20-fold between products (californidine 0.13-2.55 mg/g). Mouse sedative and sleep-inducing effects occurred above 100 mg/kg.

Why this grade: No placebo-controlled trial has tested California poppy on its own for insomnia. The only human sleep data come from uncontrolled, open-label studies of multi-ingredient products (poppy plus valerian; poppy plus melatonin, B6, passionflower and lemon balm), which cannot attribute any effect to the poppy. The single placebo-controlled RCT containing the herb studied a hawthorn-poppy-magnesium combination for anxiety, not insomnia, and its somatic anxiety endpoint did not reach significance. A 2013 systematic review of 53 plant anxiolytics placed E. californica explicitly in the group with preclinical evidence only and no human clinical trials, and a 2015 systematic review of herbal medicine for insomnia identified only four herbal monopreparations with RCT evidence, none of them this one. Sedative and sleep-inducing activity is established in mice, but animal pharmacology does not raise the grade above traditional.

Sources (9)
  1. A combination of Eschscholtzia californica Cham. and Valeriana officinalis L. extracts for adjustment insomnia: A prospective observational study
    Abdellah SA, Berlin A, Blondeau C, et al., 2019 · PMID 32257874 · mixed

    Open-label observational study (no placebo) in adults with adjustment insomnia: ISI fell from 16.09 +/- 3.67 to 11.32 +/- 4.78 over 4 weeks (p<0.0001) and sleep efficiency rose from 78.4% +/- 12.5 to 84.6% +/- 10.2 (p=0.002), but there was no improvement in sleep latency and California poppy was given together with valerian, so no effect can be attributed to the poppy alone.

  2. Plant-based medicines for anxiety disorders, Part 1: a review of preclinical studies
    Sarris J, McIntyre E, Camfield DA, 2013 · PMID 23436255 · null

    Systematic review screening 1525 papers and including 53 herbal medicines: Eschscholzia californica was listed explicitly among the 32 plants for which only preclinical evidence of anxiolytic activity exists, 'without human clinical trials', in contrast to the 21 herbs that did have human trial evidence. Direct confirmation that the human trial literature for this herb is absent rather than merely unsearched.

  3. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
    Leach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · null

    Across 14 RCTs and 1602 insomnia participants, only four orally administered herbal monopreparations had any RCT evidence (valerian, chamomile, kava, wuling); California poppy was not among them, and no herbal medicine differed significantly from placebo on any of thirteen efficacy measures.

  4. A combination of melatonin, vitamin B6 and medicinal plants in the treatment of mild-to-moderate insomnia: A prospective pilot study
    Lemoine P, Bablon JC, Da Silva C, 2019 · PMID 31331545 · mixed

    Single-arm, open-label, 40 participants: mean sleep-quality score rose 1.9 points (5.4 to 7.3, p<0.001) on a capsule containing melatonin, vitamin B6, California poppy, passionflower and lemon balm; with no control arm and melatonin in the formula, the result cannot be assigned to California poppy.

  5. Double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a fixed combination containing two plant extracts (Crataegus oxyacantha and Eschscholtzia californica) and magnesium in mild-to-moderate anxiety disorders
    Hanus M, Lafon J, Mathieu M, 2004 · PMID 14741074 · mixed

    RCT in 264 patients with generalised anxiety (not insomnia): Hamilton total score fell -10.6 with the hawthorn-poppy-magnesium product vs -8.9 with placebo (p=0.005), while the somatic subscore difference was not significant (-6.5 vs -5.7, p=0.054); adverse events occurred in 15/130 (11.5%) of the active group vs 13/134 (9.7%) of placebo, mainly mild-to-moderate digestive or psychopathological disorders.

  6. Neurophysiological effects of an extract of Eschscholzia californica Cham. (Papaveraceae)
    Rolland A, Fleurentin J, Lanhers MC, Misslin R, Mortier F, 2001 · PMID 11507727 · mixed

    Flumazenil, a benzodiazepine-receptor antagonist, suppressed the extract's sedative and anxiolytic effects in mice, indicating affinity for the benzodiazepine receptor; the extract did not protect against pentylenetetrazol convulsions and showed no muscle-relaxant, antidepressant, neuroleptic or antihistaminic activity, though peripheral analgesic effects were seen. Animal data only.

  7. Advanced In Vitro Safety Assessment of Herbal Medicines for the Treatment of Non-Psychotic Mental Disorders in Pregnancy
    Spiess D, Winker M, Dolder Behna A, Grundemann C, Simoes-Wust AP, 2022 · PMID 35814220 · mixed

    In vitro placental (BeWo b30) safety study prompted by the fact that these herbs 'have not yet been officially approved in pregnancy due to lack of safety data': California poppy extract did not significantly alter protein expression, and its marker alkaloid protopine was 'inconspicuous in all experiments and showed no adverse effects'. No tested phytochemical was genotoxic at 0.1 or 1 uM. The authors conclude California poppy is likely to be safe in pregnancy, but this is cell-culture evidence, not a clinical pregnancy study.

  8. Alkaloids in commercial preparations of California poppy - Quantification, intestinal permeability and microbiota interactions
    Chauveau A, Geirnaert A, Babst A, Treyer A, Lacroix C, Hamburger M, Potterat O, 2023 · PMID 37673017 · mixed

    Across eight commercial California poppy products, californidine content ranged from 0.13 to 2.55 mg/g, escholtzine from 0.05 to 0.63 mg/g and protopine from 0.008 to 0.200 mg/g - close to a 20-fold spread between products. In Caco-2 cells californidine was low-to-moderately permeable while escholtzine and protopine were highly permeable; none of the three alkaloids was metabolised by gut microbiota over 24 h, and neither the extract nor the alkaloids markedly affected microbial short-chain fatty acid production or bacterial viability.

  9. Behavioural effects of the American traditional plant Eschscholzia californica: sedative and anxiolytic properties
    Rolland A, Fleurentin J, Lanhers MC, et al., 1991 · PMID 1680240 · positive

    In mice, aqueous extract reduced locomotion and rearing at doses above 100 mg/kg and induced sleep at doses above 100 mg/kg, with anxiolytic effects at 25 mg/kg in the staircase and light/dark tests; no toxic effect was seen after intraperitoneal or oral dosing. Animal data only.
