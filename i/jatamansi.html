Herbs

Jatamansi (Nardostachys jatamansi)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Jatamansi is a Himalayan root that Ayurvedic and Unani physicians have used as a calming, sleep-promoting medicine for thousands of years. It clearly does something in animals, and one modern trial showed it lowers blood pressure in people. But the only human sleep study was tiny, had no placebo group, and the herb it was compared against actually did better, so its long reputation for sleep has never really been tested.

Safety

Human safety data are sparse and come almost entirely from a single 40-patient, 4-week hypertension RCT at 3 g/day, in which no safety signal was reported; there is no long-term human safety dataset, and no pregnancy, lactation or paediatric data were found. A targeted search for acute-toxicity work on this species returned only a study of a seven-plant polyherbal formulation, which cannot speak to jatamansi alone, so dedicated repeated-dose toxicology remains absent. A clinically important observation is that jatamansi significantly lowered both systolic and diastolic blood pressure in that trial, so blood-pressure effects should be treated as a real pharmacological property rather than a theoretical one. Sourcing is also a genuine quality concern: the species is reported as threatened in its natural habitat because of over-exploitation and illegal trade in its rhizomes, which raises the risk of substitution and adulteration in the commercial supply.

Who should be careful: No trial-established contraindications. Caution is warranted in people on blood-pressure medication or prone to hypotension given the documented pressure-lowering effect, and alongside prescribed sedatives. In the absence of any pregnancy, breastfeeding or paediatric safety data in the retrieved literature, avoid in those groups.

Interactions: Plausible additive hypotension with antihypertensive medication: the hypertension RCT enrolled patients already taking up to two antihypertensive drugs and observed further significant reductions in systolic and diastolic pressure. Plausible additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines and other CNS depressants, since anxiolytic effects in mice were abolished by flumazenil and picrotoxin, implicating the GABA-benzodiazepine channel complex. No formal human drug-interaction studies were found.

What the evidence says

The evidence base for jatamansi in insomnia is dominated by traditional documentation rather than trials. In the single retrieved human insomnia study, an unblinded comparative trial in primary insomnia (Anidra) with 30 completers split between arms, jatamansi churna 4 g three times daily with milk for one month produced reported improvements of 61.34% in sleep initiation, 48.25% in sleep duration, 53.08% in disturbed sleep and 43.85% in disruption of routine work (all p<0.001), but there was no placebo arm, outcomes were subjective percentage scales, and the comparator Valeriana wallichii performed better on every measure. Placebo-controlled evidence for the herb exists only outside sleep: in a single-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial in 40 patients with stage-1 essential hypertension, 3 g/day for 4 weeks lowered systolic pressure from 144.20 +/- 11.35 to 134.30 +/- 10.08 mmHg and diastolic from 94.9 +/- 3.4 to 83.10 +/- 5.29 mmHg (both p<0.001), with no significant change on placebo. In animals, rhizome powder significantly reduced horizontal activity in the open-field test in rats (p<0.001) and, in mice, shortened latency of sleep onset (p<0.01) and extended total sleep duration (p<0.05), without affecting gross behaviour or rota-rod coordination. A separate mouse study found 250 mg/kg for 7 days produced diazepam-comparable anxiolytic effects that flumazenil and picrotoxin blocked, implicating the GABA-benzodiazepine complex, yet biodistribution showed only 0.1 +/- 0.02 of the radiolabel reaching brain versus 11.65 +/- 1.33 in kidney at 1 hour, which the authors attributed to poor blood-brain-barrier penetration. A 2015 systematic review of 14 insomnia RCTs found no monopreparation trials of jatamansi.

Doses studied: 4 g of rhizome powder three times daily with milk for 1 month in the single unblinded insomnia study; 3 g/day (one capsule three times daily) for 4 weeks in the placebo-controlled hypertension RCT. No dose has been validated against placebo for sleep. Animal sedation and anxiolysis were seen at 250 mg/kg orally in mice.

Why this grade: Jatamansi is an ancient Ayurvedic and Unani nervine explicitly recommended for insomnia, but no adequate human trial supports it for that use. The only insomnia study retrieved is a small, unblinded, placebo-free comparative trial with 15 completers per arm using non-validated percentage-improvement outcomes; it also found the comparator herb (Valeriana wallichii) performed better on every measure. A 2015 systematic review of herbal medicine for insomnia found RCT evidence for only four herbal monopreparations, none of them jatamansi, and a database-wide search finds only two randomised controlled trials of this species in total - one in essential hypertension and one of a four-herb vaginal tablet - neither in sleep. Animal work shows CNS-depressant and GABA-mediated anxiolytic effects, but one of those studies found the extract largely fails to cross the blood-brain barrier, which complicates the mechanism rather than confirming it.

  1. A comparative clinical study on the effect of Tagara (Valeriana wallichii DC.) and Jatamansi (Nardostachys jatamansi DC.) in the management of Anidra (primary insomnia).
    Journal articleToolika E, Bhat NP, Shetty SK, 2015 · PMID 26730138 · mixed

    Unblinded comparative trial, 30 completers (15 per arm), jatamansi churna 4 g three times daily for 1 month in primary insomnia: reported improvements of 61.34% in sleep initiation, 48.25% in sleep duration, 53.08% in disturbed sleep and 43.85% in routine-work disturbance (all p<0.001); no placebo arm, and the Valeriana wallichii comparator outperformed jatamansi on every outcome (76.00%, 55.17%, 69.58% and 73.95% respectively).

  2. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisLeach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · null

    Systematic review of 14 RCTs in 1602 insomnia participants identified RCT evidence for only four herbal monopreparations (valerian, chamomile, kava, wuling); jatamansi was not among them, and no herbal medicine beat placebo on any of thirteen efficacy measures.

  3. Efficacy of Nardostachys jatamansi (D.Don) DC in essential hypertension: A randomized controlled study.
    Randomised trialBhat MDA, Malik SA, 2020 · PMID 33066862 · positive

    Single-blind randomised placebo-controlled trial, 40 patients with stage-1 hypertension already taking up to two antihypertensive drugs, 3 g/day for 4 weeks: systolic pressure fell from 144.20 +/- 11.35 to 134.30 +/- 10.08 mmHg and diastolic from 94.9 +/- 3.4 to 83.10 +/- 5.29 mmHg (both p<0.001), with no significant change on placebo. Condition studied was hypertension, not insomnia; this is one of only two randomised controlled trials of the species in PubMed.

  4. Central nervous system depressant activity of Jatamansi (Nardostachys jatamansi DC.) rhizome.
    Journal articlePanara K, Nariya M, Karra N, 2022 · PMID 35813358 · positive

    Rhizome powder significantly reduced horizontal activity in the open-field test in albino rats (p<0.001) and, in mice, reduced latency of onset of sleep (p<0.01) and extended total sleep duration (p<0.05) versus water control, while producing no significant change in gross behaviour or rota-rod fall-off latency. Animal data only.

  5. Anxiolytic actions of Nardostachys jatamansi via GABA benzodiazepine channel complex mechanism and its biodistribution studies.
    Journal articleRazack S, Kandikattu HK, Venuprasad MP, et al., 2018 · PMID 29934858 · mixed

    Oral extract 250 mg/kg for 7 days produced diazepam-comparable anxiolytic effects in mice that were blocked by flumazenil and picrotoxin, with raised brain GABA and monoamines; however biodistribution showed only 0.1 +/- 0.02 in brain at 1 hour versus 11.65 +/- 1.33 in kidney and 6.07 +/- 0.94 in liver, which the authors attributed to an inability to cross the blood-brain barrier.

  6. Secondary metabolite profiling, cytotoxicity, anti-inflammatory potential and in vitro inhibitory activities of Nardostachys jatamansi on key enzymes linked to hyperglycemia, hypertension and cognitive disorders.
    Journal articleBose B, Tripathy D, Chatterjee A, Tandon P, Kumaria S, 2018 · PMID 30668444 · mixed

    Documents traditional use of the rhizome as a sedative and brain tonic for insomnia, epilepsy, hysteria, anxiety and depression across Ayurveda, Unani and Chinese systems since roughly 1000-800 BC, and reports the species has become threatened in its natural habitats through over-exploitation and illegal trade of rhizomes for herbal and pharmaceutical manufacture. Phytochemical and in vitro enzyme-inhibition study only; it reports no efficacy outcome for sleep in humans or animals.
