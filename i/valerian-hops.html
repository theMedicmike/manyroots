Herbs

Valerian–hops combination

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Valerian combined with hops has been tested more seriously than either herb alone, and a few randomised trials suggest it may help people fall asleep a little faster or sleep slightly longer. But the picture is weaker than the marketing suggests: the trials that showed a benefit over a dummy pill were run by the company that makes the extract, the largest and most careful trial found almost nothing on the sleep-lab measurements, and independent reviewers who have pooled the valerian research keep concluding it does not beat placebo. It appears safe over a few weeks and did not cause rebound insomnia when stopped, but it is not a proven sleep remedy.

Safety

Across trials of up to 4 weeks, valerian-hops was well tolerated. Morin 2005 reported no serious adverse events, no significant residual next-day effects and no rebound insomnia after discontinuation. Schmitz 1998 reported a single adverse reaction (stomach complaints) and, notably, withdrawal symptoms after stopping the benzodiazepine comparator but not the herbal preparation. Maroo 2013 recorded 12 treatment-emergent adverse events, most commonly mild drowsiness, versus 16 with zolpidem. Two caveats temper this: a 2015 meta-analysis found a greater number of adverse events per person with valerian than with placebo, and Maroo 2013 explicitly named long-term benefit and dependence liability as open questions. Safety beyond about 4 weeks, and use in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children, has not been established.

Who should be careful: Not established as safe in pregnancy, lactation or children. The hops component contains potent phytoestrogens, so the same caution applies in hormone-sensitive conditions as for hops alone. Avoid before driving or operating machinery until you know how it affects you.

Interactions: Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids, sedating antihistamines and other CNS depressants. None of the trials retrieved reported liver injury, but an independent 2005 review of traditional sleep herbs advised avoiding high-titre alcoholic valerian extracts and powdered valerian root in favour of aqueous and low-titre hydro-alcoholic preparations, and choosing valerian products free of valepotriates - a reasonable precaution in liver disease or alongside hepatotoxic medication. Theoretical hormonal interaction from the hops fraction (hormone therapy, oral contraceptives, tamoxifen).

What the evidence says

The fixed valerian-hops extract Ze 91019 (500 mg valerian plus 120 mg hops) reduced sleep latency versus placebo over 4 weeks in patients with non-organic insomnia while the same 500 mg of valerian extract alone did not, in a trial authored by the extract manufacturer (Koetter 2007). The largest trial, a 9-centre US study of 184 adults with mild insomnia randomised to valerian 187 mg plus hops 41.9 mg nightly, placebo, or diphenhydramine, found only modest improvements in subjective sleep parameters, reported that few group comparisons with placebo reached statistical significance, and found no significant group difference on any polysomnographic sleep-continuity variable and no alteration of stage 3-4 or REM sleep, though quality of life (physical component) was significantly better in the valerian-hops group at 28 days (Morin 2005). A 2025 randomised placebo-controlled feasibility trial in 40 people with occasional sleep problems reported, in exploratory analysis, a mean daily sleep-duration increase of 21.7 min (p = 0.019) and 48.7 min on the shortest night, with no effect on daytime cognition or psychological outcomes (Schicktanz 2025; manufacturer co-authored). Two further trials lacked a placebo arm: a hops-valerian preparation was equivalent to bromazepam over 2 weeks (Mann-Whitney statistic 0.50, lower 95% CI bound 0.46), with withdrawal symptoms documented only for the benzodiazepine (Schmitz 1998), and a valerian-passiflora-hops polyherbal improved total sleep time, sleep latency, nightly awakenings and Insomnia Severity Index within both arms but showed no statistically significant difference from zolpidem, with 12 treatment-emergent adverse events versus 16 (Maroo 2013). Against this, independent syntheses of the valerian literature are consistently negative: a systematic review of 37 studies concluded valerian is 'safe but not effective', with none of the most methodologically rigorous studies showing an effect (Taibi 2007); a meta-analysis of 14 RCTs and 1602 participants found no statistically significant difference between any herbal medicine and placebo or active control on any of thirteen efficacy measures, with more adverse events per person on valerian than placebo (Leach 2015); and umbrella reviews in 2023 and 2024 concluded valerian did not significantly improve sleep outcomes and lacks empirical support for insomnia. A 2025 scoping review of 51 over-the-counter insomnia RCTs listed valerian with hops and passionflower among products showing benefit compared with prescription medication alone, while concluding that over-the-counter products overall show 'promising, but inconclusive' findings.

Doses studied: Ze 91019: 500 mg valerian dry extract (45% methanol, DER 5.3:1) plus 120 mg hops dry extract (DER 6.6:1) nightly for 4 weeks (Koetter 2007; Schicktanz 2025 used the same product over a 21-day treatment period). Morin 2005 used 2 tablets nightly of 187 mg valerian plus 41.9 mg hops native extracts for 28 days. No dose has been validated in an independent, adequately powered efficacy trial.

Why this grade: Several randomised trials of fixed valerian-hops extracts exist, so this is better tested than either herb alone - but the result does not meet the 'moderate' bar, which requires the evidence to be overall positive. The only trial in which the combination beat placebo on a prespecified primary sleep endpoint was run by the extract manufacturer; the largest and most rigorous trial (n = 184, 9 centres, with polysomnography) reported that few placebo comparisons reached significance and found no group difference on any objective sleep-continuity variable; the most recent positive finding was exploratory within a 40-person feasibility study, again manufacturer co-authored; and the two remaining trials had no placebo arm. Four independent syntheses of the valerian literature - a 2007 systematic review, a 2015 meta-analysis, and umbrella reviews in 2023 and 2024 - conclude there is no demonstrated efficacy for insomnia, with the 2015 meta-analysis finding no herbal medicine superior to placebo on any of thirteen efficacy measures. Graded down to 'early' on the take-the-lower rule; it is deliberately not 'not_supported' because the combination itself has never been the subject of an independent synthesis and the largest trial's own authors described a modest hypnotic effect.

Sources (9)
  1. Valerian-hops combination and diphenhydramine for treating insomnia: a randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Morin CM, Koetter U, Bastien C, Ware JC, Wooten V, 2005 · PMID 16335333 · mixed

    Multicentre RCT in 184 adults (110 women, 74 men; mean age 44.3) with mild insomnia across 9 US sleep centres: modest improvements in subjective sleep parameters occurred with valerian-hops and diphenhydramine, but few group comparisons with placebo reached statistical significance, and there was no significant group difference on any polysomnographic sleep-continuity variable and no alteration of stages 3-4 or REM sleep. Quality of life (physical component) was significantly better in the valerian-hops group at 28 days. No serious adverse events, no significant residual effects and no rebound insomnia on discontinuation.

  2. A systematic review of valerian as a sleep aid: safe but not effective.
    Taibi DM, Landis CA, Petry H, Vitiello MV, 2007 · PMID 17517355 · negative

    Systematic review of 36 articles describing 37 studies (29 controlled trials, 8 open-label) out of 592 identified. Most studies found no significant differences between valerian and placebo either in healthy individuals or in people with general sleep disturbance or insomnia, and none of the most recent studies - which were also the most methodologically rigorous - found significant effects of valerian on sleep. The authors concluded that the evidence supports valerian as safe with only rare adverse events but does not support its clinical efficacy as a sleep aid for insomnia.

  3. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Leach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · negative

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomised controlled trials involving 1602 participants with insomnia across four orally administered herbal monopreparations (valerian, chamomile, kava, wuling). There was no statistically significant difference between any herbal medicine and placebo, or any herbal medicine and active control, for any of the thirteen measures of clinical efficacy. On safety, a similar or smaller number of adverse events per person was reported with kava, chamomile and wuling than placebo, but a greater number of events per person was reported with valerian. The authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to support herbal medicine for insomnia.

  4. Does valerian work for insomnia? An umbrella review of the evidence.
    Valente V, Machado D, Jorge S, Drake CL, Marques DR, 2024 · PMID 38359657 · negative

    Umbrella review of systematic reviews of valerian for sleep disturbance; 70 records retrieved, 8 eligible for qualitative analysis. The data showed a good safety profile but no evidence of efficacy for the treatment of insomnia; valerian appeared effective for subjective improvement in sleep quality but this was not demonstrated with quantitative or objective measurements. The authors concluded that despite widespread use and prescription by general practitioners and psychiatrists, valerian does not have empirical support for insomnia.

  5. A randomized, double blind, placebo-controlled, prospective clinical study to demonstrate clinical efficacy of a fixed valerian hops extract combination (Ze 91019) in patients suffering from non-organic sleep disorder.
    Koetter U, Schrader E, Käufeler R, Brattström A, 2007 · PMID 17486686 · positive

    In patients with non-organic insomnia (ICD-10 F51.0-51.2) and baseline sleep latency >= 30 min, 4 weeks of the fixed valerian (500 mg) plus hops (120 mg) extract combination significantly reduced sleep latency versus placebo, while the identical amount of valerian extract alone failed to be superior to placebo. Objective sleep parameters were recorded with a home QUISI recorder. The lead author's affiliation is the extract manufacturer, Max Zeller Söhne AG.

  6. Effects of a Valerian-Hops Extract Combination (Ze 91019) on Sleep Duration and Daytime Cognitive and Psychological Parameters in Occasional Insomnia: A Randomized Controlled Feasibility Trial.
    Schicktanz N, Gerhards C, Schlitt T, et al., 2025 · PMID 40462685 · positive

    Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled feasibility trial in 40 adults with occasional sleep problems (21-day run-in, 21-day treatment, Fitbit tracking). Exploratory analyses showed a statistically significant mean daily sleep-duration increase of 21.7 min versus placebo (p = 0.019) and a 48.7 min increase on the shortest night of the treatment period, with no statistically significant effect on cognitive or psychological outcomes. The primary aim was feasibility, not efficacy, and three authors are affiliated with the manufacturer Max Zeller Soehne AG.

  7. Efficacy and safety of a polyherbal sedative-hypnotic formulation NSF-3 in primary insomnia in comparison to zolpidem: a randomized controlled trial.
    Maroo N, Hazra A, Das T, 2013 · PMID 23543804 · mixed

    Double-blind RCT in primary insomnia (91 recruited, 39 completers per arm) comparing a valerian-passiflora-hops polyherbal with zolpidem 10 mg for two weeks: total sleep time, sleep latency, nightly awakenings and Insomnia Severity Index improved significantly within both groups but showed no statistically significant difference between groups. Twelve treatment-emergent adverse events occurred on the herbal versus 16 on zolpidem, most commonly drowsiness, all mild. There was no placebo arm, and the authors flagged that sustained benefit and dependence liability with long-term use remain unexplored.

  8. [Comparative study for assessing quality of life of patients with exogenous sleep disorders (temporary sleep onset and sleep interruption disorders) treated with a hops-valarian preparation and a benzodiazepine drug].
    Schmitz M, Jäckel M, 1998 · PMID 9757514 · mixed

    Randomised, double-blind, parallel-group trial in patients with DSM-IV sleep disorders found a hops-valerian preparation equivalent to a benzodiazepine (bromazepam) for sleep quality, fitness and quality of life over 2 weeks (Mann-Whitney statistic 0.50, lower 95% CI bound 0.46). Only one adverse drug reaction was reported (stomach complaints, on both preparations); withdrawal symptoms were documented with the benzodiazepine but not the herb. No placebo arm, so absolute efficacy cannot be inferred.

  9. Over-the-counter products for insomnia in adults: A scoping review of randomised controlled trials.
    Salame A, Mathew S, Bhanu C, et al., 2025 · PMID 40054227 · mixed

    Scoping review of 51 randomised controlled trials of over-the-counter insomnia products (sample sizes 10-405). Valerian with hops and passionflower was among the products described as promising in demonstrating benefits compared with prescription medication alone, while valerian and melatonin were identified as having substantial evidence. The overall conclusion was that over-the-counter products show 'promising, but inconclusive findings'; side effects across studies were mostly mild and transient with no serious adverse events reported.
