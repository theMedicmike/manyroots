Conventional medicine

OTC sedating antihistamines (diphenhydramine, doxylamine)

Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

The antihistamines in most drugstore sleep aids (like Benadryl or Unisom) make many people drowsy, but careful studies show they do little for actual insomnia — and the sleep experts' guideline specifically advises against using them for it. The drowsiness effect also fades within just a few days of regular use. They can leave you groggy the next day, and long-term heavy use of this drug family has been linked to higher dementia risk in older adults, so they are a poor choice for ongoing sleep trouble.

Safety

These drugs are anticholinergic sedatives, and their harms accumulate with age and duration of use. Reported problems include next-day drowsiness and impaired vigilance plus atropinic (anticholinergic) effects such as dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and urinary retention. Tolerance to the sedative effect develops rapidly — a randomized crossover trial found it complete within 3-4 days of twice-daily diphenhydramine dosing, and a systematic review reports rapid tolerance with OTC antihistamines used for insomnia — so continued use tends to deliver side effects without benefit. A prospective cohort study linked higher cumulative use of strong anticholinergics, including first-generation antihistamines, to a 54% higher hazard of dementia at the highest exposure level.

Who should be careful: Older adults should generally avoid these for sleep given confusion, falls, urinary retention, and the dementia association; people with narrow-angle glaucoma, prostate enlargement or urinary retention, or significant cognitive impairment are at particular risk from anticholinergic effects. Anyone with chronic insomnia should be evaluated rather than self-medicating long term.

Interactions: Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids, and other CNS depressants; additive anticholinergic burden with tricyclic antidepressants, bladder antimuscarinics, and other anticholinergic drugs increases confusion and dementia-associated exposure. Diphenhydramine also inhibits CYP2D6, which can raise levels of some antidepressants and beta-blockers — a pharmacist should review concurrent medications.

What the evidence says

The 2017 AASM clinical practice guideline suggests clinicians not use diphenhydramine for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia (WEAK recommendation versus no treatment). In the largest dedicated RCT (184 adults with mild insomnia), diphenhydramine 50 mg produced only modest subjective improvements, with few comparisons versus placebo reaching significance and no significant differences on any polysomnographic sleep-continuity variable, though sleep efficiency improved during the first 14 days. A randomized crossover study found tolerance to diphenhydramine's daytime sedative effect was complete by day 4 of 50 mg twice-daily dosing, undermining continued hypnotic benefit. A systematic review of 16 H1-antagonist trials concluded results are mixed, adequately powered trials are lacking, and tolerance develops rapidly with OTC antihistamines. Doxylamine specifically has minimal dedicated trial evidence in insomnia, though the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis found it more efficacious than placebo for acute treatment (within the SMD 0.36-0.83 range). On safety, a prospective cohort of 3,434 older adults found the highest cumulative exposure to strong anticholinergics — first-generation antihistamines among the most common — was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.54 for incident dementia.

Doses studied: Diphenhydramine 25-50 mg at bedtime in trials (50 mg in the key RCT); doxylamine typically 25 mg at bedtime in OTC use, with sparse dedicated trial data.

Why this grade: The 2017 AASM guideline explicitly suggests clinicians NOT use diphenhydramine for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia; the best RCT found mostly non-significant differences from placebo with no polysomnographic benefit; tolerance to the sedative effect develops within days; doxylamine has almost no dedicated insomnia-trial evidence despite a positive acute signal in one network meta-analysis; and cumulative anticholinergic exposure is associated with increased dementia risk. A guideline recommendation against, plus mixed-to-null trial data, meets the not_supported definition.

  1. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    GuidelineSateia MJ, Buysse DJ, Krystal AD, Neubauer DN, Heald JL (2017) · PMID 27998379 · negative

    The AASM guideline states: 'We suggest that clinicians not use diphenhydramine as a treatment for sleep onset and sleep maintenance insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults' (WEAK recommendation).

  2. Valerian-hops combination and diphenhydramine for treating insomnia: a randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialMorin CM, Koetter U, Bastien C, Ware JC, Wooten V (2005) · PMID 16335333 · mixed

    In 184 adults with mild insomnia, diphenhydramine 50 mg produced modest subjective improvements with few comparisons versus placebo reaching statistical significance; sleep efficiency increased significantly during the first 14 days, but there was no significant group difference on any polysomnographic sleep-continuity variable.

  3. Tolerance to daytime sedative effects of H1 antihistamines.
    Randomised trialRichardson GS, Roehrs TA, Rosenthal L, Koshorek G, Roth T (2002) · PMID 12352276 · negative

    In a randomized, double-blind crossover study of 15 healthy men taking diphenhydramine 50 mg twice daily, objective and subjective sleepiness were higher than placebo on day 1 but indistinguishable from placebo by day 4 — tolerance to the daytime sedative effect was complete within 3 days of administration.

  4. Histamine-1 receptor antagonism for treatment of insomnia.
    Narrative reviewVande Griend JP, Anderson SL (2012) · PMID 23229983 · mixed

    A systematic review (16 included trials) found H1-antagonist effectiveness results mixed and limited by sample size and generalizability, with large appropriately controlled trials lacking; OTC antihistamines may have at most a short-term role in younger adults because tolerance develops rapidly.

  5. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisDe Crescenzo F, D'Alò GL, Ostinelli EG, et al. (2022) · PMID 35843245 · positive

    In network meta-analysis, doxylamine was among the agents more efficacious than placebo for acute treatment of insomnia (within the reported SMD range of 0.36-0.83, high-to-moderate certainty), the strongest positive evidence available for an OTC antihistamine — though long-term data were absent.

  6. Cumulative use of strong anticholinergics and incident dementia: a prospective cohort study.
    Journal articleGray SL, Anderson ML, Dublin S, et al. (2015) · PMID 25621434 · negative

    In 3,434 adults 65 and older followed a mean 7.3 years, the highest cumulative exposure to strong anticholinergics (first-generation antihistamines among the most common classes) was associated with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.54 (95% CI 1.21-1.96) for incident dementia, with a dose-response relationship (P < .001).
