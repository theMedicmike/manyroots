Herbs

Willow bark (Salix species)

Early evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Willow bark is where aspirin originally came from, and it has been tested for two different problems with two different answers. For flare-ups of long-standing back pain, a couple of old studies found the higher dose helped more than a dummy pill, though nobody has repeated that work properly in over twenty years. For arthritis, the best study found it did nothing, and we know that study could detect an effect because the anti-inflammatory drug it was tested against worked clearly. One expert panel reviewing supplements for long-term muscle and joint pain recommended against using it. Because willow bark is essentially a low dose of natural aspirin, anyone who cannot take aspirin should not take this.

Safety

Willow bark delivers salicin, which the body converts to salicylic acid, the same active family as aspirin, though at much lower salicylate exposure than an analgesic aspirin dose. Adverse effects across trials were minor and appeared lower than with NSAIDs including aspirin (PMID 25997859), and the 2014 Cochrane review noted no significant adverse events within the included herbal trials (PMID 25536022). The exception worth naming: one participant in the 210-person low back pain trial suffered a severe allergic reaction possibly attributable to the extract (PMID 10936472). Cochrane also records that willow bark had only minimal effect on platelet thrombosis compared with a cardioprotective dose of acetylsalicylate in one 51-participant trial, which means it should not be assumed to deliver aspirin's cardiovascular protection either. Nothing is known about use beyond six weeks or above 240 mg salicin/day, because nothing above that has been tested.

Who should be careful: Do not take willow bark if you have an aspirin or salicylate allergy or sensitivity, or aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease; allergic reaction in salicylate-sensitive people is the primary documented safety concern (PMID 25997859), and a severe allergic reaction occurred in one trial participant (PMID 10936472). Standard salicylate precautions follow from its salicylate content: it is not appropriate for children or adolescents, particularly during a viral illness, and should be avoided with active peptic ulcer disease or by anyone who has been told to avoid aspirin. It has not been studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Stop it well before planned surgery and tell your surgical team.

Interactions: Because willow bark supplies salicylates, combining it with aspirin, other NSAIDs, or anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs could plausibly add to bleeding risk. This has not been formally tested: no willow bark trial reviewed here enrolled patients on anticoagulants or ran a bleeding-risk endpoint, so the absence of reported bleeding events reflects absence of study, not demonstrated safety. Anyone on warfarin, a direct oral anticoagulant, clopidogrel, low-dose aspirin, or regular NSAIDs should clear willow bark with a pharmacist or their anticoagulation clinic before starting it. Willow bark is frequently sold inside multi-ingredient blends, in which case the interaction profile of every component applies.

What the evidence says

The evidence divides by condition and should not be averaged. In osteoarthritis, the best-designed trial was null: 127 outpatients with hip or knee osteoarthritis were randomized to willow bark extract at 240 mg salicin/day, diclofenac 100 mg/day, or placebo for 6 weeks, and WOMAC pain fell 8 mm (17%) with willow bark versus 5 mm (10%) with placebo, a non-significant difference of -2.8 mm (95% CI -12.1 to 6.4, p=0.55), while diclofenac fell 23 mm (47%) for a highly significant -18.0 mm versus placebo (95% CI -27.2 to -8.8, p=0.0002); the parallel 26-patient rheumatoid arthritis trial was also null (-0.8 mm, 95% CI -20.9 to 19.3, p=0.93). In chronic low back pain, a 210-patient 4-week trial found 27 of 65 patients (39%) on 240 mg salicin/day were pain-free in the final week versus 15 of 67 (21%) on 120 mg and 4 of 59 (6%) on placebo (p<0.001). The 2014 Cochrane review rated that low back pain result moderate-quality evidence from two trials and 261 participants, and graded an open-label comparison showing equivalence to rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day as very low quality. A 2009 systematic review reached the same split verdict: moderate evidence in low back pain, conflicting results in osteoarthritis, and no significant effect in a grossly underpowered rheumatoid arthritis trial. Against this, a 2019 GRADE-based evidence review of 19 dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain made an explicit recommendation against willow bark extract. All trials used ethanolic extract at up to 240 mg salicin/day for no more than six weeks, several share the same investigator group and proprietary product, and a fresh search confirms no adequately powered replication has appeared since 2004, with newer randomized trials testing willow bark only inside multi-ingredient blends.

Doses studied: Ethanolic willow bark extract standardized to 120 mg or 240 mg of salicin per day, over 4 to 6 weeks. The 240 mg/day dose was the one that produced the low back pain benefit (PMID 10936472) and also the one that failed in osteoarthritis (PMID 15517622). A 2009 systematic review confirms all trials investigated ethanolic extracts at daily doses up to 240 mg salicin over periods of up to six weeks (PMID 19140170). Nothing above 240 mg salicin/day, and nothing beyond six weeks of use, has been tested. Modern products often place willow bark inside multi-ingredient blends at undisclosed salicin content, which is not equivalent to the tested preparations.

Why this grade: The evidence splits sharply by condition and the aggregate does not reach 'moderate'. In osteoarthritis the best-designed trial was frankly null: willow bark at 240 mg salicin/day did not separate from placebo on WOMAC pain (difference -2.8 mm, 95% CI -12.1 to 6.4, p=0.55) in a trial where the diclofenac comparator separated clearly, proving the trial could detect a real effect. In chronic low back pain Cochrane rated the willow bark result moderate-quality evidence, but that rests on just two trials totalling 261 participants, conducted in 2000-2001, using one proprietary ethanolic extract, with substantial overlap in investigators; a fresh search confirms no adequately powered replication has appeared in more than two decades, only multi-ingredient blends. Every trial ran six weeks or less at 240 mg salicin/day or below. A 2019 GRADE-based evidence review of dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain went further and recommended against willow bark extract, which is an argument for grading this 'not_supported'; it is held at 'early' only because the Cochrane low back pain finding is a genuine moderate-quality positive that would be misrepresented by a not_supported label. Few, small, old, single-source trials with a null result in one target condition and a formal recommendation against in the other is 'early', and the lower of the two plausible grades is taken deliberately.

Sources (7)
  1. Efficacy and safety of willow bark extract in the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis: results of 2 randomized double-blind controlled trials.
    Randomised trialBiegert C, Wagner I, Ludtke R, Kotter I, Lohmuller C, Gunaydin I, Taxis K, Heide L, 2004 · PMID 15517622 · negative

    Two randomized double-blind controlled trials with 6-week follow-up. In the osteoarthritis trial, 127 outpatients with hip or knee osteoarthritis and WOMAC pain of at least 30 mm received willow bark extract (240 mg salicin/day, n=43), diclofenac 100 mg/day (n=43) or placebo (n=41). WOMAC pain decreased 8 mm (17%) with willow bark and 23 mm (47%) with diclofenac, versus 5 mm (10%) with placebo. The willow bark versus placebo difference was not significant (-2.8 mm, 95% CI -12.1 to 6.4, p=0.55, ANCOVA) while diclofenac versus placebo was highly significant (-18.0 mm, 95% CI -27.2 to -8.8, p=0.0002). The parallel 26-patient rheumatoid arthritis trial was also null (-0.8 mm, 95% CI -20.9 to 19.3, p=0.93). Authors conclude willow bark 'showed no relevant efficacy in patients with OA.'

  2. Treatment of low back pain exacerbations with willow bark extract: a randomized double-blind study.
    Randomised trialChrubasik S, Eisenberg E, Balan E, Weinberger T, Luzzati R, Conradt C, 2000 · PMID 10936472 · positive

    210 patients with an exacerbation of chronic low back pain and current pain of 5 or more out of 10 were randomized to oral willow bark extract at 120 mg or 240 mg salicin/day or placebo, with tramadol as sole rescue medication, in a 4-week blinded trial; 191 completed. Patients pain-free without tramadol for at least 5 days in the final week were 27 of 65 (39%) on high dose, 15 of 67 (21%) on low dose and 4 of 59 (6%) on placebo (p<0.001). The high-dose response was evident after one week. Significantly more placebo patients required tramadol each week (p<0.001). One patient suffered a severe allergic reaction, perhaps to the extract.

  3. Herbal medicine for low-back pain.
    Meta-analysisOltean H, Robbins C, van Tulder MW, Berman BM, Bombardier C, Gagnier JJ, 2014 · PMID 25536022 · mixed

    Cochrane review of 14 RCTs (2,050 participants) in non-specific low back pain. Daily doses of Salix alba standardized to 120 mg or 240 mg salicin 'are probably better than placebo for short-term improvements in pain and rescue medication' - moderate-quality evidence from two trials, 261 participants. An additional trial demonstrating relative equivalence to rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day (228 participants) was graded very low quality. In one 51-participant trial, willow bark minimally affected platelet thrombosis compared with a cardioprotective dose of acetylsalicylate. No significant adverse events were noted within the included trials, and the completeness of reporting across the herbal trials was described as generally poor.

  4. Dietary Ingredients as an Alternative Approach for Mitigating Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: Evidence-Based Recommendations for Practice and Research in the Military.
    Journal articleCrawford C, Boyd C, Paat CF, Meissner K, Lentino C, Teo L, Berry K, Deuster P, 2019 · PMID 30986309 · negative

    A steering committee assessed 19 dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain using systematic review, meta-analysis where feasible, and GRADE, to inform practice and self-care decisions. Recommendations were made AGAINST the use of willow bark extract, alongside collagen, creatine, devil's claw, l-carnitine, methylsulfonylmethane, pycnogenol and vitamin E. Eight other ingredients including curcuma and vitamin D were conditionally recommended. The panel concluded that the scientific evidence overall is 'insufficiently robust to establish definitive clinical practice guidelines,' but that providers have enough evidence to make informed decisions about safe use.

  5. A systematic review on the effectiveness of willow bark for musculoskeletal pain.
    Systematic reviewVlachojannis JE, Cameron M, Chrubasik S, 2009 · PMID 19140170 · mixed

    Seven manuscripts reporting four confirmatory and four exploratory trials, after excluding repetitious reports of the same trial data. One confirmatory and two exploratory studies indicate a dose-dependent analgesic effect not inferior to rofecoxib in patients with low back pain. In osteoarthritis, one exploratory and one confirmatory study achieved conflicting results. No significant effect was seen in a confirmatory rheumatoid arthritis study, which the authors describe as grossly underpowered. All studies investigated ethanolic extracts at daily doses up to 240 mg salicin over periods of up to six weeks, with minor adverse events during treatment. The authors' verdict is moderate evidence of effectiveness in low back pain only.

  6. Treatment of low back pain with a herbal or synthetic anti-rheumatic: a randomized controlled study. Willow bark extract for low back pain.
    Randomised trialChrubasik S, Kunzel O, Model A, Conradt C, Black A, 2001 · PMID 11752510 · mixed

    Open (unblinded) randomized post-marketing study in 228 outpatients with acute exacerbations of low back pain over 4 weeks: 114 received willow bark extract (Assalix) at 240 mg salicin/day and 114 received rofecoxib 12.5 mg/day. The Arhus index improved by about 20%, its pain component by about 30% and the Total Pain Index by about 35% in both arms, with roughly 20 pain-free patients and about 60% responders per group, and similar adverse event incidence. 'There was no significant difference in effectiveness between the two treatments at the doses chosen.' Without blinding or a placebo arm this cannot establish efficacy over placebo; the 2014 Cochrane review graded it very low quality evidence.

  7. Efficacy and Safety of White Willow Bark (Salix alba) Extracts.
    Narrative reviewShara M, Stohs SJ, 2015 · PMID 25997859 · mixed

    Narrative review. Despite a use history spanning thousands of years, 'relatively few human and animal studies have been published,' and only a small number of clinical studies support use in chronic lower back and joint pain and osteoarthritis. Extracts are standardized to salicin but also contain other salicylates, polyphenols and flavonoids that may contribute to activity. Adverse effects appear minimal compared with NSAIDs including aspirin; the primary cause for concern relates to allergic reactions in salicylate-sensitive individuals. No human studies specifically document the sports-performance or weight-loss benefits for which willow bark extracts are widely marketed.
