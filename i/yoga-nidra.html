Yoga Nidra and Guided Body-Scan Relaxation

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Yoga nidra is a guided lie-down practice where a voice walks your attention through your body while you stay still and awake. People often find it deeply pleasant and calming, and a few small studies suggest it may help sleep - but when researchers pooled the randomized trials, the sleep improvements were not statistically reliable, and a sleep-lab study found no change in brain waves, heart rhythm, or how fast people fell asleep. The honest summary is that there is far too little good research here to say whether it helps insomnia, only that it seems pleasant, appears safe as far as anyone has bothered to check, and is reasonable to try if you enjoy it.

Safety

Safety for yoga nidra is essentially unstudied rather than established as good - a 2025 systematic review of all six RCTs explicitly found 'a lack of accurate reporting of adverse events and safety data across the studies,' which is the single most important safety fact to state honestly. What is known is reassuring but limited: the sleep-lab trial reported good acceptability, good tolerability and only a 5% dropout rate, and the broader autogenic-training and guided-imagery literature reports no adverse events (while noting they were seldom actually investigated). Nothing is ingested and there are no plausible physiological interactions. The practical concern is that a practice explicitly designed to induce deep relaxation while lying down can produce sleep, so it must never be done while driving, operating machinery, or supervising a child in water or near a road.

Who should be careful: No established medical contraindications, largely because no one has looked for them. Use clinician-guided caution in people with PTSD, dissociative disorders, or psychosis: sustained inward body-focused attention and guided imagery can occasionally trigger intrusive memories, depersonalization or increased distress, and trauma-informed adaptations exist for exactly this reason. Anyone with untreated sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, or insomnia driven by an untreated medical or psychiatric condition should be evaluated for that first - a relaxation recording will not address it.

Interactions: No known pharmacological interactions. Because pooled trial results did not reach statistical significance, yoga nidra should not be used as a reason to delay or replace CBT-I, which remains the guideline-recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, or to alter a prescribed sleep medication without your prescriber.

What the evidence says

Evidence for yoga nidra in insomnia is early and, at the pooled level, null. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis found only five unique studies eligible across three separate analyses. For sleep quality, two RCTs (n=181) produced a large but statistically non-significant effect (SMD -1.04, p=0.5036, I-squared = 0); for insomnia severity, two RCTs (n=99) also fell short of significance (SMD -1.00, p=0.0616, I-squared = 71.4%). Only the two observational studies (n=45) reached significance (SMD -1.82, p=0.0261). The authors rated certainty VERY LOW and described their conclusions as 'preliminary and hypothesis-generating,' noting the geographic concentration of trials in India. A separate 2025 systematic review of six RCTs (n=244 total) reported that most trials showed improvements in sleep onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency, but that most had moderate-to-high risk of bias from lack of blinding and incomplete outcome reporting, and that adverse events and safety data were not accurately reported anywhere in the literature. Objective physiological data are negative: a randomized sleep-lab study in 22 adults with self-reported insomnia, in which participants lay quietly for 90 minutes at one visit and half were randomized to 30 minutes of yoga nidra first at a second visit, found no between-group changes in occipital alpha EEG power (intervention 13% plus or minus 70% versus control -20% plus or minus 40%), heart rate variability, or sleep onset latency; only respiratory rate differed (-1.4 breaths per minute during and -2.1 after, versus +0.2 and +0.4 in controls, p=0.03 for both). Individual positive trials exist but are fragile: a 79-person open-label trial in frontline COVID-19 healthcare workers found ISI fell from 6.10 to 3.03 with yoga nidra (p<0.001) versus 6.09 to 5.93 with relaxation-to-music (p=0.828), though at a baseline ISI of about 6 those participants were below the ISI threshold of 8 for clinically significant insomnia. A 41-person trial in diagnosed chronic insomnia found improved N2 and N3 percentages and reduced salivary cortisol (p=0.041) after yoga nidra, though both arms also increased the lightest sleep stage (N1%) and repeat polysomnography was performed only in volunteers rather than the full sample. A further 24-person trial in acute insomnia used yoga nidra only once weekly within a mixed yoga programme, so it cannot isolate the practice. Notably, the closest larger literature, on autogenic training and guided imagery, shows the same pattern: significant versus waitlist (SMD -1.16, 95% CI -1.92 to -0.40) but NOT versus sham (SMD -0.50, 95% CI -1.19 to 0.19).

Doses studied: Trials used 12 to 30 minutes of guided audio practice, ranging from three sessions per week for 4 weeks to 30 minutes daily for 2 weeks. Single 30-minute sessions were used in the sleep-lab study. The chronic-insomnia trial does not document its session schedule in the published abstract beyond the authors' advice that the practice be used after supervised practice sessions. One trial in acute insomnia delivered yoga nidra only once weekly for 30 days as part of a broader 60-minute daily yoga programme, so its dose is not attributable to yoga nidra alone.

Why this grade: The entire randomized evidence base for yoga nidra in sleep amounts to roughly six small trials with about 244 participants combined, most at moderate-to-high risk of bias and heavily concentrated in India - and at least one of those six delivered yoga nidra only once weekly inside a mixed yoga package. When the 2026 meta-analysis pooled them, neither outcome reached statistical significance: PSQI SMD -1.04 (p=0.5036) across two RCTs (n=181) and ISI SMD -1.00 (p=0.0616) across two RCTs (n=99), with the authors rating certainty VERY LOW and calling their own findings 'preliminary and hypothesis-generating.' A sleep-lab trial found no effect on EEG, heart rate variability or sleep onset latency. This is small and preliminary human evidence - 'early.' It is not 'traditional', because real randomized trials in insomnia do exist; and it is not 'moderate', because those trials do not pool to a significant result.

Sources (7)
  1. Effect of yoga Nidra (yogic sleep) in sleep disturbances and insomnia: systematic review and metanalysis.
    Singh S, Dhanlika D, Singh U, Haldar P, Sharma G, 2026 (Sleep and Breathing) · PMID 42043659 · null

    Only five unique studies were eligible. Two RCTs (n=181) gave a non-significant PSQI effect (SMD -1.04, p=0.5036, I-squared = 0); two RCTs (n=99) gave a non-significant ISI effect (SMD -1.00, p=0.0616, I-squared = 71.4%); only two observational studies (n=45) reached significance (SMD -1.82, p=0.0261, I-squared = 79.8%). Certainty of evidence was VERY LOW and the authors state findings 'should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating,' with generalizability limited by trials being concentrated in India.

  2. A closer look at yoga nidra- early randomized sleep lab investigations.
    Sharpe E, Butler MP, Clark-Stone J, Soltanzadeh R, Jindal R, Hanes D, Bradley R, 2023 (Journal of Psychosomatic Research) · PMID 36731199 · null

    22 adults with self-reported insomnia; all lay quietly for 90 minutes at Visit 1, and at Visit 2 half were randomized to 30 minutes of yoga nidra first. There were NO between-group changes in alpha EEG power at O1 (intervention 13% plus or minus 70%; control -20% plus or minus 40%), heart rate variability, or sleep onset latency in response to yoga nidra. Only respiratory rate differed significantly (-1.4 breaths/min during and -2.1 after, versus +0.2 and +0.4 in controls; p=0.03 for both). The practice showed good acceptability and tolerability with a 5% dropout rate.

  3. Efficacy of Yoga Nidra in Managing Sleep Disorders: A Systematic Review of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Dutta A, Mooventhan A, Nivethitha L, Dharani E, 2025 (Journal of Integrative and Complementary Medicine) · PMID 41144325 · mixed

    Six RCTs totaling 244 participants across chronic insomnia, hypertension, acute insomnia, COVID-19 healthcare workers and sportspersons. Most reported improvements in sleep onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency versus comparators including cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, progressive muscle relaxation and music-based relaxation. However, most studies had moderate-to-high risk of bias due to lack of blinding and incomplete outcome reporting, protocols and outcome measures were heterogeneous, and 'there was a lack of accurate reporting of adverse events and safety data across the studies.'

  4. Yoga nidra practice shows improvement in sleep in patients with chronic insomnia: A randomized controlled trial.
    Datta K, Tripathi M, Verma M, Masiwal D, Mallick HN, 2021 (National Medical Journal of India) · PMID 34825538 · positive

    41 patients with chronic insomnia randomized to CBT-I (n=20) or yoga nidra (n=21). Both arms improved subjective total sleep time, sleep efficiency, wake after sleep onset and sleep quality, and both improved objective total sleep time and total wake duration on polysomnography - while both also increased N1%, the lightest sleep stage, as a proportion of total sleep time. Yoga nidra showed additional improvement in N2% and N3%, and salivary cortisol fell significantly after yoga nidra (p=0.041). Repeat polysomnography was only performed in volunteers, not the full sample.

  5. Efficacy of Yoga Nidra on Depression, Anxiety, and Insomnia in Frontline COVID-19 Healthcare Workers: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial.
    Gunjiganvi M, Rai S, Awale R, Mishra P, Gupta D, Gurjar M, 2023 (International Journal of Yoga Therapy) · PMID 37327384 · positive

    79 frontline healthcare workers randomized open-label to 30 minutes daily of yoga nidra (n=39) or relaxation-to-music (n=40) for a 2-week duty period, delivered via YouTube. Insomnia Severity Index fell from 6.10 plus or minus 3.53 to 3.03 plus or minus 2.88 with yoga nidra (p<0.001) versus 6.09 plus or minus 5.37 to 5.93 plus or minus 5.95 with music (p=0.828). Note that a baseline ISI of about 6 is below the ISI cut-off of 8 for clinically significant insomnia, so these were not insomnia patients, and the trial was open-label with no blinding.

  6. Effects of Yoga on Psychological Health and Sleep Quality of Patients With Acute Insomnia: A Preliminary Study.
    Verma K, Srivastava A, Singh D, 2022 (Advances in Mind-Body Medicine) · PMID 36351185 · mixed

    24 patients with acute insomnia randomized 12 to a yoga group and 12 to conventional treatment for 30 days. The yoga programme ran 60 minutes daily but included yoga nidra only ONCE per week alongside Jala Neti three times weekly, so yoga nidra cannot be isolated as the active element. Post-intervention, reductions in perceived stress and improvements in PSQI sleep quality were significantly greater in the yoga group, but cognitive failure did not improve and environmental quality of life did not improve. Very small, single-site, unblinded, and counted among the six RCTs in the 2025 systematic review.

  7. Hypnotherapy for insomnia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Lam TH, Chung KF, Yeung WF, Yu BYM, Yung KP, Ng THY, 2015 (Complementary Therapies in Medicine) · PMID 26365453 · mixed

    Thirteen trials, 502 subjects, of which seven studied autogenic training or guided imagery - the closest studied analogues to guided body-scan relaxation. These shortened sleep latency versus waitlist (SMD -1.16, 95% CI -1.92 to -0.40, p=0.003, I-squared = 0) but NOT versus sham intervention (SMD -0.50, 95% CI -1.19 to 0.19, p=0.15, I-squared = 0). Eleven of 13 studies had low methodological quality (modified Jadad score below 3). No adverse events were reported, though they were seldom investigated.
