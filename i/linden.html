Linden flower (Tilia species)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Linden flower tea is one of the oldest bedtime drinks in Europe and is officially recognised there as a traditional remedy for restless sleep. The honest answer is that people have used it for centuries and nobody has properly tested it: there is no trial of linden on its own for insomnia. It is gentle enough that reviewers call it safe for adults, but two things are worth knowing before you brew a cup - one analysis found caffeine in commercial linden sleep teas, and another found botulism spores in loose linden flowers, which is why linden tea should never be given to a baby.

Safety

Linden's safety profile is reassuring for adults but largely inferred from long food and tisane use rather than from trials, and there are three documented cautions. First and most important, linden flowers can carry Clostridium botulinum: spores were found in 3% of 100 unwrapped linden samples at 30 spores per 100 g, with types A and B identified, while none of 100 industrially bagged samples were contaminated - and the study exists precisely because linden tea is traditionally given to infants as a sedative, making it a recognised potential vehicle for infant botulism. Second, analytical work found caffeine in linden extracts within commercial sleep-aid teas, up to 110 mg/kg in the herbal material and up to 226 micrograms per cup, a stimulant in a product sold for sleep. Third, Tilia cordata is a recognised tree aeroallergen: 11.4% of skin-prick-tested paediatric allergy patients in one Portuguese cohort were sensitised to it. A 2005 independent drug-bulletin review nonetheless grouped littleleaf linden with the traditional sleep plants that are safe enough to use despite unproven efficacy, in contrast to others it advised avoiding on toxicity grounds. No human safety trials, pregnancy data or lactation data for linden were found. Note also that most of the sedative animal pharmacology used Tilia americana var. mexicana, not the Tilia cordata or Tilia platyphyllos typically sold as European linden tea, and flavonoid content varies substantially between collection regions.

Who should be careful: Do not give linden flower tea to infants, given the documented presence of Clostridium botulinum spores in loose linden flowers and the resulting infant botulism risk; if linden is used in a household with an infant, commercially bagged product showed no contamination in the sampling. Avoid in people with known Tilia or tree-pollen sensitisation. Because no pregnancy, breastfeeding or paediatric safety studies were found, these groups should not assume the adult food-use safety record extends to concentrated extracts.

Interactions: No human drug-interaction studies were retrieved. The realistic concern is contamination rather than pharmacology: caffeine detected in commercial linden sleep teas could counteract the intended effect and add to other caffeine intake. Additive sedation with CNS depressants is theoretical only, since the sedative data are from rodents given injected extracts and one rodent study of a linden-dominant blend actually shortened total sleep time.

What the evidence says

No controlled trial has tested linden flower alone for insomnia. A 2005 independent drug-bulletin review of plants approved in France as traditionally used for minor sleep disturbances concluded that virtually nothing is known about the efficacy or dangers of most of them, and placed littleleaf linden specifically among remedies with no demonstrated efficacy but acceptable safety, alongside vervain, melissa and orange flower. A 2013 systematic review of 53 herbal anxiolytics likewise listed Tilia spp. among the 32 plants with preclinical evidence only and no human clinical trials. The only human sleep data retrieved come from an uncontrolled 41-participant pilot (mean age 45.3) of a fixed combination of linden, hawthorn, vitamin B1 and melatonin, in which Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores fell 22.4% and Insomnia Severity Index scores 19.6% over 30 days from baseline values of 8.2 and 12.7 respectively; the authors state explicitly that no placebo control was used, and the presence of melatonin makes attribution to linden impossible. Preclinical findings conflict: a bioactivity-guided study of Tilia americana var. mexicana hexane extract found that 10 and 30 mg/kg intraperitoneally potentiated pentobarbital-induced sleeping time and increased open-arm time in mice, with beta-sitosterol anxiolytic at 1-10 mg/kg and sedative at 30 mg/kg, whereas a rat electrocorticography study of an intraperitoneally injected polyherbal formulation containing 35% linden found the blend significantly shortened total sleep time versus saline while enhancing the 0.5-3 Hz slow component of somatosensory-cortex activity during sleep. Two contamination findings bear on real-world use: caffeine was detected in linden extracts within commercial sleep-aid teas at up to 110 mg/kg of herbal material and up to 226 micrograms per prepared cup, and Clostridium botulinum spores were found in 3% of unwrapped linden flower samples at 30 spores per 100 g.

Doses studied: No linden dose has been tested for insomnia in humans. The only human sleep exposure retrieved was an unspecified daily dose of linden inside a four-ingredient supplement also containing hawthorn, vitamin B1 and melatonin, taken for 30 days. Animal sedative effects were seen at 10-30 mg/kg intraperitoneally in mice using a Tilia americana var. mexicana hexane extract; the contrary rat result used an intraperitoneally injected polyherbal blend, not an oral or infused preparation.

Why this grade: Linden flower is a classic European bedtime tisane with formal traditional-use standing but no efficacy trial of its own. A search of the randomised and placebo-controlled literature for Tilia in insomnia or sleep quality returns only two records: a drug-bulletin review and an uncontrolled combination pilot. A 2005 Prescrire review stated flatly that littleleaf linden has no demonstrated efficacy while classing it as safe enough to use, and a 2013 systematic review of plant anxiolytics placed Tilia spp. among the herbs with preclinical evidence only and no human clinical trials. The single human sleep study retrieved is an uncontrolled 41-participant pilot of a four-ingredient supplement containing melatonin alongside linden, explicitly without a placebo control, so it cannot speak to linden. Animal pharmacology is real but points in conflicting directions: Tilia americana extracts potentiate pentobarbital sleeping time in mice, yet an injected polyherbal blend that was 35% linden significantly shortened total sleep time in rats. Traditional is the correct grade, and both a caffeine-contamination finding and a botulinum-spore finding in commercial linden argue against rounding up.

  1. Management of insomnia: a place for traditional herbal remedies.

    Independent drug-bulletin review of about 20 plants approved in France for minor sleep disturbances: states that virtually nothing is known of their efficacy or potential dangers, that most are suspected of toxicity and should be avoided, but specifically classes littleleaf linden, vervain, melissa and orange flower as having no demonstrated efficacy while being safe and therefore usable.

  2. Plant-based medicines for anxiety disorders, Part 1: a review of preclinical studies.
    Narrative reviewSarris J, McIntyre E, Camfield DA, 2013 · PMID 23436255 · null

    Systematic review of 53 herbal medicines: Tilia spp. was listed among the 32 plants with 'preclinical evidence of anxiolytic activity (without human clinical trials)', as distinct from the 21 herbs that had human trial evidence. Confirms the absence of human trial data for linden is a real feature of the literature.

  3. Linden flower (Tilia spp.) as potential vehicle of Clostridium botulinum spores in the transmission of infant botulism.
    Journal articleBianco MI, Luquez C, De Jong LIT, Fernandez RA, 2009 · PMID 20085187 · negative

    Because linden flower tea is used as a household remedy and given to infants as a sedative, 100 samples of unwrapped linden flowers and 100 samples of linden in tea bags were analysed: botulinum spores were found in 3% of the unwrapped samples with a spore load of 30 spores per 100 grams, C. botulinum type A in two samples and type B in one, while NONE of the industrialised tea-bag samples were contaminated. The authors conclude linden flowers must be considered a potential vehicle of C. botulinum and that drinking linden flower tea can be a risk factor for infant botulism.

  4. Enhancing Sleep Quality: Assessing the Efficacy of a Fixed Combination of Linden, Hawthorn, Vitamin B1, and Melatonin.
    Journal articleDe Simone M, De Feo R, Choucha A, Ciaglia E, Fezeu F, 2023 · PMID 38249078 · mixed

    Uncontrolled 41-participant pilot (mean age 45.3) of linden plus hawthorn, vitamin B1 and melatonin for 30 days: PSQI fell 22.4% from a baseline of 8.2 and ISI fell 19.6% from a baseline of 12.7, with psychological distress down 19.5%. The authors state 'No placebo-control was used in this study', and melatonin in the formula prevents any attribution of benefit to linden.

  5. Polyherbal Formulation Enhancing Cerebral Slow Waves in Sleeping Rats.
    Journal articleYamashiro K, Aoki M, Matsumoto N, Ikegaya Y, 2020 · PMID 32879209 · negative

    In rats given an intraperitoneal blend of 35% linden with 21% mulberry, 20% lavandin, 20% butterfly pea and 4% tulsi, the formulation significantly SHORTENED total sleep time compared with saline, while enhancing the 0.5-3 Hz slow component of primary somatosensory cortex activity during sleep. The sleep-duration result runs contrary to the herb's traditional reputation, though the route and the multi-herb blend limit what it says about linden tea.

  6. Unexpected occurrence of caffeine in sleep-inducing herbal teas.
    Journal articleMathona C, Edder P, Christen P, Bieri S, 2014 · PMID 25437162 · negative

    Caffeine was unambiguously identified in linden (Tilia spp.) extracts sold within sleep-aid herbal teas; across 11 linden-based samples concentrations ranged from traces to 110 mg/kg in the herbal material and from traces to 226 micrograms per prepared cup. A stimulant contaminant in a product sold for sleep.

  7. Bioactivity-guided isolation of beta-sitosterol and some fatty acids as active compounds in the anxiolytic and sedative effects of Tilia americana var. mexicana.
    Journal articleAguirre-Hernandez E, Rosas-Acevedo H, Soto-Hernandez M, Martinez AL, Moreno J, Gonzalez-Trujano ME, 2007 · PMID 17823876 · positive

    In mice, hexane extract at 10 and 30 mg/kg intraperitoneally potentiated pentobarbital-induced sleeping time and increased time in the open arms of the plus maze, with reduced ambulation, head dipping and rearing; isolated beta-sitosterol was anxiolytic at 1-10 mg/kg and sedative at 30 mg/kg, comparable to diazepam 0.1 mg/kg. Animal data only, and for a Mexican Tilia species rather than the European tisane.

  8. Aeroallergens sensitization in an allergic paediatric population of Cova da Beira, Portugal.
    Journal articleLoureiro G, Rabaca MA, Blanco B, Andrade S, Chieira C, Pereira C, 2005 · PMID 16045856 · negative

    Among 371 paediatric allergy outpatients skin-prick tested, 11.4% were sensitised to Tilia cordata, placing linden alongside plane tree (11.4%) and black locust (12.2%) as a documented tree aeroallergen in this population.
