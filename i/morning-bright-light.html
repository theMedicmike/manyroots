Bright Light Therapy

Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood Early evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For depression & low mood

Sitting in front of a bright white light box for about half an hour each morning has reasonable evidence behind it, strongest for depression that comes on in the darker months. In one carefully blinded trial, morning light beat a dummy treatment while an antidepressant did not - though that was a small study. Three honest limits: adding light on top of an antidepressant you are already established on has not shown a benefit, blue-light devices have not been shown to work at all, and light can tip some people into a high or agitated state, which matters a lot if you have or might have bipolar disorder. Talk to a clinician before starting if that could apply to you.

Safety

Mostly well tolerated, but not benign. The clearest documented harm is hypomania: a Cochrane review of 20 studies in non-seasonal depression found it significantly more common with bright light than control (risk ratio 4.91, 95% CI 1.66 to 14.46), with a number needed to harm of 8 (95% CI 5 to 20). Anyone with bipolar disorder, a history of manic or hypomanic episodes, or a family history of bipolar disorder should only use light therapy under clinical supervision, and anyone who becomes unusually energised, sleepless, irritable, or fast-thinking after starting should stop and contact a clinician. Beyond that, in the 122-participant double-blind non-seasonal trial all treatments including light were generally well tolerated with few significant differences in treatment-emergent adverse events. Commonly described minor effects in clinical use include eye strain, headache, and nausea, usually settling with reduced exposure time or greater distance from the box. Reporting is incomplete: the Cochrane review of preventive light therapy noted that investigators provided no information on adverse events at all, and the non-seasonal Cochrane review noted many reports did not record adverse effects systematically. The trials that generated these results used early-morning administration; other timings were not tested, so evening use should not be assumed equivalent, since light exposure shifts circadian timing and can disturb sleep.

Who should be careful: Talk to a clinician first if you have retinal or macular disease, cataract or recent eye surgery, or take photosensitising medication - the trials did not systematically assess ophthalmic outcomes, so safety in these groups is not established rather than reassured. Bipolar disorder is the key caution: pooled data from 7 double-blind trials in bipolar depression found no increased affective switch (OR 1.30), but the confidence interval (0.38-4.44) is wide and does not exclude a meaningful increase, and it sits against a Cochrane finding of a roughly fivefold increase in hypomania in the broader non-seasonal literature. Light therapy in bipolar depression should therefore be used adjunctively, under clinical supervision, alongside mood-stabilising treatment, never alone. Evidence is null for depression in people living with dementia, and blue-light devices have not been shown to work at all.

Interactions: No direct drug interactions established in these trials, but three points matter. Adding bright light on top of ongoing antidepressant pharmacotherapy for non-seasonal depression showed no benefit in pooled analysis (effect size -0.01, 95% CI -0.36 to 0.34), in contrast to the combination started simultaneously in the Lam trial, so light should not be assumed to boost an antidepressant you are already established on. Photosensitising drugs, including some antibiotics, antipsychotics, and herbal products, warrant a clinician conversation before starting daily high-intensity light exposure. And because light can precipitate hypomania, anyone taking a mood stabiliser or antipsychotic for bipolar disorder should introduce light therapy only with their prescriber's involvement.

What the evidence says

Evidence is strongest for treating seasonal depression, moderate and uneven for non-seasonal depression, and close to absent for prevention. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials found bright light treatment in seasonal affective disorder had an effect size of 0.84 (95% CI 0.60-1.08, 8 studies) and dawn simulation 0.73 (95% CI 0.37-1.08, 5 studies), with bright light in non-seasonal depression at 0.53 (95% CI 0.18-0.89, 3 studies); however, only 13% of identified studies met the review's methodological inclusion criteria, and bright light added to ongoing antidepressant pharmacotherapy for non-seasonal depression showed no effect whatsoever (effect size -0.01, 95% CI -0.36 to 0.34, 5 studies). A Cochrane review of 20 studies in non-seasonal depression found that treatment response with bright light was better than control but did not reach statistical significance overall, with significant benefit confined to high-quality studies (SMD -0.90, 95% CI -1.50 to -0.31), morning administration (SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.62 to -0.14), and sleep-deprivation responders (SMD -1.02, 95% CI -1.60 to -0.45) - and found hypomania significantly more common in the bright light group than control (risk ratio 4.91, 95% CI 1.66 to 14.46; number needed to harm 8). A more recent synthesis of 15 randomised trials (883 patients with non-seasonal major depressive disorder) reported a positive pooled effect (SMD 0.48, 95% CI 0.22-0.74, p<.001) with higher effectiveness in trials of two weeks or less. The best-controlled non-seasonal trial randomised 122 adults with at least moderate major depressive disorder to 10,000-lux white light for 30 minutes each early morning, fluoxetine 20 mg, both, or double placebo for 8 weeks under double-blind sham-and-placebo conditions: mean MADRS improvement was 13.4 points (SD 7.5) for light, 16.9 (SD 9.2) for the combination, 8.8 (SD 9.9) for fluoxetine, and 6.5 (SD 9.6) for placebo, with light monotherapy (d=0.80, 95% CI 0.28-1.31) and combination (d=1.11, 95% CI 0.54-1.64) significantly superior to placebo while fluoxetine monotherapy was not (d=0.24, 95% CI -0.27 to 0.74). Wavelength matters: nine randomised trials of blue-light therapy (347 participants, seasonal and non-seasonal) found no evidence of efficacy versus inactive light conditions (mean difference 2.43, 95% CI -1.28 to 6.14, p=0.20), with the authors concluding blue light remains unproven. For preventing a seasonal episode in people with a history of one, a Cochrane review identified only a single eligible trial of 46 people: bright white light gave a risk ratio of 0.64 (95% CI 0.30-1.38) versus no light therapy and infrared 0.50 (95% CI 0.21-1.17), with evidence rated very low quality, no adverse event data reported, and no conclusions possible. In bipolar depression, 7 randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trials (259 patients) found adjunctive light therapy improved Hamilton depression scores (SMD 0.43, 95% CI 0.04-0.82, p=0.03) and clinical response (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.12-4.81) but not remission, with no increase in affective switch (OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.38-4.44). Effects do not generalise across all populations: pooled across trials in people living with dementia, light therapy showed no significant effect on depression (SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.80 to 0.40, I²=85%).

Doses studied: The best-controlled non-seasonal trial used a 10,000-lux fluorescent white light box for 30 minutes each day in the early morning over 8 weeks. The single seasonal-prevention trial used 2,500 lux delivered via visors. Bipolar trials used adjunctive light alongside existing medication. In a 15-trial synthesis of non-seasonal major depression, sessions of 60 minutes or more of daily exposure were associated with larger effects - as were trials lasting two weeks or less, which is a caution about the durability of the effect rather than an argument for short courses.

Why this grade: Randomised evidence exists and is positive for both seasonal and non-seasonal depression, including one well-conducted double-blind sham-controlled trial where light monotherapy beat placebo (d=0.80) while fluoxetine did not, and a 15-trial synthesis in non-seasonal depression (SMD 0.48). But the trial base is small and uneven: the foundational meta-analysis included only 13% of identified studies, blinding to a bright light box is intrinsically difficult, the Cochrane review of non-seasonal depression found the overall response advantage did not reach statistical significance with benefit confined to subgroups, adjunctive use alongside established antidepressants showed no effect at all, blue-light devices show no efficacy versus inactive light, evidence for preventing seasonal episodes rests on a single 46-person trial, and pooled effects were null in dementia. A documented harm - hypomania roughly five times more common than control, number needed to harm 8 - further argues against a higher grade. Positive but modest, uneven, and not risk-free: moderate.

Sources (8)
  1. Efficacy of Bright Light Treatment, Fluoxetine, and the Combination in Patients With Nonseasonal Major Depressive Disorder: A Randomized Clinical Trial.
    Lam, Levitt, Levitan, et al., 2016 · PMID 26580307 · positive

    122 adults aged 19-60 with non-seasonal major depressive disorder of at least moderate severity randomised to 10,000-lux white light 30 min/day early morning plus placebo pill, inactive negative ion generator plus fluoxetine 20 mg, combination, or double placebo for 8 weeks. Mean MADRS change: light 13.4 (SD 7.5), fluoxetine 8.8 (9.9), combination 16.9 (9.2), placebo 6.5 (9.6). Light monotherapy (d=0.80, 95% CI 0.28-1.31) and combination (d=1.11, 95% CI 0.54-1.64) were superior to placebo; fluoxetine monotherapy was not (d=0.24, 95% CI -0.27 to 0.74). Remission: 43.8% light, 58.6% combination, 19.4% fluoxetine, 30.0% placebo. All treatments generally well tolerated, with few significant differences in treatment-emergent adverse events.

  2. Light therapy for non-seasonal depression.
    Tuunainen, Kripke & Endo (Cochrane), 2004 · PMID 15106233 · mixed

    Twenty studies (49 reports) of bright light versus inactive placebo in non-seasonal depression; most applied light as an adjunct to drug therapy, sleep deprivation, or both. Treatment response in the bright light group was better than in the control group but did not reach statistical significance overall. Light was significantly better than control in high-quality studies (SMD -0.90, 95% CI -1.50 to -0.31), in studies applying morning light (SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.62 to -0.14), and in sleep-deprivation responders (SMD -1.02, 95% CI -1.60 to -0.45). Hypomania was more common in the bright light group than control (risk ratio 4.91, 95% CI 1.66 to 14.46; number needed to harm 8, 95% CI 5 to 20). Reporting quality was generally poor and many reports did not report adverse effects systematically.

  3. The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence.
    Golden, Gaynes, Ekstrom, et al., 2005 · PMID 15800134 · mixed

    Only 13% of identified studies met predefined methodological inclusion criteria. Bright light treatment in seasonal affective disorder gave effect size 0.84 (95% CI 0.60-1.08, 8 studies) and dawn simulation 0.73 (95% CI 0.37-1.08, 5 studies); bright light in non-seasonal depression gave 0.53 (95% CI 0.18-0.89, 3 studies). Bright light as an adjunct to antidepressant pharmacotherapy for non-seasonal depression was not effective (effect size -0.01, 95% CI -0.36 to 0.34, 5 studies). Authors emphasised that many reports of light therapy efficacy are not based on rigorous designs.

  4. Blue-Light Therapy for Seasonal and Non-Seasonal Depression: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Do, Li, Huang, et al., 2022 · PMID 35522196 · null

    Nine randomised controlled trials, 347 participants, of blue-light therapy in seasonal (7 studies) and non-seasonal (2 studies) major depressive disorder. Random-effects meta-analysis found no evidence of efficacy for blue light versus inactive light conditions on the primary depression score (mean difference 2.43, 95% CI -1.28 to 6.14, p=0.20), and no difference versus active light conditions (MD -0.11, 95% CI -2.38 to 2.16, p=0.93). No significant differences in response or remission rates versus either inactive or active comparators. Blue-light therapy was overall well tolerated. Authors concluded its efficacy remains unproven.

  5. Light therapy for preventing seasonal affective disorder.
    Nussbaumer, Kaminski-Hartenthaler, Forneris, et al. (Cochrane), 2015 · PMID 26558494 · null

    After screening 2,986 citations and assessing 91 full-text papers, only one randomised trial providing data on 46 people met eligibility criteria for preventive light therapy in adults with a history of seasonal affective disorder. Bright white light (2,500 lux via visors) reduced SAD incidence with risk ratio 0.64 (95% CI 0.30-1.38) and infrared 0.50 (95% CI 0.21-1.17) versus no light therapy - confidence intervals too broad to estimate effect. Quality of evidence for all outcomes was very low; investigators provided no information on adverse events. Authors concluded no conclusions on preventive light therapy could be drawn.

  6. The effect of bright light therapy on major depressive disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Tong, Dong, Lam & Lee, 2024 · PMID 39067131 · positive

    Fifteen randomised controlled trials published between 1996 and 2024, 883 patients with non-seasonal major depressive disorder. Bright light therapy showed positive effects on depressive symptoms versus control (SMD 0.48, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.74, p<.001) and a higher response rate at the end of the trial. Subgroup analyses found higher therapeutic effectiveness in trials lasting two weeks or less and in trials using 60 minutes or more of daily exposure.

  7. Light Therapy for Patients With Bipolar Depression: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Lam, Teng, Jung, et al., 2020 · PMID 31826657 · positive

    Seven randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trials, 259 patients with bipolar disorder. Adjunctive light therapy significantly improved Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores (SMD 0.43, 95% CI 0.04-0.82, p=0.03) and clinical response (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.12-4.81, p=0.024) but not remission. Treatment-emergent affective switches did not differ between active light and control (OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.38-4.44, p=0.67). Limitations: different light parameters, small samples, short durations, variable quality; authors describe the evidence as positive but non-conclusive.

  8. The Effects of Light Therapy on Sleep, Agitation and Depression in People With Dementia: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Fong, Ge, Ting, Wei & Cheung, 2023 · PMID 36924042 · null

    Eleven randomised controlled studies pooled from 16 eligible articles. Light therapy showed no significant effect on depression in people with dementia (6 comparisons; SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.80 to 0.40, I²=85%) and no significant effect on agitation (4 comparisons; SMD -0.28, 95% CI -1.02 to 0.45, I²=87%). It did significantly reduce the number of night-time awakenings (4 comparisons; SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.56 to -0.05, I²=0%). Authors concluded light therapy appears more effective for sleep disturbance than for depression, with long-term effects unclear.

For insomnia

Getting bright light in the morning — from a light box or the outdoors — helps set your body clock, and some studies suggest it can modestly improve sleep for people with insomnia, particularly time spent awake during the night. The research is still thin and mixed, though: the most careful trial in older adults found a bright light box worked no better than a dim one. It is a low-risk practice worth trying, but the evidence behind it is early-stage.

Safety

Commonly reported side effects of light-box use are mild and transient — headache, eye strain, and nausea. The 2023 meta-analysis reported no worsening of sleep measures with morning exposure in insomnia trials, though the memory-impaired population is a documented exception. Morning outdoor light is a free, essentially benign alternative for most healthy adults.

Who should be careful: People with bipolar disorder should use bright light only with clinical guidance, as light therapy can trigger hypomanic/manic symptoms. Those with retinal or other significant eye disease, or taking photosensitizing medications (e.g., certain antibiotics, retinoids, amiodarone), should consult a clinician or ophthalmologist first. Bright-light protocols are not established as beneficial in older adults with memory impairment — in a randomized trial, a morning protocol reduced their sleep (Friedman 2012, PMID 22406033).

Interactions: Photosensitizing drugs may increase risk of eye or skin reactions with intense light exposure. Timing matters — evening exposure can delay the body clock, and in a randomized trial in memory-impaired older adults a 30-minute morning bright-light protocol reduced actigraphy-measured total sleep time and sleep efficiency (Friedman 2012, PMID 22406033).

What the evidence says

Evidence for bright-light exposure in insomnia is preliminary and inconsistent. A meta-analysis of 53 light-therapy studies (n=1,154 across sleep problems) found a small-to-medium pooled effect for insomnia specifically (Hedges' g = 0.47), larger with higher light intensity, but with indication of publication bias. A 2023 meta-analysis restricted to insomnia disorder (22 studies, n=685; 13 meta-analyzed) found light therapy significantly improved wake after sleep onset (SMD -0.61 by actigraphy, weighted difference ~11 min; SMD -1.09 by diary, ~36 min) but showed no effect on sleep onset latency, total sleep time, or sleep efficiency; morning exposure advanced sleep-wake timing. The strongest negative evidence is a 12-week placebo-controlled RCT in 51 older adults with primary insomnia, in which ~4,000-lux scheduled bright light (morning or evening, 45 min/day) shifted circadian phase as expected but produced subjective and objective sleep changes no different from ~65-lux dim light. In addition, a 2-week randomized trial of 30-minute morning bright light (~4,200 lux) in older memory-impaired adults found the active light actually reduced actigraphy-measured total sleep time and sleep efficiency in the memory-impaired participants. Overall, human data are limited to small trials with heterogeneous protocols.

Doses studied: Protocols vary widely; the key verified trials used roughly 4,000-4,200 lux for 30-45 minutes daily, typically within 30-60 minutes of waking, for 2-12 weeks, and meta-analysis found larger insomnia effects at higher light intensities.

Why this grade: Human trials exist but are small and heterogeneous, and results conflict: one meta-analysis found small-to-medium benefit (g=0.47) with indications of publication bias; a more recent meta-analysis found improvement only in wake after sleep onset and none in sleep latency, total sleep time, or sleep efficiency; the largest dedicated RCT in older adults with insomnia found bright light no better than dim-light placebo; and one randomized trial in memory-impaired older adults found morning bright light reduced sleep. Torn between moderate and early, the lower grade applies.

Sources (4)
  1. The effects of light therapy on sleep problems: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    van Maanen et al., 2016 (Sleep Medicine Reviews) · PMID 26606319 · positive

    Across 53 studies (1,154 participants), light therapy was effective for sleep problems overall (g=0.39) and for insomnia specifically (g=0.47), with larger insomnia effects at higher light intensity — but effect sizes were mostly small-to-medium and there was indication of publication bias.

  2. Light therapy in insomnia disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Chambe et al., 2023 (Journal of Sleep Research) · PMID 37002704 · mixed

    In 22 studies of insomnia disorder (n=685; 13 meta-analyzed), light therapy improved wake after sleep onset (SMD -0.61 actigraphy, ~11 min; SMD -1.09 sleep diary, ~36 min) but showed NO significant effect on sleep onset latency, total sleep time, or sleep efficiency; morning exposure advanced sleep-wake rhythms. Heterogeneity and publication bias limited interpretation.

  3. Scheduled bright light for treatment of insomnia in older adults.
    Friedman et al., 2009 (Journal of the American Geriatrics Society) · PMID 19187411 · null

    In a 12-week single-blind placebo-controlled RCT (51 older adults with primary insomnia), 45 min/day of ~4,000-lux bright light (morning or evening) produced subjective and objective (actigraphy, polysomnography) sleep changes that were not significantly different from ~65-lux dim light, despite predictably shifting circadian phase — the authors concluded support was not found for bright-light treatment in this population.

  4. Brief morning light treatment for sleep/wake disturbances in older memory-impaired individuals and their caregivers.
    Friedman et al., 2012 (Sleep Medicine) · PMID 22406033 · negative

    In 54 caregiver/care-recipient dyads randomized to 2 weeks of 30-min daily morning bright white (~4,200 lux) versus dim red (~90 lux) light, actigraphy- and log-determined time in bed and total sleep time DECLINED in the memory-impaired care recipients under active bright light (p<0.05), with the total-sleep-time decrease accompanied by reduced sleep efficiency and increased wake after sleep onset (p<0.001); caregivers' sleep and mood improved in both conditions, independent of the light itself.
