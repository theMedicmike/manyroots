Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

Strong evidence · for anxiety & stress

For anxiety & stress

CBT is a structured, skills-based talk therapy where you learn to notice and change the thought and behavior patterns that keep worry going. Of all the non-drug options for anxiety it has the deepest research behind it, and in the largest head-to-head trial about 7 in 10 people responded to it. Two honest caveats: many of the underlying studies are of low quality and compared CBT against a waiting list rather than against another real treatment, and the benefit shrinks somewhat past a year.

Safety

CBT is drug-free and generally safe. The most common downsides are temporary increases in distress when confronting feared thoughts or situations, the time and cost commitment, and dropout: in the PTSD trials of the placebo-controlled meta-analysis, dropout was higher with CBT (29.0%) than placebo (17.2%), though no dropout difference was found across the other anxiety disorders. Quality depends heavily on the therapist and on fidelity to the protocol, and effects were stronger in completer samples than in intent-to-treat samples — which is another way of saying that the people who finish do better than the average person who starts.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications. Not a substitute for urgent psychiatric care in people with active suicidal ideation or psychosis, where it should be delivered only as part of comprehensive clinical management.

Interactions: No drug interactions. CBT is commonly combined with medication; decisions about combined treatment should be made with the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

Evidence for CBT in generalized anxiety and anxiety-related disorders is the deepest of any behavioral intervention, though not as clean as its reputation suggests. A meta-analysis of 41 randomized placebo-controlled trials (N=2,843) found a moderate pooled effect on target-disorder symptoms (Hedges' g = 0.56) and a response odds ratio of 2.97 versus placebo, with large effect sizes for GAD, OCD and acute stress disorder. In a 3-arm randomized trial of 226 adults with GAD, twelve weeks of CBT produced a 70.8% response rate versus 33.0% for an active stress-education control (OR 5.00, 95% CI 2.12-11.82; number needed to treat 2.62). A meta-analysis of 41 GAD psychotherapy studies (2,132 patients) found a large pooled effect across 38 comparisons from 28 studies (g = 0.84, 95% CI 0.71-0.97) and on depression (g = 0.71), but reported indications of publication bias and too few comparative trials to judge CBT against other therapies or medication. The countervailing evidence is substantial. Across 69 randomized trials (4,118 outpatients) that were mainly of low quality, effects for GAD versus control ran g = 0.07-0.40 through 12 months and only g = 0.22 (k=10) at 12 or more months. In older adults, CBT beat waitlist and treatment-as-usual on worry but showed only a small, non-significant advantage over active control conditions at end of treatment, with equivalent outcomes at follow-up. And a meta-analysis of 12 GAD trials (N=531) rated 8 of 12 as high risk of bias, judged the overall evidence 'moderate' and not yet robust, and found that psychological placebo alone reduced symptoms significantly more than placement on a waiting list — meaning waitlist-controlled effect sizes overstate what CBT itself contributes.

Doses studied: In the largest GAD trial cited here, CBT was delivered as twelve weekly 120-minute group sessions with 20 minutes of daily homework over 12 weeks. Formats vary widely across the meta-analysed trials; one placebo-controlled synthesis found larger effects for individual than group CBT in social anxiety disorder and PTSD studies, and larger effects in completer than intent-to-treat samples.

Why this grade: Multiple independent meta-analyses of randomized trials — including 41 placebo-controlled trials — show consistent benefit in generalized anxiety and related disorders, CBT beat an active stress-education control in a well-powered 3-arm GAD trial, and CBT is guideline first-line care. Graded strong despite substantial caveats that are stated rather than buried: the underlying trials are mainly rated low quality, most comparisons used waiting lists rather than active controls (and psychological placebo alone outperforms waitlist), the advantage over active comparators is small and non-significant in older adults, and the effect at 12-plus months is small.

Sources (6)
  1. Cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety and related disorders: A meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials.
    Carpenter JK, Andrews LA, Witcraft SM, et al. (2018), Depression and Anxiety · PMID 29451967 · positive

    Across 41 placebo-controlled RCTs (N=2,843), CBT produced moderate effects on target-disorder symptoms (Hedges' g = 0.56), small-to-moderate effects on other anxiety symptoms (g = 0.38) and depression (g = 0.31), and a response odds ratio of 2.97 versus placebo, with large effect sizes for OCD, GAD and acute stress disorder but only small-to-moderate effects for PTSD, social anxiety disorder and panic disorder.

  2. Efficacy of Yoga vs Cognitive Behavioral Therapy vs Stress Education for the Treatment of Generalized Anxiety Disorder: A Randomized Clinical Trial.
    Simon NM, Hofmann SG, Rosenfield D, et al. (2021), JAMA Psychiatry · PMID 32805013 · positive

    In 226 adults with GAD randomized to twelve 120-minute group sessions, CBT produced a 12-week response rate of 70.8% versus 33.0% for active stress education (OR 5.00, 95% CI 2.12-11.82; number needed to treat 2.62), and Kundalini yoga (54.2%) was not shown to be noninferior to CBT (difference 16.6%; P = .42 for noninferiority), supporting CBT as first-line.

  3. Psychological treatment of generalized anxiety disorder: a meta-analysis.
    Cuijpers P, Sijbrandij M, Koole S, et al. (2014), Clinical Psychology Review · PMID 24487344 · positive

    Across 41 studies (2,132 GAD patients, most examining CBT and most using waiting lists as the control), the pooled effect of 38 comparisons from 28 studies was large (g = 0.84, 95% CI 0.71-0.97), with a large effect on depression (g = 0.71); the authors reported some indications of publication bias and too few comparative trials to draw conclusions about CBT versus other psychotherapies or medication.

  4. Long-term Outcomes of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Anxiety-Related Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    van Dis EAM, van Veen SC, Hagenaars MA, et al. (2020), JAMA Psychiatry · PMID 31758858 · mixed

    Across 69 randomized trials (4,118 outpatients) that were mainly of low quality, CBT for GAD was associated with improved outcomes versus control conditions from post-treatment through 12 months (Hedges' g, 0.07-0.40), and the effect at 12 or more months of follow-up was small (g = 0.22, k = 10); relapse rates were reported in only 6 of the 69 trials.

  5. Efficacy of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Generalized Anxiety Disorder in Older Adults: Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and Meta-Regression.
    Hall J, Kellett S, Berrios R, et al. (2016), American Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry · PMID 27687212 · mixed

    In 14 RCTs (N=985) of outpatient older adults with GAD, CBT significantly outperformed waitlist or treatment-as-usual on worry at end of treatment and 6-month follow-up, but against active control conditions showed only a small, non-significant advantage at end of treatment and equivalent outcomes at follow-up; the authors concluded that superiority over other commonly available treatments remains to be tested.

  6. Comparison of psychological placebo and waiting list control conditions in the assessment of cognitive behavioral therapy for the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder: a meta-analysis.
    Zhu Z, Zhang L, Jiang J, et al. (2014), Shanghai Archives of Psychiatry · PMID 25642106 · mixed

    Across 12 GAD RCTs (combined N=531), CBT was more effective than either control, but 8 of the 12 studies were classified as high risk of bias, the overall level of evidence was graded only 'moderate' (results 'cannot, as yet, be considered robust'), and psychological placebo alone produced a significantly greater symptom reduction than placement on a waiting list.
