Minerals & nutrients

Glucosamine and chondroitin

Not supported by evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Glucosamine and chondroitin are among the best-selling joint supplements in the world, and they have been tested more thoroughly than almost anything else in this category. Across very large, independently funded trials, people taking them improved — but so did people taking placebo, by about the same amount. One large trial in people with chronic low back pain found no benefit at all. One well-known trial did find the combination worked about as well as an anti-inflammatory drug, but it had no dummy-pill group, which is the comparison that matters most. The honest summary is that the money spent on these has not been matched by results.

Safety

Tolerability is the strongest thing that can be said for these products. In GAIT, adverse events were described as mild, infrequent and evenly distributed across all treatment groups including celecoxib and placebo, and in the 2-year extension serious adverse events were rare across all arms. In the 2017 combination trial, severe adverse events were uncommon and equally distributed, and in MOVES adverse events were low and similarly distributed. The most common complaints are mild gastrointestinal upset. The realistic harm here is financial and the opportunity cost of using an ineffective product in place of exercise therapy or effective care.

Who should be careful: Most glucosamine is manufactured from shellfish (crustacean shells), so people with shellfish allergy should avoid it or seek a documented non-shellfish source. Chondroitin is typically bovine, porcine or shark-derived, which matters for people with religious, dietary or allergy restrictions. People with diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance should tell their clinician they are taking glucosamine and continue routine glucose monitoring.

Interactions: A case report plus FDA MedWatch and WHO pharmacovigilance data (Knudsen & Sokol 2008) describe increased INR, bleeding or bruising when glucosamine, with or without chondroitin, is taken by people on warfarin — 20 MedWatch reports and 21 WHO reports, one of them a fatal-outcome intracranial bleed. If you take warfarin or another anticoagulant, do not start these supplements without telling the clinician who manages your INR. Because these products are sold as supplements rather than drugs, content and purity vary between brands.

What the evidence says

The independent placebo-controlled randomised evidence does not support glucosamine or chondroitin for chronic musculoskeletal pain. In the NIH-funded GAIT trial (n=1,583, 24 weeks), the placebo response rate for a 20% reduction in knee pain was 60.1%; glucosamine added 3.9 percentage points (P=.30), chondroitin 5.3 (P=.17) and the combination 6.5 (P=.09), while active-comparator celecoxib added 10.0 (P=.008). The 2-year GAIT extension (n=662) found odds ratios versus placebo for 20% WOMAC pain reduction of 1.16 for glucosamine, 0.69 for chondroitin and 0.83 for the combination, none statistically significant and none reaching a clinically important difference. A Bayesian network meta-analysis of 10 trials in 3,803 patients found pain differences versus placebo of -0.4 cm for glucosamine, -0.3 cm for chondroitin and -0.5 cm for the combination on a 10 cm VAS, with no credible interval crossing the prespecified minimum clinically important difference of -0.9 cm. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 5 independent placebo-controlled trials (n=1,625) found no benefit at 3 or 24 months and no benefit in any subgroup defined by baseline pain, BMI, sex, structural abnormality or inflammation. In chronic low back pain with degenerative lumbar osteoarthritis (n=250), glucosamine 1,500 mg/day was no better than placebo on the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire at 6 months or 1 year (P=.72). The main result pointing the other way is MOVES (n=606), where chondroitin sulfate plus glucosamine hydrochloride was non-inferior to celecoxib for WOMAC pain at 6 months (adjusted mean change -185.7 vs -186.8; difference -1.11, 95% CI -22.0 to 19.8) — a comparison that included no placebo arm and therefore cannot show either treatment beat placebo.

Doses studied: Glucosamine 500 mg three times daily (1,500 mg/day) and chondroitin sulfate 400 mg three times daily (1,200 mg/day), alone or combined, for 6 to 24 months; glucosamine 1,500 mg/day for 6 months in the chronic low back pain trial; chondroitin sulfate 1,200 mg plus glucosamine sulfate 1,500 mg as a single daily dose for 6 months; chondroitin sulfate 400 mg plus glucosamine hydrochloride 500 mg three times daily for 6 months in MOVES.

Why this grade: This is the best-tested supplement in the whole field and the independent placebo-controlled evidence is negative. The NIH-funded GAIT trial (n=1,583) missed its primary endpoint for glucosamine, chondroitin and the combination, and its 2-year extension (n=662) found no treatment reached a clinically important difference. A network meta-analysis of 10 large trials (3,803 patients) found every estimate fell short of the prespecified minimum clinically important difference, and found that industry-independent trials produced smaller effects than commercially funded ones (P=0.02 for interaction). An individual-patient-data meta-analysis representing 55% of all participants in published placebo-controlled glucosamine RCTs found no benefit at 3 or 24 months and no benefit in any subgroup. A 2017 trial found the combination inferior to placebo in the modified intention-to-treat analysis. Critically for this node, the one adequately sized placebo-controlled trial in chronic low back pain with degenerative lumbar osteoarthritis was flatly null. The strongest counter-evidence, and it is reported here rather than omitted, is the MOVES trial (n=606), in which the combination matched celecoxib for pain relief at 6 months — but MOVES had no placebo arm, and in GAIT, which did, celecoxib beat placebo by only 10 percentage points, so 'as good as celecoxib' in an unblinded-to-placebo design is weak support. The widely cited moderate-to-severe pain subgroup finding from GAIT was an exploratory analysis that later, purpose-built trials did not replicate.

Sources (8)
  1. Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis.
    Clegg DO, Reda DJ, Harris CL, et al., 2006 (New England Journal of Medicine) · PMID 16495392 · null

    In the 1,583-patient GAIT trial, neither glucosamine 1,500 mg/day, chondroitin sulfate 1,200 mg/day nor the combination beat placebo for a 20% reduction in knee pain at 24 weeks: against a 60.1% placebo response, glucosamine was +3.9 points (P=.30), chondroitin +5.3 (P=.17), combination +6.5 (P=.09), while celecoxib was +10.0 (P=.008). An exploratory subgroup with moderate-to-severe baseline pain showed 79.2% vs 54.3% (P=.002) for combination therapy. Adverse events were mild, infrequent and evenly distributed among the groups.

  2. Clinical efficacy and safety of glucosamine, chondroitin sulphate, their combination, celecoxib or placebo taken to treat osteoarthritis of the knee: 2-year results from GAIT.
    Sawitzke AD, Shi H, Finco MF, et al., 2010 (Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases) · PMID 20525840 · null

    Over 24 months in 662 GAIT participants with radiographically confirmed knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren/Lawrence grade 2 or 3), odds of a 20% reduction in WOMAC pain versus placebo were 1.16 for glucosamine, 0.69 for chondroitin sulfate, 0.83 for the combination and 1.21 for celecoxib, none statistically significant. No treatment achieved a clinically important difference in WOMAC pain or function compared with placebo; adverse reactions were similar across groups and serious adverse events were rare.

  3. Effects of glucosamine, chondroitin, or placebo in patients with osteoarthritis of hip or knee: network meta-analysis.
    Wandel S, Jüni P, Tendal B, et al., 2010 (BMJ) · PMID 20847017 · negative

    Across 10 large trials in 3,803 patients with hip or knee osteoarthritis, differences in pain versus placebo on a 10 cm VAS were -0.4 cm (95% credible interval -0.7 to -0.1) for glucosamine, -0.3 cm (-0.7 to 0.0) for chondroitin and -0.5 cm (-0.9 to 0.0) for the combination; for none of these did the credible interval cross the prespecified minimum clinically important difference of -0.9 cm. Industry-independent trials showed smaller effects than commercially funded ones (P=0.02 for interaction), and differences in joint space narrowing were minute with intervals overlapping zero.

  4. Effect of glucosamine on pain-related disability in patients with chronic low back pain and degenerative lumbar osteoarthritis: a randomized controlled trial.
    Wilkens P, Scheel IB, Grundnes O, Hellum C, Storheim K, 2010 (JAMA) · PMID 20606148 · null

    In 250 patients with chronic low back pain of more than 6 months and degenerative lumbar osteoarthritis, 1,500 mg/day oral glucosamine for 6 months produced the same mean Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire score as placebo at 6 months (5.0 in both groups) and no significant difference in change at 1 year, with no difference in pain at rest (P=.91), pain during activity (P=.97) or quality of life (P=.20). This is the key back-pain-specific result and it is null.

  5. Combined Treatment With Chondroitin Sulfate and Glucosamine Sulfate Shows No Superiority Over Placebo for Reduction of Joint Pain and Functional Impairment in Patients With Knee Osteoarthritis: A Six-Month Multicenter, Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Clinical Trial.
    Roman-Blas JA, Castañeda S, Sánchez-Pernaute O, Largo R, Herrero-Beaumont G, 2017 (Arthritis & Rheumatology) · PMID 27477804 · negative

    In 164 patients with Kellgren/Lawrence grade 2-3 knee osteoarthritis and moderate-to-severe pain, chondroitin sulfate 1,200 mg plus glucosamine sulfate 1,500 mg daily for 6 months was inferior to placebo in the modified intention-to-treat population: global VAS pain fell -11.8 mm (19%) with the combination versus -20.5 mm (33%) with placebo, a peak between-group difference of 8.7 mm favouring placebo (P<0.03). No between-group difference was seen among per-protocol completers, and severe adverse events were uncommon and equally distributed.

  6. Subgroup analyses of the effectiveness of oral glucosamine for knee and hip osteoarthritis: a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis from the OA trial bank.
    Runhaar J, Rozendaal RM, van Middelkoop M, et al., 2017 (Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases) · PMID 28754801 · null

    Individual patient data from 5 independent placebo-controlled trials (n=1,625, representing 55% of all participants in published placebo-controlled glucosamine RCTs) showed glucosamine was no better than placebo for pain or function at 3 and 24 months, and no better in any predefined subgroup based on baseline pain severity, BMI, sex, structural abnormalities or presence of inflammation. Results were unchanged when restricted to knee osteoarthritis or stratified by glucosamine type.

  7. Combined chondroitin sulfate and glucosamine for painful knee osteoarthritis: a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, non-inferiority trial versus celecoxib.
    Hochberg MC, Martel-Pelletier J, Monfort J, et al., 2016 (Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases) · PMID 25589511 · mixed

    In 606 patients with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2-3 knee osteoarthritis and moderate-to-severe pain (MOVES), chondroitin sulfate 400 mg plus glucosamine hydrochloride 500 mg three times daily was non-inferior to celecoxib 200 mg daily at 6 months: adjusted mean WOMAC pain change -185.7 (95% CI -200.3 to -171.1, a 50.1% decrease) versus -186.8 (-201.7 to -171.9, 50.2%), difference -1.11 (95% CI -22.0 to 19.8, p=0.92) against a non-inferiority margin of -40, with 79.7% versus 79.2% meeting OMERACT-OARSI response criteria and similar adverse events. This trial had no placebo arm, so it cannot establish that either treatment outperforms placebo.

  8. Potential glucosamine-warfarin interaction resulting in increased international normalized ratio: case report and review of the literature and MedWatch database.
    Knudsen JF, Sokol GH, 2008 (Pharmacotherapy) · PMID 18363538 · negative

    A 71-year-old man stable on warfarin for 5 years had his INR rise from 2.3 to 3.9 and then 4.7 after increasing his glucosamine-chondroitin dose, returning to 2.6 after the supplement was stopped. An accompanying pharmacovigilance review identified 20 FDA MedWatch reports of altered coagulation (raised INR, bleeding or bruising) in warfarin-treated patients taking glucosamine or glucosamine-chondroitin — including one intraventricular bleed with subdural haematoma resulting in a persistent vegetative state — and 21 WHO database reports of increased INR with glucosamine, 17 of which resolved when glucosamine was stopped.
