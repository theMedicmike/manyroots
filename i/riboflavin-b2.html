Riboflavin (Vitamin B2)

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

High-dose vitamin B2 taken daily has been shown to reduce how often migraines happen, and it is one of the better-supported supplements for prevention. The catch is that most of the adult evidence traces back to one fairly small study, and the trials in children have come out both ways. It is very cheap, very well tolerated, and it turns your urine bright yellow — which is harmless.

Safety

Riboflavin is among the best-tolerated supplements studied for migraine. In the pivotal trial, three minor adverse events occurred across 55 patients over three months — diarrhea and polyuria on riboflavin, abdominal cramps on placebo — and none were serious. It reliably turns urine bright fluorescent yellow; this is harmless excess vitamin, not a warning sign, but it startles people who were not told. There is no established toxic dose, but safety at 400 mg/day beyond the 3-month trial windows has not been formally characterized.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications are established. Doses of 400 mg/day are roughly 300 times dietary intake and have not been studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so discuss with a clinician before use in those situations. Use in children should be clinician-supervised — two placebo-controlled pediatric trials found no benefit on migraine frequency. Bright yellow urine can interfere with the interpretation of some urine laboratory tests; tell the lab you are taking it.

Interactions: No clinically important drug interactions are well established at migraine-prevention doses. Some medications, including certain antipsychotics, tricyclic antidepressants and probenecid, can affect riboflavin status. As with magnesium, separating high-dose supplements from tetracycline antibiotics is a reasonable precaution.

What the evidence says

PREVENTIVE only — riboflavin has not been studied as an abortive treatment during an attack. Schoenen et al. randomized 55 adults to riboflavin 400 mg/day or placebo for 3 months and found riboflavin superior for attack frequency (p = 0.005) and headache days (p = 0.012), with 59% responders versus 15% on placebo (p = 0.002) and a number-needed-to-treat of 2.3. A 2021 meta-analysis of 9 studies (673 subjects) reported significant reductions in migraine days (p = 0.005, I² 89%), duration (p = 0.003, I² 0%), frequency (p = 0.001, I² 65%) and pain score (p = 0.015, I² 84%), and a 2026 dose-response meta-analysis of 12 trials (n = 749) found frequency reduced by a weighted mean difference of -1.39 (95% CI -2.52 to -0.25) with I² = 91.7% and a linear benefit up to 400 mg/day, while noting that some included studies lacked a control group. Contrary evidence: Maizels et al. found riboflavin 400 mg with magnesium and feverfew produced the same 50% response rate as a 'placebo' containing 25 mg riboflavin (42% versus 44%, p = 0.87); a 130-adult trial of a riboflavin-magnesium-CoQ10 combination did not significantly reduce migraine days versus placebo (6.2 to 4.4 versus 6.2 to 5.2, p = 0.23); and a 2019 systematic review restricted to placebo-controlled vitamin and mineral trials could not pool riboflavin at all because the data were not sufficiently reported. In children the picture is mixed: 200 mg/day produced a 50% response in 12/27 versus 14/21 on placebo (not significant, p = 0.125) and 50 mg/day did not reduce migraine attack frequency (p = 0.44), while a three-arm Iranian trial found high-dose riboflavin reduced attack frequency and duration versus placebo (both p < 0.001) but not intensity (p = 0.74), with no benefit at low dose. The AAN/AHS guideline classifies riboflavin as Level B, probably effective for episodic migraine prevention.

Doses studied: 400 mg/day as a single oral dose, taken daily for about 3 months — the pivotal adult trial ran 3 months, and dose-response analysis found benefit rising linearly up to 400 mg/day with no threshold below it. Pediatric placebo-controlled trials used 50 mg/day and 200 mg/day and were null for migraine frequency; a third pediatric trial reported benefit at a 'high dose' whose milligram value was not stated in its abstract.

Why this grade: The adult preventive evidence rests heavily on one small but clean placebo-controlled trial (Schoenen 1998, n = 55, NNT 2.3) plus two meta-analyses that pool positive results with severe statistical heterogeneity (I² up to 91.7%) and, by the 2026 authors' own admission, some included studies that had no control group. Against that, a trial using riboflavin 400 mg in combination showed no advantage over a 25 mg riboflavin comparator, a 130-adult combination trial did not significantly reduce migraine days versus placebo, and a 2019 systematic review could not pool riboflavin at all because the trials did not report data sufficiently. Pediatric results are mixed rather than uniformly null: two placebo-controlled trials at 50 mg and 200 mg found no effect on migraine frequency, while a third found benefit at high dose. AAN/AHS rates riboflavin Level B — probably effective, not first-line. Overall positive but heterogeneous and thinly replicated in adults, which is 'moderate', not 'strong'.

Sources (10)
  1. Effectiveness of high-dose riboflavin in migraine prophylaxis. A randomized controlled trial.
    Schoenen, Jacquy & Lenaerts, 1998 · PMID 9484373 · positive

    In 55 adults over 3 months, riboflavin 400 mg/day beat placebo for attack frequency (p = 0.005) and headache days (p = 0.012); 59% of riboflavin patients improved by at least 50% versus 15% on placebo (p = 0.002), number-needed-to-treat 2.3. Three minor adverse events occurred overall — diarrhea and polyuria on riboflavin, abdominal cramps on placebo — none serious.

  2. Effect of Vitamin B2 supplementation on migraine prophylaxis: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Chen et al., 2021 · PMID 33779525 · positive

    Nine studies (673 subjects, 8 RCTs plus 1 controlled clinical trial) pooled: riboflavin 400 mg/day for three months significantly reduced migraine days (p = 0.005, I² 89%), duration (p = 0.003, I² 0%), frequency (p = 0.001, I² 65%) and pain score (p = 0.015, I² 84%).

  3. The effect of riboflavin on the mean attack frequency, severity, and duration of migraine headaches: A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of clinical trials.
    Amini et al., 2026 · PMID 41769676 · positive

    Twelve trials (n = 749) showed riboflavin reduced attack frequency (weighted mean difference -1.39, 95% CI -2.52 to -0.25) and duration (-1.36, 95% CI -2.69 to -0.03), with a linear dose-response up to 400 mg/day; heterogeneity was very high (I² 91.7% and 90.4%) and the authors list the absence of a control group and small sample size in some included studies as limitations.

  4. A combination of riboflavin, magnesium, and feverfew for migraine prophylaxis: a randomized trial.
    Maizels, Blumenfeld & Burchette, 2004 · PMID 15447697 · null

    Riboflavin 400 mg plus magnesium 300 mg and feverfew 100 mg produced a 50%-or-greater migraine reduction in 10/24 (42%) versus 11/25 (44%) on a 'placebo' containing riboflavin 25 mg (p = 0.87); the authors concluded the 25 mg comparator may itself have been active and that evidence for these compounds is conflicting.

  5. Improvement of migraine symptoms with a proprietary supplement containing riboflavin, magnesium and Q10: a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, multicenter trial.
    Gaul, Diener & Danesch, 2015 · PMID 25916335 · mixed

    In 130 adults over 3 months, a fixed combination containing riboflavin reduced migraine days from 6.2 to 4.4 versus 6.2 to 5.2 on placebo — not significant (p = 0.23). Pain intensity (p = 0.03) and HIT-6 burden score (p = 0.01) improved significantly versus placebo.

  6. High-dose riboflavin for migraine prophylaxis in children: a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial.
    MacLennan et al., 2008 · PMID 18984840 · null

    In 48 children, riboflavin 200 mg/day produced a 50%-or-greater headache reduction in 12/27 versus 14/21 on placebo (not significant, p = 0.125), with no differences on any secondary outcome; the authors concluded riboflavin was not effective in this population and noted a high placebo response rate.

  7. Medium-dose riboflavin as a prophylactic agent in children with migraine: a preliminary placebo-controlled, randomised, double-blind, cross-over trial.
    Bruijn et al., 2010 · PMID 20974610 · null

    Crossover trial in 42 children aged 6-13 found no significant difference between riboflavin 50 mg/day and placebo in reduction of mean migraine attack frequency (p = 0.44); a significant reduction was seen only for headaches with a tension-type phenotype (p = 0.04). The authors concluded there is no evidence that 50 mg riboflavin prevents migraine attacks in children.

  8. Prophylactic effect of riboflavin on pediatric migraine: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.
    Talebian et al., 2018 · PMID 29629048 · mixed

    Ninety children allocated to placebo, low-dose or high-dose riboflavin for 12 weeks: the high-dose group had significant reductions in migraine frequency and mean duration versus placebo (both p < 0.001), but no significant reduction in intensity (p = 0.74); the low-dose group showed no significant benefit on frequency (p = 0.49) or duration (p = 0.69). A single-centre trial in a low-profile journal, so it carries less weight than the two negative pediatric trials.

  9. Vitamins and Minerals for Migraine Prophylaxis: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Okoli et al., 2019 · PMID 30764890 · null

    Of 18 eligible placebo-controlled trials, only coenzyme Q10 and magnesium could be meta-analysed; riboflavin and the other vitamins could not be pooled 'due to a lack of sufficiently reported data', and the authors concluded high-quality adequately powered RCTs are still needed.

  10. Evidence-based guideline update: NSAIDs and other complementary treatments for episodic migraine prevention in adults.
    Holland et al. (AAN/AHS), 2012 · PMID 22529203 · positive

    Guideline panel classified riboflavin as Level B — probably effective for episodic migraine prevention — a tier below Petasites/butterbur (Level A) and above coenzyme Q10 (Level C).
