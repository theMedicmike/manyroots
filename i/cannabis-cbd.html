Cannabis and CBD

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Cannabis and CBD are among the most talked-about options for long-term pain, and the honest answer is that the research is much weaker than the marketing. Pooling many kinds of chronic pain, the average benefit is small — about half a point on a ten-point scale — and most of that research was not done in people with arthritis or back pain. In the studies that were, the results were flat: a good trial of CBD on its own in people with hand arthritis found no difference from a dummy capsule, and a trial of the THC ingredient in people with knee arthritis found no pain relief either. There are also real downsides: dizziness, drowsiness, driving risk, and mental health risks for some people, and the legal situation depends entirely on where you live.

Safety

The most consistent trial harms are dizziness (risk difference up to 28% in trials lasting 3 months or more), drowsiness, dry mouth, nausea, vomiting, impaired attention and transient cognitive impairment; central nervous system adverse events were about three times more likely than on placebo and psychological effects about three times more likely. Do not drive or operate machinery while impaired — observational evidence links cannabis use to a 27% higher motor vehicle crash rate. Regular use can lead to cannabis use disorder and to a withdrawal syndrome on stopping. Smoked and vaporised cannabis has barely been studied in these trials (30 of 32 trials in the main meta-analysis were oral), so the trial evidence above does not describe it. Over-the-counter CBD products are poorly standardised: actual CBD content often differs from the label and some contain THC, which matters for anyone subject to workplace or federal drug testing. Legal status varies enormously by jurisdiction — cannabis remains a federally controlled substance in the United States while many states permit medical or adult use, hemp-derived CBD is regulated differently again, and rules differ country to country. Check your own jurisdiction and, if you are a veteran, federal employee, or in a safety-sensitive role, check your employer's and your clinic's policy before starting.

Who should be careful: Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding — convincing observational evidence links cannabis use to small-for-gestational-age (1.61) and low birth weight (1.43). Avoid in adolescents and young adults, whose brains are still developing. Avoid in anyone with a personal or family history of psychosis, schizophrenia, or bipolar disorder, or who has had psychotic symptoms — cannabis use is associated with roughly 71% higher odds of psychosis in the general population, and worsened positive psychotic symptoms at GRADE-high certainty. Use great caution with unstable cardiovascular disease, a history of substance use disorder, or significant liver disease. Do not use before driving, operating machinery, or any safety-sensitive work.

Interactions: CBD inhibits several liver cytochrome P450 enzymes and can raise blood levels of other medicines; clinically important interactions have been documented with clobazam and valproate (liver enzyme elevations) and with warfarin (raised INR and bleeding risk). Additive sedation and dizziness occur with opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, muscle relaxants, sedating antihistamines and alcohol — a real fall risk in older adults with osteoarthritis, and the knee-osteoarthritis trial above found that adding dronabinol to hydromorphone produced more moderate adverse events without adding analgesia. THC-containing products can compound the cognitive effects of these same drugs. Tell your prescriber and pharmacist before starting, and do not stop a prescribed pain medicine in order to substitute cannabis without medical supervision.

What the evidence says

The largest synthesis (32 RCTs, 5174 adults, 29 placebo-controlled, 30 oral and 2 topical, 28 in chronic non-cancer pain and 4 in cancer-related pain) found that non-inhaled medical cannabis or cannabinoids produce a small improvement in pain versus placebo: weighted mean difference -0.50 cm on a 10 cm visual analogue scale (95% CI -0.75 to -0.25, moderate certainty), a modelled 10% absolute increase (95% CI 5% to 15%) in the proportion reaching the 1 cm minimally important difference, alongside a very small functional gain (1.67 points on the 100-point SF-36 physical functioning scale, high certainty), a small sleep-quality gain (WMD -0.35 cm, 6% modelled RD, high certainty), and no improvement in emotional, role, or social functioning; harms scaled with duration, with dizziness risk difference 9% in trials under 3 months and 28% at 3 months or more. An independent review published in the Pain task-force supplement covering 36 studies and 7217 participants reached a more sceptical conclusion — all studies at high or uncertain risk of bias, benefit found only for cannabis under 7 days (RD 0.33, 2 trials, 231 patients) and nabiximols beyond 7 days (RD 0.06, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.12, 6 trials, 1484 patients), 81% of subgroup analyses negative, and evidence that neither supports nor refutes claims of efficacy and safety. Within musculoskeletal pain the record is thin and null: a systematic review to April 2015 of cannabinoids in chronic pain from rheumatic disease found only 4 eligible RCTs (71 fibromyalgia, 30 chronic spinal pain, 58 rheumatoid arthritis patients), identified no randomised trial in osteoarthritis at all, judged 3 of 4 at high risk of bias, and concluded there is insufficient evidence to recommend any cannabinoid preparation. Two adequate musculoskeletal trials have since reported and both were null: synthetic CBD 20-30 mg daily for 12 weeks in 136 randomised patients (129 analysed) with hand osteoarthritis or psoriatic arthritis gave a between-group pain difference of 0.23 mm on a 100 mm scale (95% CI -9.41 to 9.90, P=0.96) with response rates of 22% versus 21% and no effect on sleep, anxiety, depression or pain catastrophising; and in 37 adults with knee osteoarthritis, dronabinol 10 mg produced no significant analgesic effect on clinical pain severity or physical functioning, alone or added to hydromorphone. For chronic widespread pain, Cochrane found only 2 trials (72 participants) of nabilone 1 mg at bedtime in fibromyalgia, all very low quality, with a third-tier signal of greater pain reduction than placebo in one study but more withdrawals for adverse events on nabilone (4/52) than on placebo (1/20) or amitriptyline (0/32), and concluded there is no convincing, unbiased, high-quality evidence of value. An umbrella review of 101 meta-analyses recorded a GRADE-high 30% pain-reduction benefit (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.93) across 7 mixed conditions and concluded cannabis-based medicines are effective in chronic pain, but alongside GRADE-high increases in central nervous system adverse events (OR 2.84), psychological effects (OR 3.07) and vision effects (OR 3.00), and convincing observational associations with motor vehicle crash (1.27), psychosis in the general population (1.71), small-for-gestational-age (1.61) and low birth weight (1.43).

Doses studied: Trials have used oral or oromucosal preparations far more than smoked or vaporised cannabis: 30 of 32 trials in the largest meta-analysis were oral and 2 topical. Regimens with musculoskeletal relevance include synthetic CBD 20-30 mg daily for 12 weeks (hand osteoarthritis and psoriatic arthritis, null), dronabinol 10 mg alone or with hydromorphone 4 mg (knee osteoarthritis, null), and nabilone 1 mg at bedtime for 4-6 weeks (fibromyalgia). THC/CBD oromucosal spray (nabiximols) has been studied mainly in neuropathic pain and multiple sclerosis. No standard dose has been established for osteoarthritis or chronic low back pain, and over-the-counter CBD products are unstandardised.

Why this grade: The cannabinoid trial record for chronic pain is large and the average benefit is real but small, which is what 'moderate' describes. The best synthesis (32 trials, 5174 patients) found a 0.50 cm improvement on a 10 cm scale at moderate certainty, roughly a 10% absolute increase in the proportion of patients reaching the minimally important difference, plus high-certainty but very small gains in physical function and sleep and no gain in emotional, role or social functioning. An independent review of 36 trials in 7217 participants reached a more sceptical verdict — every study at high or uncertain risk of bias, 81% of subgroup analyses negative, evidence that 'neither supports nor refutes' efficacy — and an umbrella review of 101 meta-analyses recorded a GRADE-high 30% pain-reduction benefit alongside GRADE-high increases in central nervous system, psychological and visual adverse events. That combination is the definition of moderate: real randomised and meta-analytic evidence, overall positive, modest and mixed. The condition-specific picture is weaker and must not be overstated: a systematic review of the rheumatic-disease literature found no randomised trial in osteoarthritis at all and only 30 patients' worth of chronic spinal pain data; the two adequate musculoskeletal trials since are both null, one of CBD 20-30 mg daily in hand osteoarthritis and psoriatic arthritis and one of dronabinol 10 mg in knee osteoarthritis; and Cochrane found no convincing evidence for cannabinoids in fibromyalgia from 2 trials and 72 participants. 'Strong' is excluded by the small effect size and the harms; 'early' would misdescribe dozens of randomised trials.

Sources (8)
  1. Medical cannabis or cannabinoids for chronic non-cancer and cancer related pain: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised clinical trials.
    Wang L, Hong PJ, May C, et al. (BMJ), 2021 · PMID 34497047 · positive

    Across 32 trials in 5174 adults (29 placebo-controlled; 30 oral, 2 topical), non-inhaled medical cannabis produced a small pain benefit versus placebo: WMD -0.50 cm on a 10 cm VAS (95% CI -0.75 to -0.25, moderate certainty), a modelled 10% (95% CI 5% to 15%) absolute increase in patients reaching the 1 cm minimally important difference. Physical functioning improved by only 1.67 points on a 100-point scale and sleep quality by 0.35 cm (both high certainty), emotional, role and social functioning did not improve at all, and dizziness risk difference was 9% in trials under 3 months versus 28% at 3 months or longer.

  2. Cannabinoids, cannabis, and cannabis-based medicine for pain management: a systematic review of randomised controlled trials.
    Fisher E, Moore RA, Fogarty AE, et al. (Pain), 2021 · PMID 32804836 · null

    Across 36 studies and 7217 participants, all with high or uncertain risk of bias and GRADE low or very low quality, benefit was found only for cannabis under 7 days (RD 0.33, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.46, 2 trials, 231 patients) and nabiximols beyond 7 days (RD 0.06, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.12, 6 trials, 1484 patients); 81% of subgroup analyses were negative and cannabis, nabiximols and THC all produced more adverse events than control. The authors concluded the evidence neither supports nor refutes claims of efficacy and safety for cannabinoids, cannabis, or cannabis-based medicine in pain management.

  3. Efficacy, tolerability and safety of cannabinoids in chronic pain associated with rheumatic diseases (fibromyalgia syndrome, back pain, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis): A systematic review of randomized controlled trials.
    Fitzcharles MA, Baerwald C, Ablin J, Häuser W, 2016 · PMID 26767993 · null

    Searching to April 2015, only 4 randomised controlled trials qualified across all rheumatic chronic pain conditions: two nabilone trials in 71 fibromyalgia patients, one 4-week nabilone trial in 30 chronic spinal pain patients, and one 5-week THC/CBD trial in 58 rheumatoid arthritis patients. No RCT in osteoarthritis patients was found at all, risk of bias was high for 3 of the 4 studies, and findings of superiority over placebo or amitriptyline were not consistent. The authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to recommend any cannabinoid preparation for symptom management in chronic pain associated with rheumatic diseases.

  4. Cannabidiol treatment in hand osteoarthritis and psoriatic arthritis: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.
    Vela J, Dreyer L, Petersen KK, Arendt-Nielsen L, Duch KS, Kristensen S, 2022 · PMID 34510141 · null

    In 136 randomised patients (129 analysed) with hand osteoarthritis or psoriatic arthritis and moderate pain despite therapy, synthetic CBD 20-30 mg daily for 12 weeks produced a between-group pain difference of 0.23 mm on a 0-100 mm scale (95% CI -9.41 to 9.90, P=0.96). Response rates were essentially identical (22% CBD versus 21% placebo achieving more than 30 mm reduction), with no statistically significant effect on sleep quality, anxiety, depression, or pain catastrophising.

  5. Within-subject, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled evaluation of combining the cannabinoid dronabinol and the opioid hydromorphone in adults with chronic pain.
    Campbell CM, Mun CJ, Hamilton KR, Bergeria CL, Huhn AS, Speed TJ, Vandrey R, Dunn KE, 2023 · PMID 37202479 · null

    In 37 adults (65% women, mean age 62) with knee osteoarthritis of at least 3/10 average pain intensity, receiving placebo-placebo, hydromorphone 4 mg, dronabinol 10 mg, and the combination, no significant analgesic effects were observed for clinical pain severity or physical functioning across all drug conditions, and dronabinol added little to hydromorphone on evoked pain measures. The combination produced more moderate adverse events than either placebo or hydromorphone alone, and subjective drug effects and some human abuse potential ratings were increased.

  6. Cannabinoids for fibromyalgia.
    Walitt B, Klose P, Fitzcharles MA, Phillips T, Häuser W (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2016 · PMID 27428009 · null

    Only 2 trials with 72 participants total met inclusion criteria, both using nabilone 1 mg at bedtime for 4-6 weeks, with all outcomes rated very low quality. No study reported the proportion achieving 30% or 50% pain relief; third-tier evidence indicated greater pain reduction than placebo in one study and better sleep than amitriptyline in the other, but withdrawals for adverse events were higher on nabilone (4/52) than placebo (1/20) or amitriptyline (0/32). The review found no convincing, unbiased, high-quality evidence that nabilone is of value in fibromyalgia, and no eligible trial of herbal cannabis or other cannabinoids.

  7. Balancing risks and benefits of cannabis use: umbrella review of meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials and observational studies.
    Solmi M, De Toffol M, Kim JY, et al. (BMJ), 2023 · PMID 37648266 · mixed

    Across 101 meta-analyses, cannabis-based medicines reduced pain by 30% across 7 conditions (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.93, GRADE high) but increased psychological distress, and the authors concluded cannabis-based medicines are effective in chronic pain 'but not without adverse events'. GRADE-high harms included central nervous system adverse events (OR 2.84, 95% CI 2.16 to 3.73), psychological effects (OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.79 to 5.26) and vision effects (OR 3.00, 95% CI 1.79 to 5.03); convincing observational evidence linked cannabis to motor vehicle crash (1.27, 95% CI 1.21 to 1.34), psychosis in the general population (1.71, 95% CI 1.47 to 2.00), and small-for-gestational-age (1.61) and low birth weight (1.43) in pregnancy.

  8. Cannabis-based medicines for chronic neuropathic pain in adults.
    Mücke M, Phillips T, Radbruch L, Petzke F, Häuser W (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews), 2018 · PMID 29513392 · mixed

    In chronic neuropathic pain (16 studies, 1750 participants) — a different pain population from osteoarthritis or back pain, and not transferable to them — cannabis-based medicines may increase 50% pain relief from 17% to 21% (RD 0.05, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.09, NNTB 20, low quality). Withdrawals for adverse events were 10% versus 5% on placebo (NNTH 25), nervous system adverse events 61% versus 29% (NNTH 3) and psychiatric disorders 17% versus 5% (NNTH 10); the authors concluded potential benefits might be outweighed by potential harms.
