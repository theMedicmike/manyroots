Conventional medicine

Low-dose doxepin (3-6 mg)

Moderate evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Doxepin is an old antidepressant that, at a very small dose of 3 or 6 mg, acts mainly as an antihistamine and is FDA-approved specifically for insomnia. The trials are consistent on the main point: it helps people stay asleep and wake less in the second half of the night, adding roughly 20-30 minutes of sleep, without a next-day hangover and without rebound insomnia when it is stopped. Whether it also helps you fall asleep faster is less clear - some trials found a small effect and others found none - so it is best thought of as a gentle option for staying asleep rather than a powerful one for getting to sleep.

Safety

At 3-6 mg the trials reported an adverse-event profile comparable to placebo, with no spontaneous reports of anticholinergic effects, memory impairment, complex sleep behaviours, weight gain or increased appetite, no significant next-day residual sedation, and no rebound insomnia or withdrawal on stopping - findings replicated in the 12-week elderly trial. The dose distinction is the critical safety point: doxepin at antidepressant doses (tens to hundreds of mg) is strongly anticholinergic and sedating and carries a very different risk profile, so the low-dose insomnia tablet is not interchangeable with generic doxepin capsules or oral solution. It is an antidepressant class member and carries the standard warning about emergent suicidal thinking in younger adults. Not a controlled substance.

Who should be careful: Untreated narrow-angle glaucoma; severe urinary retention; MAOI use within 14 days. Caution in adults with a history of sleep apnea, since sedatives can worsen breathing during sleep, and in older adults at risk of falls or with cognitive impairment.

Interactions: Concomitant MAOIs must be avoided. Cimetidine and CYP2D6/CYP2C19 inhibitors (e.g. fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline) can raise doxepin levels and doses may need reducing. Additive sedation with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, z-drugs and sedating antihistamines; alcohol should be avoided. Caution with other anticholinergic medicines, since anticholinergic burden is cumulative even when each individual dose is low.

What the evidence says

Low-dose doxepin (3-6 mg) is FDA-approved for insomnia, works through histamine H1 antagonism rather than a GABA mechanism, and is not a controlled substance. A 35-day sleep-laboratory RCT randomising 221 adults with chronic primary insomnia to doxepin 3 mg, 6 mg or placebo found significant improvement in wake after sleep onset at nights 1, 15 and 29 for both doses, improvement in total sleep time, no significant next-day residual effects, no anticholinergic complaints or memory impairment, and no rebound insomnia after discontinuation. The longest trial - 12 weeks in 240 older adults - found doxepin 3 mg improved wake after sleep onset (the primary endpoint), total sleep time and sleep efficiency, sustained to night 85. A network meta-analysis of 24 RCTs in 5,917 older adults ranked low-dose doxepin the optimal agent for subjective total sleep time (+28.19 min versus placebo) and for objective sleep efficiency (+6.08%). The sleep-onset picture is genuinely mixed and should not be overstated in either direction: the 35-day trial found significant improvement in latency to persistent sleep on night 1 (3 mg P=0.0047; 6 mg P=0.0007) and the 12-week elderly trial found significant patient-reported onset improvement at 3 mg, but a pooled analysis of two phase 3 trials (310 patients) put the polysomnographic effect at only 6.4 min on night 1 (risk ratio 0.78, 95% CI 0.64-0.94) with a non-significant 12% change in patient-reported sleep-onset latency (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.73-1.05), and a 4-week outpatient trial of 6 mg in 254 older adults found no significant difference in latency to sleep onset at all. Cochrane rated the tricyclic evidence (five of six trials used doxepin) moderate quality for subjective sleep quality (SMD -0.39, 95% CI -0.56 to -0.21; 4 studies, 518 participants), sleep efficiency (+6.29 percentage points) and total sleep time (+22.88 min), with little or no effect on sleep latency (MD -4.27 min, 95% CI -9.01 to 0.48). The AASM suggests doxepin for sleep-maintenance insomnia but only as a WEAK recommendation; the 2023 European guideline permits low-dose sedating antidepressants for short-term use at level B behind first-line CBT-I; and the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 170 trials found doxepin well tolerated while stating that data on efficacy and other important outcomes were scarce and did not allow firm conclusions.

Doses studied: 3 mg and 6 mg at bedtime, the FDA-approved insomnia doses. The 35-day laboratory RCT used 3 mg or 6 mg nightly for five weeks in adults; a 12-week trial in older adults used 1 mg or 3 mg nightly; a 4-week outpatient trial in older adults used 6 mg nightly; and 1 mg, 3 mg and 6 mg were compared against placebo in two-night crossover polysomnography studies in adults and in older adults. These are 10- to 100-fold lower than antidepressant doxepin dosing.

Why this grade: Graded moderate rather than strong. Multiple placebo-controlled polysomnographic RCTs show a consistent sleep-maintenance benefit, it is FDA-approved for insomnia, and a network meta-analysis in 5,917 older adults ranked it best for subjective total sleep time and objective sleep efficiency. But the magnitude is modest (roughly 20-30 min of additional sleep); the sleep-onset effect is inconsistent across trials and small where present (the pooled phase 3 estimate was 6.4 min, which its own authors said did not reach the clinical significance threshold, and a 4-week trial of 6 mg in older adults found no sleep-onset difference at all); the AASM recommendation is WEAK and limited to sleep maintenance; the 2023 European guideline supports low-dose sedating antidepressants only at level B and only for short-term use; and the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis judged the efficacy data too scarce to allow firm conclusions. Held at moderate on the conservative rule.

Sources (9)
  1. Efficacy and safety of doxepin 3 and 6 mg in a 35-day sleep laboratory trial in adults with chronic primary insomnia.
    Randomised trialKrystal et al., 2011 · PMID 21966075 · positive

    221 adults randomised to doxepin 3 mg (n=75), 6 mg (n=73) or placebo (n=73) for 35 nights: significant improvement in wake after sleep onset at nights 1, 15 and 29 for both doses (3 mg P=0.0025 and 6 mg P=0.0009 at N15), improved total sleep time, and significant improvement in latency to persistent sleep on night 1 (3 mg P=0.0047; 6 mg P=0.0007). No significant next-day residual effects, no spontaneous reports of anticholinergic effects, memory impairment, complex sleep behaviours, weight gain or increased appetite, and no rebound insomnia after discontinuation.

  2. Efficacy and Safety of Doxepin 1 mg and 3 mg in a 12-week Sleep Laboratory and Outpatient Trial of Elderly Subjects with Chronic Primary Insomnia.
    Randomised trialKrystal et al., 2010 · PMID 21102997 · positive

    240 older adults randomised to doxepin 1 mg (n=77), 3 mg (n=82) or placebo (n=81) for 12 weeks - the longest placebo-controlled double-blind polysomnographic trial of nightly insomnia pharmacotherapy in this age group. Doxepin 3 mg significantly improved wake after sleep onset (primary endpoint, P<0.0001 on night 1), total sleep time, overall sleep efficiency and sleep efficiency in the last quarter of the night, sustained at night 85. It also significantly improved patient-reported latency to sleep onset at weeks 1, 4 and 12. No significant next-day residual effects and no reports of memory impairment or anticholinergic effects.

  3. Comparative efficacy and safety of hypnotics for insomnia in older adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisChiu et al., 2021 · PMID 33249496 · positive

    24 articles, 5,917 older adults: low-dose doxepin ranked the optimal therapy for prolonging subjective total sleep time (+28.19 min vs placebo) and for increasing objective sleep efficiency (+6.08%); eszopiclone ranked best for objective total sleep time (+26.69 min). The authors concluded that once study quality and the adverse effects of benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepines are weighed, low-dose doxepin appears to be the optimal pharmacotherapy for total sleep time and sleep efficiency in this age group.

  4. The effect of doxepin 3 mg on sleep latency: a pooled analysis of two phase 3 trials.
    Randomised trialFisher et al., 2025 · PMID 40229607 · mixed

    310 patients (157 doxepin 3 mg, 153 placebo): a statistically significant but small 22% improvement in latency to persistent sleep on night 1 (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.64-0.94), equal to a 6.4-min reduction overall (11 min in the subgroup with baseline latency >35 min), and a non-significant 12% reduction in patient-reported sleep-onset latency (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.73-1.05). The authors concluded the effect 'did not reach the clinical significance threshold'.

  5. Efficacy and safety of doxepin 6 mg in a four-week outpatient trial of elderly adults with chronic primary insomnia.
    Randomised trialLankford et al., 2012 · PMID 22197474 · mixed

    254 older adults randomised to doxepin 6 mg (n=130) or placebo (n=124) for four weeks: significantly more subjective total sleep time and less subjective wake after sleep onset at week 1 (both p<0.0001), sustained at weeks 2-4 (all p<0.05), plus improved sleep quality and Insomnia Severity Index scores. Critically, there were NO significant differences in latency to sleep onset for doxepin 6 mg versus placebo. No reports of anticholinergic effects or memory impairment.

  6. Antidepressants for insomnia in adults.
    Meta-analysisEveritt et al., 2018 · PMID 29761479 · mixed

    Cochrane review: tricyclics (five of six trials used doxepin) gave a moderate improvement in subjective sleep quality versus placebo (SMD -0.39, 95% CI -0.56 to -0.21; 4 studies, 518 participants, moderate-quality evidence), sleep efficiency +6.29 percentage points and total sleep time +22.88 min, but there may have been little or no impact on sleep latency (MD -4.27 min, 95% CI -9.01 to 0.48). Little or no difference in adverse events versus placebo (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.86-1.21).

  7. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisDe Crescenzo et al., 2022 · PMID 35843245 · mixed

    Across 170 trials and 47,950 participants, benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, zolpidem and zopiclone were worse than placebo, doxepin, seltorexant and zaleplon for the number of individuals with side-effects at study endpoint (OR range 1.27-2.78), but the authors stated that for doxepin, seltorexant and zaleplon 'data on efficacy and other important outcomes were scarce and do not allow firm conclusions'.

  8. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    GuidelineSateia et al., 2017 · PMID 27998379 · positive

    'We suggest that clinicians use doxepin as a treatment for sleep maintenance insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults. (WEAK)'. The recommendation is for sleep maintenance only, is graded WEAK, and the task force noted that downgrading the quality of evidence was predictable across all these agents given the funding source for most trials, the small number of eligible trials per drug, and observed heterogeneity.

  9. The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.
    GuidelineRiemann et al., 2023 · PMID 38016484 · mixed

    Current European guideline: CBT-I is first-line for chronic insomnia in adults of any age (A), and when it is not sufficiently effective 'low-dose sedating antidepressants (B) can be used for the short-term treatment of insomnia (<= 4 weeks)', with longer-term treatment initiated in some cases considering advantages and disadvantages (B). Support for this drug class is therefore level B and short-term by default - weaker than the level A given to benzodiazepine receptor agonists, daridorexant and orexin receptor antagonists.
