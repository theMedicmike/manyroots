Ginger (Zingiber officinale)

Early evidence · for gut & digestion Early evidence · for migraine & headache

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For gut & digestion

Ginger is a long-standing home remedy for an unsettled stomach, and it is one of the cheapest and best-tolerated things people try. But the one proper trial in people with IBS found it worked no better than a dummy capsule — if anything slightly worse — and a second placebo-controlled trial found no change in bowel symptoms either. The honest summary is that ginger has not been shown to help IBS. The trials were small, so it is not the final word, but there is nothing positive here to point to.

Safety

Ginger is one of the better-tolerated supplements studied in this area. In the placebo-controlled IBS trial, side effects were mild and actually less common on ginger (16.7%) than on placebo (35.7%), with no serious adverse events, and the 51-person healthy-adult trial reported no moderate or severe adverse events over 14 days. The most commonly reported complaints at supplement doses are heartburn, belching and a burning aftertaste; because ginger speeds gastric emptying, people with reflux may find it worsens heartburn. Culinary amounts in food are not a concern. Doses tested were 1-2 g daily for 4 weeks and 1.2 g daily for 2 weeks, so longer-term safety at supplement doses has not been studied in this population.

Who should be careful: Stop ginger supplements at least a week before planned surgery or any procedure with bleeding risk. Avoid supplement-strength ginger if you have a bleeding disorder, low platelets, or are being investigated for unexplained bleeding. People with gallstones or bile duct disease should ask a clinician first. In pregnancy, ginger is widely used for nausea, but supplement doses should be discussed with a midwife or doctor rather than self-selected. New or alarm symptoms — weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, onset after 50 — need medical assessment rather than a supplement.

Interactions: The clearest documented concern is warfarin: a review of case reports lists Zingiber officinale among herbs associated with serious bleeding events including postoperative bleeding, haematemesis, melaena and intracranial haemorrhage during concomitant use. By extension, use caution with other anticoagulants and with antiplatelet drugs including aspirin and clopidogrel. Because ginger affects gastric emptying, it could in theory alter the absorption rate of other oral medicines; separate doses if you notice a change. Check with a pharmacist if you take diabetes or blood pressure medication.

What the evidence says

The only dedicated placebo-controlled trial of ginger in IBS randomised 45 patients at the University of North Carolina to placebo, 1 g ginger daily or 2 g ginger daily for 28 days; responders (at least a 25% reduction in IBS symptom severity) were 57.1% on placebo, 46.7% on 1 g and 33.3% on 2 g, adequate relief was reported by 53.3% on placebo and 53.3% across both ginger arms combined, and the authors concluded ginger was well tolerated but did not perform better than placebo. A second double-blind placebo-controlled trial gave 1.2 g/day standardised ginger root powder for 14 days to 51 healthy adults and found no difference from placebo in bowel function, gastrointestinal symptoms, depression, anxiety, stress, fatigue or quality of life, and no change in microbial alpha or beta diversity, although some shifts in bacterial abundance occurred and one indigestion measure improved (P=0.015). A randomised double-blind crossover study in 11 patients with functional dyspepsia — a different condition from IBS — showed 1.2 g ginger accelerated gastric half-emptying to a median 12.3 minutes (range 8.5-17.0) versus 16.1 minutes (8.3-22.6) on placebo (P<=0.05), with a trend toward more antral contractions (P=0.06), but no effect on gastrointestinal symptoms and no change in GLP-1, motilin or ghrelin. A prospective Iraqi trial in 40 IBS patients used ginger inside a three-herb powder with Mentha longifolia and Cyperus rotundus versus mebeverine for 8 weeks and reported comparable improvement, but there was no placebo arm and ginger's individual contribution cannot be isolated. A 2026 review mapping plant extracts against international guidelines places ginger among agents showing mixed or inconsistent results, and identifies peppermint oil rather than ginger as the only plant-based therapy consistently acknowledged in adult and paediatric IBS guidelines. No trial cited here tested ginger separately in IBS-C versus IBS-D.

Doses studied: 1 g or 2 g of ginger daily for 28 days in the only placebo-controlled IBS trial (van Tilburg 2014). 1.2 g/day of standardised ginger root powder (84 mg/day gingerols and shogaols) for 14 days in healthy adults (Crichton 2023). A single 1.2 g dose in the functional dyspepsia gastric-emptying study (Hu 2011). An 8-week Iraqi trial used ginger inside a three-herb powder combination with no placebo arm.

Why this grade: 'Early' here describes only the immaturity of the evidence base; it is not a positive signal. Two placebo-controlled human trials exist and both are null in direction: the single dedicated IBS trial (n=45) found responder rates numerically LOWER on ginger (46.7% on 1 g, 33.3% on 2 g) than on placebo (57.1%), and a 14-day trial in 51 healthy adults found no change in bowel function, gastrointestinal symptoms, mood or quality of life. Remaining human data are a single-dose physiology study in functional dyspepsia that improved gastric emptying but changed no symptoms, and an open combination trial with no placebo arm from which ginger's contribution cannot be separated. This does not meet the bar for 'moderate', since no controlled trial shows benefit in IBS. It also does not meet the definitional bar for 'not_supported', which requires adequate evidence: 96 participants across two short trials cannot exclude a modest effect, and the IBS trial's own authors state larger trials are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. Neither trial reported IBS-C versus IBS-D subtype results, so no subtype claim is supportable.

Sources (6)
  1. Is ginger effective for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome? A double blind randomized controlled pilot trial.
    van Tilburg MAL, Palsson OS, Ringel Y, Whitehead WE, 2014 · PMID 24559811 · null

    45 IBS patients randomised to placebo, 1 g ginger or 2 g ginger daily for 28 days. Responders (at least 25% IBS-SS reduction): 57.1% placebo, 46.7% on 1 g, 33.3% on 2 g — numerically worse on ginger at both doses. Adequate relief reported by 53.3% on placebo and 53.3% in both ginger groups combined. Side effects were mild and reported by 35.7% on placebo versus 16.7% on ginger. The authors conclude ginger was well tolerated but did not perform better than placebo, and that larger trials are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn.

  2. Effect of Ginger Root Powder on Gastrointestinal Bacteria Composition, Gastrointestinal Symptoms, Mental Health, Fatigue, and Quality of Life: A Double-Blind Placebo-Controlled Trial.
    Crichton M, Marshall S, Marx W, Isenring E, et al., 2023 · PMID 37690779 · mixed

    51 healthy adults aged 18-30 randomised to 1.2 g/day standardised ginger root powder (84 mg/day gingerols and shogaols) or microcrystalline cellulose placebo for 14 days. Ginger altered some aspects of gastrointestinal bacterial composition (greater relative abundance of Actinobacteria, P=0.033) but did NOT change alpha or beta diversity, bowel function, gastrointestinal symptoms, mood or quality of life. One secondary measure, indigestion symptoms, improved (P=0.015). No moderate or severe adverse events were reported. A second placebo-controlled human trial pointing to no overall gut-symptom benefit.

  3. Effect of ginger on gastric motility and symptoms of functional dyspepsia.
    Hu ML, Rayner CK, Wu KL, et al., 2011 · PMID 21218090 · mixed

    Randomised double-blind crossover study in 11 patients with functional dyspepsia (not IBS): 1.2 g ginger shortened median gastric half-emptying time to 12.3 minutes (range 8.5-17.0) versus 16.1 minutes (8.3-22.6) on placebo (P<=0.05), with a trend toward more antral contractions (P=0.06). Crucially, there was no impact on gastrointestinal symptoms and no change in GLP-1, motilin or ghrelin. A physiological effect without symptomatic benefit, in a different condition.

  4. Plant-Derived Treatments for IBS: Clinical Outcomes, Mechanistic Insights, and Their Position in International Guidelines.
    Pastras P, Aggeletopoulou I, Bali M, Triantos C, 2026 · PMID 41599795 · mixed

    Narrative synthesis of plant extracts studied in IBS alongside international guideline positions. Ginger is grouped with agents showing mixed or inconsistent results, reflecting heterogeneous study designs and mechanisms. Peppermint oil is identified as the most effective botanical agent and the only plant-based therapy consistently acknowledged across adult and paediatric guideline recommendations; ginger is not.

  5. Treatment of irritable bowel syndrome using a selected herbal combination of Iraqi folk medicines.
    Sahib AS, 2013 · PMID 23707209 · positive

    Prospective randomised study in 40 IBS patients aged 25-60, comparing mebeverine (n=20) with a capsule combining powdered Mentha longifolia, Cyperus rotundus and Zingiber officinale (n=20) over 8 weeks. Symptom scores improved in the herbal group to a degree the author describes as comparable to mebeverine, with no adverse effects reported. There was no placebo arm and ginger's individual contribution cannot be isolated, so this cannot establish efficacy for ginger.

  6. Warfarin interactions with medicinal herbs.
    Milic N, Milosevic N, Golocorbin Kon S, Bozic T, Abenavoli L, Borrelli F, 2014 · PMID 25233607 · negative

    Review of Medline case reports of warfarin-herb interactions. Zingiber officinale is listed among herbs (with Panax ginseng, Hypericum perforatum, Ginkgo biloba, Allium sativum and others) implicated in case reports of severe adverse events during concomitant warfarin use, including spontaneous postoperative bleeding, haematoma formation, haematemesis, melaena, thrombosis, subarachnoid haemorrhage and subdural haematoma. This is a case-report-level safety signal, not trial data.

For migraine & headache

The honest answer on ginger is that nobody yet knows. The most recent and most careful review of all the trials concluded the evidence is too thin and too uncertain to say whether ginger helps a migraine that has already started, though smaller earlier studies had found more people pain-free at two hours and clearly less nausea and vomiting. What is clearer is that it does not seem to prevent migraines: the one properly sized study of taking ginger daily found it worked no better than a dummy capsule. It is cheap and generally well tolerated, so trying it during an attack is low-risk — just treat it as an experiment rather than a treatment, and not as a daily preventive.

Safety

Ginger was well tolerated across the migraine trials, and tolerability is the strongest thing that can be said for it here. The 2020 meta-analysis found no excess of total adverse events versus control (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.46-1.41) and significantly less nausea and vomiting (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30-0.77 as summarised by Andrade 2021), which matters in a condition where nausea is part of the attack (PMIDs 33293189, 34851560); the 2026 GRADE review likewise found no safety signal that would argue against a trial of it (PMID 42601183). In the head-to-head trial, ginger's adverse-effect profile was better than sumatriptan's (PMID 23657930). In the sublingual feverfew-plus-ginger pilot, oral numbness and nausea were the most frequent adverse events (PMID 21631494). A narrative review of a decade of randomized pain trials concluded that using ginger for pain relief is safe, while noting there is no consensus on an appropriate dose for long-term therapy (PMID 32436242) — note that review includes authors from a botanical extract manufacturer. Concentrated extracts can still cause gastrointestinal upset in some individuals even though the pooled trials showed no overall excess of adverse events.

Who should be careful: People taking warfarin or other anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication should not add concentrated ginger supplements without clinician oversight, because ginger is among the herbs and foods identified with increased bleeding risk in warfarin users (PMID 32478963). Ginger supplements at trial doses have not been established as safe in pregnancy for migraine specifically; the migraine trials enrolled non-pregnant adults aged roughly 18-60. Culinary ginger is not the same exposure as a standardised 400 mg extract, and the trial results apply only to the extracts studied, not to ginger tea or food, because chemical constituents vary by source (PMID 34851560).

Interactions: A systematic review of warfarin interactions with food, herbal and dietary supplements names ginger among the products associated with increased bleeding risk in people on warfarin; reported events across the reviewed herbs ranged from minor bleeding such as gum bleeding and purpura to major events including intracranial bleeding that led to death (PMID 32478963). Ginger is also among the herbal products most commonly used by older adults taking prescription drugs, which makes unreported concurrent use a practical risk (PMID 29196903). Within migraine trials, ginger was combined with intravenous ketoprofen and compared against sumatriptan without excess adverse events (PMIDs 29768938, 23657930), so the concern is bleeding risk in anticoagulated patients rather than interaction with standard acute migraine drugs.

What the evidence says

Ginger's migraine evidence sits almost entirely in acute (abortive) use, and the preventive literature consists of one adequately sized trial that is null. The most recent synthesis — a systematic review and meta-analysis of eight RCTs in 594 patients, searched to April 2026 and assessed with GRADE — concluded that the volume of evidence is limited, the certainty low to very low across all outcomes, and that it is currently too uncertain whether ginger is effective as either an acute or a preventive treatment; it characterised ginger added to ketoprofen as showing possibly little to no difference in two-hour headache severity (MD 1.27 lower, 95% CI 1.46 to 1.08 lower) on the strength of a single 60-participant study, and rated ginger versus sumatriptan and the feverfew-ginger combination as very uncertain. An earlier meta-analysis of three trials (227 participants) had been more favourable, finding an improved proportion pain-free at two hours (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.04-3.09, p=0.04) but no significant effect on treatment response (RR 2.04, 95% CI 0.35-11.94, p=0.43) and no excess of total adverse events (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.46-1.41, p=0.44), with nausea and vomiting less frequent on ginger (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30-0.77 as summarised by Andrade 2021). The underlying emergency-department trial (n=60) did find significantly better clinical response with 400 mg ginger dry extract added to intravenous ketoprofen at 1 hour (p=0.04), 1.5 hours (p=0.01) and 2 hours (p=0.04), and a 100-patient trial found ginger powder and sumatriptan comparably reduced headache severity at two hours with fewer adverse effects on ginger — but that trial had no placebo arm, so it establishes comparability to an active drug rather than efficacy over placebo. For prevention, a 107-patient three-month randomized trial of 200 mg dry extract three times daily found no difference from placebo in responder rate, days with severe pain, acute analgesic use or attack duration. A 2021 appraisal reached the same bottom line the 2026 review would later confirm: the evidence base is too small for formal recommendations, and findings generalise only to the specific extract studied.

Doses studied: Acute (during an attack): 400 mg of ginger dry extract (5% active ingredient) as a single dose added to 100 mg intravenous ketoprofen; a separate trial used ginger powder head-to-head against sumatriptan (dose not stated in the abstract). Preventive (null result): 200 mg dry extract, 5% active ingredient, three times daily for three months. A sublingual feverfew-plus-ginger combination product was used in the acute pilot trial. Results generalise only to the specific extract studied, because ginger's chemical constituents vary by source.

Why this grade: The 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis — 8 RCTs, 594 patients, GRADE-assessed — concludes it is currently too uncertain whether ginger is effective as either an acute or a preventive treatment, with certainty of evidence rated low to very low for every outcome measured. The earlier positive signal for acute use rests on a meta-analysis of just three small trials (227 participants) with wide confidence intervals, one of which had no placebo arm, and its headline two-hour pain estimate traces to a single 60-participant study. The one adequately sized preventive trial (n=107, three months) was flatly null on every endpoint. That is preliminary human evidence for acute use and no support for daily preventive use, so 'early' — and a 2021 appraisal independently concluded the evidence base is too small for formal recommendations. Conflating the acute and preventive literatures here would be the single easiest way to overstate ginger.

Sources (7)
  1. Ginger for the treatment and prevention of migraine headaches: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Atkins, Manger & Albarqouni, 2026 (BMJ Evid Based Med) · PMID 42601183 · mixed

    Eight RCTs, 594 patients; six databases searched to March 2025 and updated April 2026, plus citation searching; risk of bias by Cochrane RoB-2 and certainty by GRADE (PROSPERO CRD420251025768). For acute treatment, ginger with ketoprofen versus placebo with ketoprofen may have little to no difference in headache severity at 2 hours (MD 1.27 lower, 95% CI 1.46 lower to 1.08 lower; 1 study, 60 participants; LOW certainty), and it was very uncertain whether ginger combined with feverfew versus placebo, or ginger alone versus sumatriptan, improved pain relief, pain-free proportion or mean severity at 2 hours. For prevention, ginger added to an active drug may reduce headache severity at 3 months (pooled MD -3.18, 95% CI -3.94 to -2.42; 2 studies, 183 participants; LOW certainty), but effects on frequency and duration were very uncertain. Overall certainty ranged from LOW TO VERY LOW for all outcome measures, and the authors conclude it is currently too uncertain whether ginger is effective as either an acute or preventative treatment, with more trials required.

  2. Double-blind placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial of ginger (Zingiber officinale Rosc.) in the prophylactic treatment of migraine.
    Martins, Rodrigues, Monteze, Tibaes, Amaral, Gomez, Teixeira & Ferreira, 2020 (Cephalalgia) · PMID 31398997 · null

    107 patients with episodic migraine, aged 18-60 and not taking any prophylactic medication, randomised 1:1 to 200 mg ginger dry extract (5% active ingredient) three times daily or cellulose placebo for three months after a one-month observation period. The percentage of patients responding (50% reduction in attacks) did not differ between groups. Days with severe pain, acute analgesic use and attack duration decreased in both groups with no significant difference between ginger and placebo. Authors concluded ginger provides no greater benefit than placebo in the prophylactic treatment of migraine (NCT02570633).

  3. The efficacy of ginger for the treatment of migraine: A meta-analysis of randomized controlled studies.
    Chen & Cai, 2020 (Am J Emerg Med) · PMID 33293189 · mixed

    Three RCTs pooled under a random-effects model. Ginger improved pain-free status at 2 hours (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.04-3.09, p=0.04) and reduced 2-hour pain scores (MD -1.27, 95% CI -1.46 to -1.07, p<0.00001), but showed no significant effect on treatment response (RR 2.04, 95% CI 0.35-11.94, p=0.43) and no difference in total adverse events (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.46-1.41, p=0.44); nausea and vomiting were lower with ginger. Read with caution: only three trials were available, the treatment-response confidence interval spans a 34-fold range, and the 2026 GRADE review attributes that same 2-hour pain estimate to a single 60-participant study rated low certainty rather than to a robust three-trial pool.

  4. Double-blind placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial of ginger (Zingiber officinale Rosc.) addition in migraine acute treatment.
    Martins, Rodrigues, Rodrigues, dos Santos, Teixeira & Ferreira, 2019 (Cephalalgia) · PMID 29768938 · positive

    60 adults with episodic migraine (one to six attacks per month) presenting to a general hospital emergency room received 400 mg ginger extract (5% active ingredient) or cellulose placebo in addition to 100 mg intravenous ketoprofen. The ginger group showed significantly better clinical response at 1 hour (p=0.04), 1.5 hours (p=0.01) and 2 hours (p=0.04), with reduction in pain and improvement in functional status at all assessed timepoints (NCT02568644). Small single-centre add-on design; this is the single study underpinning the pooled 2-hour pain estimate, and the 2026 review rates the resulting evidence low certainty.

  5. Comparison between the efficacy of ginger and sumatriptan in the ablative treatment of the common migraine.
    Maghbooli, Golipour, Moghimi Esfandabadi & Yousefi, 2014 (Phytother Res) · PMID 23657930 · positive

    100 patients with acute migraine without aura randomised double-blind to ginger powder or sumatriptan across five consecutive attacks. Mean headache severity decreased significantly two hours after either drug, and efficacy of ginger powder and sumatriptan was statistically comparable; patient satisfaction and willingness to continue did not differ. Clinical adverse effects were fewer with ginger than sumatriptan. No placebo arm, so this shows comparability to an active comparator rather than superiority over placebo — and the 2026 GRADE review rated the ginger-versus-sumatriptan comparison very uncertain.

  6. A double-blind placebo-controlled pilot study of sublingual feverfew and ginger (LipiGesic M) in the treatment of migraine.
    Cady, Goldstein, Nett, Mitchell, Beach & Browning, 2011 (Headache) · PMID 21631494 · positive

    60 patients treated 208 evaluable attacks of migraine at the mild-headache phase; 45 subjects treated 163 attacks with sublingual feverfew/ginger and 15 subjects treated 58 attacks with placebo, with evaluable diaries completed for 151 active and 57 placebo attacks. At 2 hours, 32% on active versus 16% on placebo were pain-free (p=0.02), and 63% versus 39% had pain relief (p=0.002); pain-level change was -0.24 versus -0.04 on a 4-point scale (p=0.006). This is a combination product with 3:1 randomisation, so the contribution of ginger alone cannot be isolated, and the 2026 review rated this comparison very uncertain. Oral numbness and nausea were the most frequent adverse events.

  7. Ginger for Migraine.
    Andrade, 2021 (J Clin Psychiatry) · PMID 34851560 · mixed

    Appraisal noting one RCT found ginger no better than placebo for preventing migraine episodes, and that the acute meta-analysis (pooled N=227) showed a higher pain-free proportion at 2 hours (RR 1.79, 95% CI 1.04-3.09, 2 RCTs), reduced nausea and vomiting (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.30-0.77, 3 RCTs) and no increased adverse-event risk (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.46-1.41, 3 RCTs). Concludes the evidence base is clearly too small for formal recommendations, that ginger's comparison against established acute treatments is unknown, and that results generalise only to the specific extract studied because chemical constituents vary by source. Suggests ginger may be useful as a home remedy for those who cannot take established first-line acute treatments.
