Herbs

Saffron (Crocus sativus)

Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood Moderate evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For depression & low mood

Saffron is one of the better-researched herbs for low mood, and across many small studies people taking it reported feeling meaningfully less depressed - often about as much as those given a standard antidepressant, with fewer side effects. The honest caveats are real, though: when clinicians rated people's depression rather than people rating themselves, the benefit largely vanished, most of the research comes from a small number of teams in one part of the world, and a 2025 study in France found no benefit at all. It looks genuinely promising for mild low mood, but it is not settled science, and it is not a substitute for treatment if your depression is severe.

Safety

At the doses used in trials (typically 28-30 mg/day of extract) saffron is well tolerated. Pooled data show fewer adverse events than SSRIs (risk difference -0.06, 95% CI -0.09 to -0.04), the 2005 comparator trial found no significant difference in side effects versus fluoxetine, and the largest trial to date reported no serious adverse reactions. Reported effects are mild and include nausea, appetite change, headache and drowsiness. Two practical cautions. First, the trials used specific standardised extracts at specific doses; a supplement that does not disclose its standardisation is not the product that was tested, and 'more is better' does not follow from any of this evidence - stay at studied doses. Second, saffron is the world's most expensive spice, which creates an obvious commercial incentive around product identity, so buy only products that state their standardisation.

Who should be careful: Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding. The trials did not enrol pregnant or breastfeeding women and no adequate safety data exist for either, so this is a precaution based on absence of evidence rather than on a demonstrated harm - but with a supplement taken for a non-urgent reason, absence of evidence is reason enough to wait. Not established for bipolar depression: like any agent with antidepressant activity a theoretical risk of mood switching exists and has not been tested, so anyone with a history of mania should be assessed first. Not a substitute for treatment of severe depression or for care when suicidal thinking is present.

Interactions: Interaction data in humans are thin. Because saffron's proposed mechanism is partly serotonergic, combining it with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, triptans or other serotonergic drugs should be done only with clinician oversight - note that the comparator trials studied saffron INSTEAD of an SSRI, never on top of one. Caution is also reasonable alongside anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs and antihypertensives. The absence of documented interactions here reflects a lack of study, not proof of safety: no trial in this evidence base was designed to detect them.

What the evidence says

A 2026 GRADE-assessed meta-analysis of 34 RCTs (1,769 participants) found saffron reduced Beck Depression Inventory scores (14 trials, 817 participants; WMD -4.39, 95% CI -6.64 to -2.15, p<0.001, I-squared 92.3%) and Beck Anxiety Inventory scores (6 trials, 339 participants; WMD -5.06, 95% CI -8.44 to -1.68, p=0.003), but found no significant effect on the clinician-rated Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale, or the Profile of Mood States; certainty was rated moderate. An earlier meta-analysis of 21 trials reported the identical pattern: significant on BDI (WMD -4.86, 95% CI -6.58 to -3.14) and BAI, non-significant on HDRS-D and HARS-A. Against SSRIs, a meta-analysis of 8 depression trials found no significant difference (SMD 0.10, 95% CI -0.09 to 0.29) with fewer adverse events on saffron (risk difference -0.06, 95% CI -0.09 to -0.04, I-squared 0%) - non-inferiority within small trials, not demonstrated superiority. The two most recent trials in adults with subclinical depressive symptoms disagree with each other: the largest trial to date (202 adults, 12 weeks, 28 mg/day, manufacturer-supported) found a modest benefit on its primary outcome (DASS-21 depression beta -2.92, 95% CI -5.13 to -0.71, Cohen's d 0.39; 72.3% vs 54.3% achieving clinically significant change, p=0.010) but no between-group difference on any secondary outcome and a large placebo response, while an independent 51-participant, 6-week trial found no effect on its primary composite outcome of depression, anxiety and fatigue, on any individual symptom category, or on inflammatory and HPA-axis measures. An international meta-review of phytoceuticals in psychiatry found supportive meta-analytic evidence for saffron in depression while explicitly cautioning that the saffron meta-analyses may have significant publication bias.

Doses studied: 30 mg/day of saffron stigma extract (commonly 15 mg twice daily) is the most-studied dose, used in the original Iranian fluoxetine-comparator trial and in much subsequent work; standardised commercial extracts have been trialled at 28 mg/day. The pooled meta-analysis included any trial of at least 4 weeks; the individual trials cited here ran 6 to 12 weeks. Preparations differ between studies (stigma extracts and proprietary standardised commercial extracts), which limits direct comparison between products - the results belong to the specific extracts tested, not to saffron generally.

Why this grade: Saffron has accumulated a substantial RCT base (34 trials, 1,769 participants in the most recent GRADE-assessed meta-analysis) with consistently positive pooled results and repeated non-inferiority to SSRIs, which rules out the 'early' grade. It is held at moderate rather than strong because of four documented problems. First, a specific and repeated discrepancy: the pooled benefit appears on patient self-report scales (Beck Depression Inventory) but disappears on clinician-rated scales (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) in two independent meta-analyses. Second, heterogeneity is extreme (I-squared 92%), most trials are small and concentrated in a single region and set of research groups, and an international meta-review flagged likely publication bias. Third, the largest and most rigorous trial to date - funded by an extract manufacturer - found only a modest effect (Cohen's d 0.39) against a very large placebo response, with no benefit on any secondary outcome. Fourth, an independent 2025 placebo-controlled trial in the same population of adults with subclinical depressive symptoms found no effect at all on its primary outcome, so the two most recent trials in that group point in opposite directions.

For insomnia

Saffron is one of the few traditional sleep remedies that has actually been put through real randomized trials, and several found people rated their sleep as better and felt more rested. The honest catch is that the benefit shows up mostly in how people rate their own sleep, and largely vanishes when researchers measure sleep with EEG, wearables, or actigraphy. The biggest trial found no sleep benefit except in people who started out sleeping worst, a review focused specifically on insomnia found no clear effect on insomnia severity, and most of the positive studies were run with the involvement of companies that sell the extract. It may be worth trying, but expect a small nudge rather than a transformation.

Safety

Saffron was well tolerated across the trials retrieved. The 63-participant sleep RCT reported no adverse effects; the 202-participant 12-week trial reported no serious adverse reactions; the 165-participant insomnia trial reported no serious adverse events; the 46-RCT systematic review described good tolerability with few side effects. That said, trials here ran 4-48 weeks, so safety beyond about a year at supplement doses is unstudied. Doses used in sleep research (14-30 mg/day of standardized extract) are far below culinary saffron amounts associated with toxicity, but no formal upper-limit study in humans was identified in this search. Saffron is expensive and frequently adulterated, so product quality is itself a safety variable — trials used standardized, characterized extracts, which is not necessarily what a consumer buys. Note also that most positive saffron sleep trials involved the extract manufacturer as funder or co-author, which bears on how confidently tolerability as well as efficacy can be read from them.

Who should be careful: None established by the clinical trials retrieved. Traditional and pharmacological sources caution against high-dose saffron in pregnancy on abortifacient grounds; this was not tested in any trial identified here, and none of the sleep trials enrolled pregnant participants, so pregnancy and breastfeeding should be treated as unstudied and avoided. People taking prescription antidepressants should consult a clinician first. Anyone with worsening insomnia or mood symptoms should seek clinical assessment rather than escalating a supplement.

Interactions: No formal human drug-interaction studies were identified. Because saffron shows antidepressant-scale effects on mood measures and has been compared directly with conventional antidepressants, additive effects with prescription antidepressants are plausible and should be discussed with a prescriber before combining. Additive sedation with other sleep aids has not been studied. Traditional and pharmacological sources describe anticoagulant-like effects at high doses; this was not confirmed by any trial retrieved here, but caution around surgery or anticoagulant therapy is reasonable.

What the evidence says

Multiple randomized placebo-controlled trials support a modest benefit of standardized saffron extract for self-reported sleep quality, but the objective evidence is largely null and the condition-specific meta-analysis is not significant. Lopresti et al. (2020) randomized 63 adults with self-reported sleep problems to affron 14 mg twice daily or placebo for 28 days; among 55 analysed, saffron produced greater improvement in Insomnia Severity Index total score (p=0.017), Restorative Sleep Questionnaire total score (p=0.029) and Pittsburgh Sleep Diary sleep-quality ratings (p=0.014), and a follow-up 120-participant dose-ranging trial improved the primary sleep-quality rating but found no significant differences on other questionnaire and sleep-diary measures. The largest insomnia-specific trial (Schuster 2025, n=165) met its primary endpoint with a very small effect — Athens Insomnia Scale between-group adjusted mean difference -0.95 points (95% CI -1.79 to -0.11) — with all other measures non-significant. Countervailing evidence is substantial: the dedicated Crocus sativus insomnia meta-analysis (8 studies, 431 participants) found insomnia severity NOT significant (SMD 0.53, 95% CI -0.05 to 1.11, I-squared 59%, p=0.08) with sleep quality rated very low-quality evidence; a crocetin crossover trial (n=30) increased EEG delta power but produced no significant difference in sleep latency, sleep efficiency, total sleep time or wake after sleep onset; a 62-participant trial using WHOOP monitoring found no between-group difference in any outcome; and a 64-participant actigraphy trial of a saffron-containing combination found total sleep time and PSQI improved MORE on placebo. Most importantly, the largest saffron trial to date (Lopresti 2025, n=202, 28 mg/day for 12 weeks) met its primary depression endpoint (DASS-21 depression beta -2.92, 95% CI -5.13 to -0.71, Cohen's d=0.39) but reported no evidence of between-group differences in the other secondary outcomes, with a sleep-disturbance benefit (beta -2.72, 95% CI -4.99 to -0.46, d=0.44) emerging only in exploratory analysis of participants with greater baseline sleep disturbance and against large placebo responses. A broader review of 46 RCTs across neurological and psychiatric conditions reported a pooled effect of -1.91 (95% CI -2.88 to -0.93) for sleep disorders, though only a subset of those 46 trials measured sleep.

Doses studied: 28 mg/day of standardized saffron stigma extract (affron, given as 14 mg twice daily) for 28 days is the best-studied sleep dose; a 120-person trial found 14 mg and 28 mg performed similarly, giving no dose-response. Also studied: 30 mg/day for 4 weeks in older adults, 20 or 30 mg/day for 4 weeks in moderate insomnia, 15.5 mg/day for 6 weeks, and 28 mg/day for 12 weeks in the largest trial. The isolated constituent crocetin was studied at 7.5 mg/day for 14 days.

Why this grade: Genuine randomized placebo-controlled evidence with sleep endpoints exists in reasonable volume — roughly eight RCTs and two meta-analyses — which places saffron well above 'early'. But the picture is modest and mixed rather than clean, and it falls clearly short of 'strong'. The dedicated meta-analysis of Crocus sativus specifically for insomnia found NO significant effect on insomnia severity (SMD 0.53, 95% CI -0.05 to 1.11, p=0.08) and graded the sleep-quality finding 'very low-quality evidence'. Benefit appears consistently on subjective ratings and largely disappears on objective measurement: four separate trials using EEG, sleep diaries, a WHOOP wearable, and actigraphy found no significant between-group difference. The largest saffron trial (n=202) was null for sleep outside an exploratory subgroup, the largest insomnia-specific trial (n=165) found a real but very small effect of 0.95 points on the Athens Insomnia Scale, and nearly every positive trial involved the extract manufacturer. There is no clinical-guideline first-line support.
