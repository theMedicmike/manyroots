Low-carbohydrate diets (including the remission evidence)

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Cutting carbohydrate is a genuinely effective way to bring blood sugar down over the first few months, and in trials more than half of people reached a non-diabetic HbA1c reading at six months, compared with under a third on comparison diets. Two honest caveats: that headline counted people who were still on medication, and when the trials required people to reach a normal reading with no medication at all the advantage was small and no longer statistically reliable. The advantage also fades by a year, mostly because the diet is hard to keep up, and over two years low-carb and other sensible diets end up about equal. If you take insulin, a sulfonylurea, or an SGLT2 'flozin' tablet, your doses need reviewing by your prescriber before you start, because the diet can cause dangerous lows or, rarely, ketoacidosis - and with a 'flozin' that ketoacidosis can set in while your meter still reads normal, so feeling badly unwell means urgent medical care, not a stricter diet. Never adjust your doses yourself.

Safety

The main hazard is not the food but the interaction with diabetes medication: trials show insulin and sulfonylurea requirements fall quickly on low-carbohydrate diets (insulin dose 42 to 30 units by 6 months, PMID 22562179; medication-effect score fell more on the low-carbohydrate arm over 2 years, PMID 29178536; insulin use -62% and sulfonylureas -100% at 2 years in a non-randomised cohort, PMID 31231311). That means doses must be reviewed and reduced PROACTIVELY BY THE PRESCRIBER to avoid hypoglycaemia - it is a supervised safety adjustment, not something to do alone, and this page is not a reason to stop or cut medication on your own. LDL cholesterol may rise (clinically important but non-significant worsening at 12 months, PMID 33441384; total cholesterol increased in another meta-analysis, PMID 36297075). Cohort data associate very-low-carbohydrate patterns built on animal fat and protein with higher mortality (PMID 30122560). Constipation and low fibre intake are common in practice. A 2-year RCT found no adverse effect on mood, cognition, diabetes distress or quality of life compared with a high-carbohydrate diet (PMID 34018052).

Who should be careful: Not for type 1 diabetes or pregnancy without specialist supervision because of ketoacidosis risk (this site's scope excludes type 1; the warning stands). Anyone on insulin or a sulfonylurea needs a medication plan agreed with their prescriber before starting. People with advanced kidney disease should avoid high-protein versions. Not advised for people with a history of eating disorders.

Interactions: SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin and similar) combined with carbohydrate restriction or ketogenic eating have been linked to euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis in published case reports, which list low-carbohydrate/ketogenic eating among the precipitating factors (PMIDs 34527480, 37248092); this combination should be discussed with the prescriber before starting - and if you go ahead, learn the warning signs, because this is the one situation on this page where a normal glucose reading is not reassurance. In a pooled review of 13 case reports (14 patients) of ketoacidosis on an SGLT2 inhibitor plus a ketogenic or very-low-carbohydrate diet, average blood glucose on arrival was only 167.5 mg/dL (SD 41.8) alongside an average pH of 7.10 and anion gap of 23.6 mmol/L, and over 90% were hospitalised within four weeks of starting the diet (PMID 36444120); one published case had a plasma glucose of 84 mg/dL (PMID 32178617), and another had a glucose of 127 mg/dL with 3+ ketonuria and a pH of 7.22 (PMID 32855828). So: nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, breathlessness or simply feeling badly unwell while taking an SGLT2 inhibitor and cutting carbohydrate means you need URGENT medical care and a ketone test, whatever your meter says - the symptoms are non-specific and the diagnosis is easily missed precisely because glucose looks normal (PMIDs 32855828, 34123681), and expert-panel guidance is that anyone on an SGLT2 inhibitor with signs or symptoms of ketoacidosis should be investigated for it, especially when euglycaemic (PMID 28003053). Do not assume it is 'keto flu' and do not restrict carbohydrate harder to push through it: carbohydrate restriction is itself one of the listed precipitants (PMID 28003053) and the ketogenic state is what drives the ketoacidosis (PMID 36444120), so cutting further deepens it rather than resolving it. In the case at PMID 32855828 that first, milder episode was attributed to the ketogenic diet, the SGLT2 inhibitor was not identified on the medication list, and the patient returned about a month later with a pH of 6.94. Insulin and sulfonylureas: additive glucose-lowering leads to hypoglycaemia unless doses are reduced under supervision (PMIDs 22562179, 33441384).

What the evidence says

The endpoints below are SURROGATES: HbA1c, fasting glucose, weight, and 'remission' defined as an HbA1c reading below 6.5%. None of them is a prevented heart attack, stroke, amputation, kidney failure or death, and no randomised trial of carbohydrate restriction has tested those outcomes. In established type 2 diabetes, a BMJ meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (n=1,357) found that at 6 months low-carbohydrate diets (below 130 g/day or below 26% of a 2,000 kcal/day diet) achieved remission defined as HbA1c below 6.5% in 76/133 (57%) versus 41/131 (31%) of controls (risk difference 0.32, 95% CI 0.17-0.47; 8 studies, n=264); effect sizes were smaller and non-significant when remission had to be reached off medication, and remission markedly decreased in studies that included patients using insulin. At 12 months remission data were sparse and ranged from a small effect to a trivial increased risk, and the large gains in weight, triglycerides and insulin sensitivity seen at 6 months diminished, with clinically important but non-statistically-significant worsening of LDL cholesterol and quality of life (PMID 33441384). Pooled HbA1c effects elsewhere are modest: -0.28% at 1 year across 7 trials, with 15 of 18 included studies at high risk of bias (PMID 29269890); -0.41% at 3 months across 22 RCTs, with total cholesterol increased and no long-term advantage (PMID 36297075); and -0.36% across 17 RCTs in overweight/obese type 2 diabetes (PMID 39834467). The Cochrane review of 61 RCTs (n=6,925) concluded that low- versus balanced-carbohydrate weight-reducing diets probably make little or no difference to weight (-1.26 kg at 3-6 months; -0.33 kg, 95% CI -2.13 to 1.46, at 1-2 years in type 2 diabetes), HbA1c, LDL or diastolic blood pressure over 1-2 years (moderate certainty; PMID 35088407). Two long randomised trials agree: in a 2-year RCT (n=61) HbA1c fell only on the 20%-carbohydrate diet at 6 months (-4.8 mmol/mol, p=0.004) and not at 12 months (-2.2 mmol/mol, p=0.12), with no weight difference (PMID 22562179); and in a 2-year energy-matched RCT (n=115, both arms exercising) a 14%-carbohydrate diet and a 53%-carbohydrate low-fat diet produced similar changes in HbA1c (-0.6% vs -0.9%), weight, body fat, blood pressure, fasting glucose, LDL-C and flow-mediated dilatation, with the low-carbohydrate arm doing better only on medication requirement, glycaemic variability, triglycerides and HDL (PMID 29178536). For remission as an outcome the benchmark is not low-carbohydrate but the total-diet-replacement DiRECT trial: 46% versus 4% at 1 year and 36% versus 3% at 2 years (PMIDs 29221645, 30852132). An umbrella review rated ketogenic/very-low-carbohydrate remission evidence (about 20%) as very low certainty with serious and critical risk of bias, and found low-carbohydrate diets no better for weight loss than higher-carbohydrate/low-fat diets on high-quality, GRADE-high evidence (PMID 34796367); the non-randomised Virta continuous-care cohort reported 17.6% remission at 2 years and 20% among 5-year completers (PMIDs 31231311, 39433217). Cohort data show a U-shaped mortality curve, with below-40% energy from carbohydrate associated with HR 1.20 (1.09-1.32), higher when carbohydrate is replaced by animal fat and protein (HR 1.18) and lower when plant-based (HR 0.82) (PMID 30122560). There is no evidence in prediabetes prevention specifically; the 3-year PREVIEW trial of a high-protein/low-glycaemic-index (not low-carbohydrate) maintenance diet in 2,326 adults with prediabetes found no difference in diabetes incidence between diets (PMID 33026154).

Doses studied: Trials defined 'low-carbohydrate' as below 130 g/day or below 26% of a 2,000 kcal/day diet, and 'very low' as below 10% of energy or 50 g/day, for at least 12 weeks (PMIDs 33441384, 35088407); one 2-year RCT prescribed 20% of energy from carbohydrate (PMID 22562179) and another 14% of energy with 28% protein and 58% fat (PMID 29178536). Meta-analyses note that below 50 g/day was rarely sustained and judged unrealistic, and that adherence rather than carbohydrate level explained weight outcomes (PMIDs 29269890, 33441384).

Why this grade: Randomised trial meta-analyses show a clear short-term benefit in established type 2 diabetes: HbA1c falls by about 0.3-0.5 percentage points at 3-6 months and 57% versus 31% reach HbA1c below 6.5% at 6 months (Goldenberg 2021, PMID 33441384). But the effect attenuates and the comparison weakens: in the same review the remission effect became small and non-significant when it had to be achieved off medication and shrank in trials including insulin users; by 12 months remission data ranged from a small effect to a trivial increased risk. The 2022 Cochrane review found little or no difference in weight, HbA1c or LDL over 1-2 years versus balanced-carbohydrate diets (PMID 35088407), and the longest energy-matched RCT (2 years, n=115) found HbA1c change no different from a high-carbohydrate low-fat diet (PMID 29178536). The strongest remission evidence (DiRECT) comes from a low-calorie total-diet-replacement programme, not a low-carbohydrate diet, and the 5-year low-carbohydrate remission data are non-randomised. Remission here is a blood-test definition, and no randomised trial has tested this diet against heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure or death. Positive but time-limited and mixed evidence is moderate, not strong.

  1. Efficacy and safety of low and very low carbohydrate diets for type 2 diabetes remission: systematic review and meta-analysis of published and unpublished randomized trial data.
    Goldenberg JZ et al., 2021 (BMJ) · PMID 33441384 · mixed

    23 RCTs (n=1,357): at 6 months remission (HbA1c <6.5%) 76/133 (57%) vs 41/131 (31%); risk difference 0.32 (95% CI 0.17-0.47; 8 studies, n=264). Effects were smaller and non-significant when remission required being off medication, and markedly decreased in studies including insulin users. At 12 months remission data were sparse (small effect to trivial increased risk), weight/triglyceride/insulin-sensitivity gains diminished, and LDL cholesterol and quality of life showed clinically important but non-significant worsening. Moderate-to-low certainty.

  2. Low-carbohydrate versus balanced-carbohydrate diets for reducing weight and cardiovascular risk.
    Naude CE et al., 2022 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 35088407 · null

    61 RCTs (n=6,925): in type 2 diabetes, low- vs balanced-carbohydrate weight-reducing diets gave weight change -1.26 kg (95% CI -2.44 to -0.09) at 3-6 months and -0.33 kg (95% CI -2.13 to 1.46) at 1-2 years, with little or no difference in HbA1c, diastolic BP or LDL cholesterol over 1-2 years (moderate certainty). Evidence on adverse effects was too limited to draw conclusions.

  3. Effects of an energy-restricted low-carbohydrate, high unsaturated fat/low saturated fat diet versus a high-carbohydrate, low-fat diet in type 2 diabetes: A 2-year randomized clinical trial.
    Tay J et al., 2018 (Diabetes Obes Metab) · PMID 29178536 · null

    115 adults with type 2 diabetes randomised for 2 years to 14%-carbohydrate or 53%-carbohydrate energy-matched diets, both with supervised exercise (61 completers): reductions in weight (-6.8 vs -6.6 kg), body fat, blood pressure, HbA1c (-0.6% vs -0.9%) and fasting glucose were similar between groups (all P>=0.09), as were LDL-C, brachial flow-mediated dilatation, eGFR and albuminuria. The low-carbohydrate arm did better only on medication-effect score, glycaemic variability, triglycerides and HDL-C.

  4. The interpretation and effect of a low-carbohydrate diet in the management of type 2 diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Huntriss R et al., 2018 (Eur J Clin Nutr) · PMID 29269890 · positive

    18 studies (n=2,204); 7 contributed 1-year data: HbA1c -0.28% (95% CI -0.53 to -0.02), triglycerides -0.24 mmol/L, HDL +0.06 mmol/L, systolic BP -2.74 mmHg; no difference in weight, total cholesterol, LDL or diastolic BP. 15 of 18 studies were at high risk of bias; the authors note the HbA1c benefit may be blunted by concurrent medication reduction, and that below 50 g/day appears unrealistic in this population.

  5. In type 2 diabetes, randomisation to advice to follow a low-carbohydrate diet transiently improves glycaemic control compared with advice to follow a low-fat diet producing a similar weight loss.
    Guldbrand H et al., 2012 (Diabetologia) · PMID 22562179 · mixed

    2-year RCT, 61 adults with type 2 diabetes: HbA1c fell only in the 20%-carbohydrate group at 6 months (-4.8 mmol/mol, p=0.004) and not at 12 months (-2.2 mmol/mol, p=0.12); weight loss did not differ between diets; insulin dose fell more on low-carbohydrate at 6 months (42 to 30 units vs 39 to 38; p=0.046 for the between-group change).

  6. Long-Term Effects of a Novel Continuous Remote Care Intervention Including Nutritional Ketosis for the Management of Type 2 Diabetes: A 2-Year Non-randomized Clinical Trial.
    Athinarayanan SJ et al., 2019 (Front Endocrinol) · PMID 31231311 · positive

    NON-RANDOMISED, open-label, industry-run: 262 on very-low-carbohydrate continuous care vs 87 usual care; at 2 years diabetes reversal 53.5% and remission 17.6%; use of glucose-lowering drugs other than metformin fell from 55.7% to 26.8%, insulin -62%, sulfonylureas -100%; no change in usual care. Allocation was not randomised, so these figures cannot be read as a causal effect estimate.

  7. Dietary carbohydrate intake and mortality: a prospective cohort study and meta-analysis.
    Seidelmann SB et al., 2018 (Lancet Public Health) · PMID 30122560 · negative

    ARIC cohort (n=15,428, median 25 years) plus meta-analysis (n=432,179): U-shaped association, lowest mortality at 50-55% of energy from carbohydrate; below 40% HR 1.20 (95% CI 1.09-1.32) for all-cause mortality. Replacing carbohydrate with animal fat/protein HR 1.18 (1.08-1.29); with plant sources HR 0.82 (0.78-0.87).

  8. Dietary Changes Leading to Euglycemic Diabetic Ketoacidosis in Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitor Users: A Challenge for Primary Care Physicians?
    Somagutta MKR et al., 2022 (Korean J Fam Med) · PMID 36444120 · negative

    Pooled review of 13 case reports (14 patients, 12 type 2 and 2 type 1 diabetes) who developed euglycaemic ketoacidosis on a ketogenic or very-low-carbohydrate diet while taking an SGLT2 inhibitor. At presentation average blood glucose was only 167.50 +/- 41.80 mg/dL despite pH 7.10 +/- 0.10, bicarbonate 8.1 +/- 3.0 mmol/L and anion gap 23.6 +/- 3.5 mmol/L - the acidosis was severe while glucose looked near-normal. Over 90% were hospitalised within 4 weeks of starting the diet (onset ranged 1-90 days). This is the basis for the rule that a normal meter reading does not exclude ketoacidosis in this combination.

  9. SGLT2 Inhibitor-associated Diabetic Ketoacidosis: Clinical Review and Recommendations for Prevention and Diagnosis.
    Goldenberg RM et al., 2016 (Clin Ther) · PMID 28003053 · negative

    Cross-Canada expert panel review of reported cases. Ketoacidosis on an SGLT2 inhibitor is rare but may be euglycaemic (plasma glucose <14 mmol/L), and is typically precipitated by insulin omission or dose reduction, severe acute illness, dehydration, extensive exercise, surgery, LOW-CARBOHYDRATE DIETS or excessive alcohol. The panel's recommendation is that every SGLT2-inhibitor-treated patient presenting with signs or symptoms of DKA should be suspected of having it and investigated for it, especially euglycaemic patients, and that the drug be withheld when precipitants develop.

  10. A Case of Diabetic Ketoacidosis in a Patient on an SGLT2 Inhibitor and a Ketogenic Diet: A Critical Trio Not to Be Missed.
    Steinmetz-Wood S et al., 2020 (Case Rep Endocrinol) · PMID 32855828 · negative

    47-year-old man on metformin and empagliflozin following a ketogenic diet. At a FIRST emergency presentation with malaise he had anion gap 21, bicarbonate 13 mmol/L, pH 7.22 and 3+ ketonuria with a glucose of only 127 mg/dL; the findings were attributed to his ketogenic diet and the empagliflozin was not on his medication list. He returned about a month later with dyspnoea, emesis, abdominal pain and anorexia, pH 6.94, bicarbonate 5 mmol/L and beta-hydroxybutyrate 8.9 mmol/L. Documents both the symptom picture and the specific failure mode of blaming ketosis symptoms on the diet.
