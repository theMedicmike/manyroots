For fatigue & low energy

ImportantGet ferritin tested before supplementing - and keep iron tablets away from children, where overdose is a leading cause of poisoning death.

If your tiredness is coming from low iron, replacing it can make a real difference - but only if you actually are low. In the trials, women with low ferritin who took iron reported less tiredness than women taking a dummy pill, while the same treatment did nothing measurable for their stamina or fitness, and pooled analysis found the benefit vanished in people who were not iron-deficient to begin with. That is why a ferritin blood test comes before the supplement: iron you don't need won't lift your energy, often upsets the stomach, and in some people builds up to harmful levels.

Safety

Oral ferrous sulfate roughly doubles the odds of gastrointestinal side effects compared with placebo (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.74-3.08) - nausea, constipation, abdominal pain, dark stools - and the pooled analysis found no relationship between those odds and the dose taken, so lowering the dose is not a reliable fix. Iron tablets are a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children and must be stored out of reach. Taking iron without a documented deficiency has no demonstrated benefit for fatigue and, in people with an iron-loading condition, allows iron to accumulate in liver, heart and pancreas.

Who should be careful: Do not supplement without a confirmed low ferritin. Avoid in hereditary haemochromatosis and other iron-overload states, and in anaemias not caused by iron deficiency such as thalassaemia, sideroblastic or aplastic anaemia. Unexplained iron deficiency in men and in postmenopausal women needs investigation for gastrointestinal blood loss alongside, not instead of, replacement.

Interactions: Iron reduces absorption of levothyroxine, tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, levodopa, bisphosphonates and mycophenolate - separate doses by several hours. Calcium supplements, tea and coffee polyphenols, and proton pump inhibitors reduce iron absorption; vitamin C increases it. Ferritin is an acute-phase protein, so a normal result during infection, inflammation or active liver disease does not rule out deficiency.

What the evidence says

A 2018 systematic review of 18 randomised trials (1,170 participants) in iron-deficient non-anaemic adults found iron supplementation reduced self-reported fatigue (SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.23, I-squared 0%, 4 trials, 714 participants) but did not improve objective physical capacity, including maximal oxygen consumption (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.37, 9 trials, 235 participants). In the anchoring trial of 144 non-anaemic women with unexplained fatigue, fatigue fell 29% on 80 mg/day oral ferrous sulfate versus 13% on placebo over four weeks (difference 0.95 points on a 10-point scale, 95% CI 0.32 to 1.62, P=0.004), with benefit restricted on subgroup analysis to women with ferritin at or below 50 ug/L. A 12-week trial in 198 menstruating women recruited on ferritin under 50 ug/L and haemoglobin above 12.0 g/dL replicated the fatigue finding (-47.7% versus -28.8%, difference -18.9%, 95% CI -34.5 to -3.2, P=0.02) but showed no effect on quality of life (P=0.2), depression (P=0.97) or anxiety (P=0.5). A separate 2025 meta-analysis of 18 studies (1,408 participants) independently found improved fatigue in the randomised trials (d=0.34) but no benefit for attention or depression, and reported that supplementation effects were absent once participants who were not iron-deficient were excluded. Against all of this, 154 iron-deficient non-anaemic female blood donors given four weeks of ferrous sulfate showed no fatigue benefit (change -0.15 points, 95% CI -0.9 to 0.6, P=0.697) despite haemoglobin rising 5.2 g/L and ferritin 14.8 ng/mL. Ferritin is the gate that decides who is in the responding group: a Cochrane diagnostic accuracy review found that at a 30 ug/L threshold among adults presenting for medical care, ferritin was 79% sensitive (95% CI 58-91%) and 98% specific (95% CI 91-100%) for iron deficiency on low-certainty evidence, with insufficient evidence to judge its performance when screening people without symptoms.

Doses studied: 80 mg/day elemental iron as oral ferrous sulfate for 4-12 weeks in the two anchoring trials in non-anaemic women. The 12-week trial recruited on serum ferritin below 50 ug/L with haemoglobin above 12.0 g/dL; the 4-week trial recruited fatigued women with no ferritin entry criterion, and the restriction to ferritin at or below 50 ug/L came from a subgroup analysis, not from how participants were selected.

Why this grade: Two independent meta-analyses and multiple placebo-controlled RCTs show a consistent reduction in self-reported fatigue when iron deficiency is documented, and iron replacement is guideline first-line care for iron-deficiency anaemia. The grade applies only to people with confirmed low iron. Three honest limits keep it from being unqualified: the same meta-analysis found no improvement in objective physical capacity, a well-conducted trial in iron-deficient blood donors found no fatigue benefit at all, and a 2025 meta-analysis found the benefit disappeared once participants who were not iron-deficient were excluded from the analysis.
