Weight loss (including type 2 diabetes remission)

Strong evidence · for high blood pressure Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For high blood pressure

Losing weight genuinely lowers blood pressure, and the relationship is roughly proportional: about one point off the top number for every kilogram lost. That makes it one of the biggest non-drug levers there is — but the trials also show the benefit shrinks as weight comes back, and a large study in people with diabetes found that losing weight through lifestyle change improved blood pressure control without reducing heart attacks or strokes. So it is worth doing, and worth doing in a way you can sustain.

Safety

Weight loss itself is generally safe when achieved gradually through diet and activity, but the Cochrane review of weight-loss trials in hypertension found that none of the included trials reported usable adverse-event data, so harms in this specific population are poorly characterised. Very-low-calorie and rapid-loss approaches carry risks of gallstones, electrolyte disturbance and loss of lean muscle, and are best done with clinical supervision. Bariatric surgery achieves the largest blood pressure effects but carries operative risk and lifelong nutritional consequences, including the need for vitamin and mineral supplementation and monitoring.

Who should be careful: Intentional weight loss is not appropriate in pregnancy, in people who are underweight or frail, or in people with active eating disorders — for whom weight-loss framing can be actively harmful. Older adults at risk of sarcopenia should pair any weight loss with resistance training and adequate protein rather than pursuing weight loss alone.

Interactions: Blood pressure falls as weight falls, and the effect is larger in people already on antihypertensives, which means they can become over-treated: dizziness on standing, falls and fainting are the warning signs, and doses often need reducing under clinician supervision rather than being continued unchanged. The same applies to insulin and sulfonylureas, where weight loss raises hypoglycaemia risk. Do not stop or reduce blood pressure medication on your own during weight loss.

What the evidence says

A meta-analysis of 25 randomised trials (4,874 participants) found that a net weight reduction of 5.1 kg lowered systolic BP by 4.44 mmHg (95% CI -5.93 to -2.95) and diastolic by 3.57 mmHg, equating to -1.05/-0.92 mmHg per kilogram lost, with larger reductions where average loss exceeded 5 kg (-6.63/-5.12 mmHg) and a significantly larger diastolic effect in people already on antihypertensive drugs. A Cochrane review restricted to people with primary hypertension (8 RCTs, 2,100 participants) found -4.5 mmHg systolic and -3.2 mmHg diastolic for a mean 4.0 kg loss; it rated the blood pressure evidence low quality and noted that no included study reported mortality, but one included RCT did find a weight-reducing diet lowered a combined endpoint of reinstating antihypertensive therapy plus severe cardiovascular complications (hazard ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.87). In TOHP II (2,382 overweight adults with high-normal BP), the weight-loss arm was 3.7/2.7 mmHg below usual care at 6 months but only 1.3/0.9 mmHg below at 36 months as weight was partly regained — yet hypertension incidence through 48 months was still significantly lower in every active arm (relative risks 0.78-0.82). Look AHEAD randomised 5,145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes to intensive lifestyle weight loss and was stopped early for futility at a median 9.6 years: despite greater weight loss and better risk factors, the cardiovascular composite showed a hazard ratio of 0.95 (95% CI 0.83-1.09, P=0.51). Blood pressure itself did respond in that trial — a comparative analysis of Look AHEAD and ACCORD participants found the lifestyle arm achieved higher rates of systolic BP control than education and support over four years (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.18-1.81). At the largest achievable magnitude, the 5-year GATEWAY randomised trial found gastric bypass produced hypertension remission in 46.9% of patients versus 2.4% on medical therapy alone.

Doses studied: Meta-analysed trials achieved a mean net loss of 5.1 kg; Cochrane trials in people with primary hypertension achieved a mean 4.0 kg. TOHP II counselling targeted desirable weight or a reduction of 4.5 kg or more, and achieved 4.3-4.5 kg at 6 months falling to about 2 kg at 36 months. Look AHEAD achieved 8.6% weight loss at 1 year and 6.0% at study end. GATEWAY gastric bypass took BMI from 36.9 at baseline to 28.0 kg/m2 at 5 years.

Why this grade: Multiple meta-analyses of randomised trials show a consistent, dose-related blood pressure reduction of roughly 1 mmHg systolic per kilogram lost, weight loss produced the largest sustained effect of any lifestyle intervention in the review restricted to trials of six months or longer, and the Cochrane review notes that all major guidelines for antihypertensive therapy recommend it. Graded strong for blood pressure lowering, with three qualifications carried in the summaries: the Cochrane pooling rates the blood pressure evidence low quality, the effect attenuates substantially as weight is regained, and the largest cardiovascular outcome trial of intentional weight loss (Look AHEAD) was null for heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular death even though it did improve blood pressure control.

Sources (7)
  1. Influence of weight reduction on blood pressure: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
    Neter JE et al., 2003 · PMID 12975389 · positive

    25 RCTs (34 strata), 4,874 participants. Net weight reduction of 5.1 kg lowered systolic BP 4.44 mmHg (95% CI -5.93 to -2.95) and diastolic 3.57 mmHg (95% CI -4.88 to -2.25); per kilogram, -1.05 mmHg systolic and -0.92 mmHg diastolic. Populations losing more than 5 kg fell -6.63/-5.12 mmHg. The diastolic effect was significantly larger in populations taking antihypertensive drugs (-5.31 mmHg) than in untreated populations (-2.91 mmHg).

  2. Long-term effects of weight-reducing diets in people with hypertension (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews)
    Semlitsch T et al., 2016 · PMID 26934541 · mixed

    8 RCTs, 2,100 participants with primary hypertension; the review opens by noting that all major guidelines for antihypertensive therapy recommend weight loss. Low-quality evidence for -4.5 mmHg systolic (95% CI -7.2 to -1.8) and -3.2 mmHg diastolic (95% CI -4.8 to -1.5); moderate-quality evidence for -4.0 kg weight loss. No study included mortality as a predefined outcome and no useful adverse-event data were reported, so whether weight loss reduces mortality or morbidity remains unknown; however one included RCT found weight-reducing diet lowered a combined endpoint of reinstating antihypertensive therapy plus severe cardiovascular complications, hazard ratio 0.70 (95% CI 0.57 to 0.87).

  3. Effects of weight loss and sodium reduction intervention on blood pressure and hypertension incidence in overweight people with high-normal blood pressure: the Trials of Hypertension Prevention, phase II
    Trials of Hypertension Prevention Collaborative Research Group, 1997 · PMID 9080920 · mixed

    2,382 overweight adults aged 30-54 with diastolic BP 83-89 mmHg. Weight fell 4.3-4.5 kg at 6 months but only about 2 kg at 36 months. The weight-loss arm was 3.7/2.7 mmHg below usual care at 6 months and only 1.3/0.9 mmHg below at 36 months. Hypertension incidence through 48 months was nonetheless significantly lower in each active intervention arm (average relative risks 0.78-0.82).

  4. Cardiovascular effects of intensive lifestyle intervention in type 2 diabetes (Look AHEAD)
    Wing RR et al. (Look AHEAD Research Group), 2013 · PMID 23796131 · null

    5,145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes across 16 US centres, stopped for futility at median 9.6 years. Weight loss was greater with intensive lifestyle intervention (8.6% vs 0.7% at 1 year; 6.0% vs 3.5% at study end) and all cardiovascular risk factors except LDL improved, but the primary composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke or hospitalisation for angina showed a hazard ratio of 0.95 (95% CI 0.83-1.09, P=0.51).

  5. Systolic Blood Pressure Control Among Individuals With Type 2 Diabetes: A Comparative Effectiveness Analysis of Three Interventions
    Espeland MA et al., 2015 · PMID 25666468 · positive

    480 Look AHEAD and 1,129 ACCORD participants with baseline systolic BP 130-159 mmHg who met entry criteria for both trials. Across 4 years the intensive lifestyle (weight-loss) intervention produced higher rates of systolic BP below 140 mmHg than education and support (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.18-1.81), comparable to frequent goal-based monitoring with pharmacotherapy (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.16-1.97). Confirms Look AHEAD's null was for cardiovascular events, not for blood pressure control.

  6. Randomized Trial of Effect of Bariatric Surgery on Blood Pressure After 5 Years (GATEWAY)
    Schiavon CA et al., 2024 · PMID 38325988 · positive

    100 patients with obesity grade 1-2 and hypertension on at least 2 medications, baseline BMI 36.9. At 5 years, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass versus medical therapy: BMI 28.01 vs 36.40 kg/m2; at least 30% medication reduction in 80.7% vs 13.7% (RR 5.91, 95% CI 2.58-13.52); hypertension remission 46.9% vs 2.4% (RR 19.66, 95% CI 2.74-141.09); apparent resistant hypertension 0% vs 15.2%.

  7. Lowering blood pressure: a systematic review of sustained effects of non-pharmacological interventions
    Ebrahim S & Davey Smith G, 1998 · PMID 9923952 · mixed

    Across 8 weight-loss RCTs lasting at least six months, net systolic BP change was -5.2 mmHg (95% CI -8.3 to -2.0) in hypertensive and -2.8 mmHg (95% CI -3.9 to -1.8) in normotensive participants — the largest sustained effect of any lifestyle intervention reviewed. Authors nonetheless judged most trials to be of low methodological quality and the net changes to be probable overestimates.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Losing weight and keeping it off is the most powerful thing most people with early type 2 diabetes can do for their blood sugar. In a UK trial of a supervised low-calorie meal-replacement programme, nearly half had a normal HbA1c without any diabetes medication after a year, and the more weight lost, the higher the chance. But that is a blood-sugar result, not a proven heart or kidney result: in a large American trial, weight loss did not reduce heart attacks or strokes. Remission also fades as weight comes back — only about one in eight was still in remission at five years. These programmes are run with medical supervision precisely because a doctor has to adjust or withdraw diabetes and blood-pressure medicines as part of the plan. That is never something to do on your own.

Safety

Weight loss is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and a falling HbA1c is not by itself a reason to change a dose — every change in these trials was made by a prescriber. Known harms of rapid weight loss include gallstones and biliary colic (reported in DiRECT), constipation, headache, dizziness, hair thinning and loss of muscle mass; resistance exercise and adequate protein help preserve muscle. Weight regain is the expected trajectory without ongoing support, and remission is lost when weight returns. Overall serious adverse events were lower in DiRECT intervention participants than controls over 5 years.

Who should be careful: Very-low-calorie total diet replacement (about 800 kcal/day) must be medically supervised. It is not appropriate in pregnancy or breastfeeding, active eating disorders, type 1 diabetes, insulin-treated diabetes outside a specialist programme, recent cardiovascular events, significant kidney or liver disease, or frailty and sarcopenia in older adults. DiRECT excluded people on insulin, with diabetes duration over 6 years, aged over 65, or with BMI below 27, and the results do not extend to those groups.

Interactions: In DiRECT all glucose-lowering and blood-pressure drugs were stopped on day one by the clinical team as part of a supervised protocol. Do not stop or reduce any medication on your own. Continuing insulin or a sulfonylurea while sharply cutting intake causes hypoglycaemia; continuing an SGLT2 inhibitor on a very-low-calorie or very-low-carbohydrate diet raises the risk of ketoacidosis at near-normal glucose; continuing antihypertensives can cause dizziness and falls from low blood pressure as weight falls.

What the evidence says

Separate what is measured here. Weight, HbA1c and 'remission' (an HbA1c below 6.5% off all glucose-lowering drugs) are surrogates; heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure and death are outcomes, and on those the evidence is null or indirect. On the surrogates: in prediabetes, each kilogram lost in the DPP lifestyle arm (n=1079, 3.2 years) was associated with a 16% lower risk of diabetes (hazard ratio per 5 kg lost 0.42, 95% CI 0.35-0.51), weight loss being the dominant predictor after adjusting for diet and activity. In early type 2 diabetes (under 6 years, BMI 27-45, not on insulin), the DiRECT cluster RCT (306 recruited, 149 per group analysed by intention to treat) achieved mean weight loss of 10.0 kg versus 1.0 kg and remission in 46% versus 4% at 12 months (odds ratio 19.7, 95% CI 7.8-49.8), rising from 7% among those losing 0-5 kg to 34% at 5-10 kg, 57% at 10-15 kg and 86% at 15 kg or more; at 24 months remission was 36% versus 3% with a between-group HbA1c difference of -0.44 percentage points (95% CI -0.76 to -0.13) despite far less medication use (40% versus 84% on glucose-lowering drugs). DIADEM-I (n=158, ages 18-50, Qatar) replicated this with -6.08 kg net weight loss and 61% versus 12% remission at 12 months. Durability is the weak point: in the DiRECT 5-year extension only 13% of supported participants (11 of 85) remained in remission, and only 26% of those in remission at year 2 still were at year 5, although serious adverse events were less than half those in controls (4.8 versus 10.2 per 100 patient-years). On outcomes: in Look AHEAD (n=5145, established type 2 diabetes, mean duration 6 years), intensive lifestyle produced 8.6% weight loss at 1 year and greater HbA1c reduction but only 11.5% any remission at year 1 and 7.3% at year 4 (versus 2%), and over a median 9.6 years it did not reduce the cardiovascular composite (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83-1.09, P=0.51) — the trial was stopped for futility. A secondary analysis of that same trial reported 31% fewer cases of very-high-risk chronic kidney disease (0.63 versus 0.91 cases per 100 person-years, HR 0.69, 0.55-0.87), but the investigators state that albuminuria and eGFR were two among many secondary outcomes and were combined post hoc into that endpoint. Separately, participants who achieved any remission had a 33% lower rate of chronic kidney disease (HR 0.67, 0.52-0.87) and a 40% lower rate of the cardiovascular composite (HR 0.60, 0.47-0.79) than those who did not — an observational association among trial participants, not a randomised effect. In every one of these trials, medication was withdrawn or adjusted by the clinical team as part of the protocol.

Doses studied: DiRECT and DIADEM-I: total diet replacement at 825-853 kcal/day for 12-20 weeks, stepped food reintroduction over 2-8 weeks, then structured weight-maintenance support, with glucose-lowering and blood-pressure drugs withdrawn at the start by the clinical team; target 15 kg or more. Look AHEAD: calorie restriction plus increased physical activity aiming for at least 7% loss, achieving 8.6% at 1 year and 6.0% at study end. DPP: at least a 7% weight-loss goal, with risk reduction proportional to kilograms lost.

Why this grade: The randomised evidence for weight loss on blood-sugar outcomes is large and consistent: in prediabetes each kilogram lost in the DPP lifestyle arm carried a 16% lower risk of diabetes, and in early type 2 diabetes the DiRECT cluster RCT achieved remission in 46% at 1 year and 36% at 2 years while DIADEM-I reached 61% at 1 year. But remission is defined by an HbA1c threshold off medication — a surrogate state, not a prevented heart attack, stroke, amputation or death. The one adequately powered hard-outcome trial, Look AHEAD (n=5145), found no reduction in cardiovascular events (HR 0.95, 0.83-1.09) and was stopped for futility; its 31% reduction in very-high-risk chronic kidney disease comes from a composite that the investigators state was assembled post hoc from two of many secondary outcomes in that halted trial; and the 33% lower CKD and 40% lower cardiovascular rates among people who achieved remission are an observational association within the trial, not a randomised effect. Durability is the other limit: only 13% of DiRECT's supported participants were still in remission at 5 years. Consistent, sizeable, but surrogate-dominant with a null hard-outcome trial in the background — that is 'moderate', not 'strong'.

Sources (9)
  1. Effect of weight loss with lifestyle intervention on risk of diabetes.
    Hamman RF et al. (Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group), 2006 · PMID 16936160 · positive

    Analysis of the DPP intensive lifestyle arm, n=1079 adults aged 25-84 (mean 50.6 years, BMI 33.9), 3.2 years of follow-up. Weight loss was the dominant predictor of reduced diabetes incidence (hazard ratio per 5 kg lost 0.42, 95% CI 0.35-0.51, P<0.0001); for every kilogram lost there was a 16% reduction in risk after adjusting for changes in diet and activity. Among 495 participants who did not meet the weight-loss goal at year 1, those who met the physical activity goal had 44% lower diabetes incidence. Outcome is a glucose-defined diagnosis.

  2. Primary care-led weight management for remission of type 2 diabetes (DiRECT): an open-label, cluster-randomised trial.
    Lean ME et al., 2018 · PMID 29221645 · positive

    Cluster RCT in 49 UK practices; 306 adults recruited with type 2 diabetes of under 6 years and BMI 27-45, not on insulin; 149 per group analysed by intention to treat. At 12 months mean weight fell 10.0 kg versus 1.0 kg (adjusted difference -8.8 kg); 24% versus 0% lost 15 kg or more; remission (HbA1c below 6.5% off all antidiabetic drugs for at least 2 months) in 46% versus 4% (OR 19.7, 95% CI 7.8-49.8). Remission by weight change: 0% among those who gained weight, 7% with 0-5 kg loss, 34% with 5-10 kg, 57% with 10-15 kg, 86% with 15 kg or more. Nine serious adverse events in 7 of 157 intervention participants; two (biliary colic and abdominal pain, in the same person) were judged potentially related.

  3. Durability of a primary care-led weight-management intervention for remission of type 2 diabetes: 2-year results of the DiRECT open-label, cluster-randomised trial.
    Lean MEJ et al., 2019 · PMID 30852132 · positive

    At 24 months, 36% of intervention versus 3% of control participants were in remission (adjusted OR 25.82) and 11% versus 2% had maintained a loss of 15 kg or more; adjusted difference in weight change -5.4 kg and in HbA1c -0.44 percentage points (95% CI -0.76 to -0.13), with only 51 of 129 (40%) intervention versus 120 of 143 (84%) control participants on glucose-lowering drugs. Of those who maintained at least 10 kg loss, 29 of 45 (64%) were in remission. Serious adverse events in year 2 were 9 (intervention) versus 22 (control).

  4. 5-year follow-up of the randomised Diabetes Remission Clinical Trial (DiRECT) of continued support for weight loss maintenance in the UK: an extension study.
    Lean MEJ et al., 2024 · PMID 38423026 · mixed

    Of 149 original intervention participants, 95 continued low-intensity support to year 5. At 5 years the extension group (n=85) had mean weight loss of 6.1 kg and 11 of 85 (13%) were in remission; only 26% of those in remission at year 2 remained so at year 5. Original intervention versus control participants had more visits in remission (27% vs 4%) and off antidiabetic medication (51% vs 16%); serious adverse events were 4.8 versus 10.2 per 100 patient-years. Remission is substantially lost as weight is regained.

  5. Effect of intensive lifestyle intervention on bodyweight and glycaemia in early type 2 diabetes (DIADEM-I): an open-label, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial.
    Taheri S et al., 2020 · PMID 32445735 · positive

    RCT in Qatar, n=158 randomised (147 analysed), adults aged 18-50 with type 2 diabetes of 3 years or less and BMI 27 or more. At 12 months weight fell 11.98 kg versus 3.98 kg (adjusted difference -6.08 kg, 95% CI -8.37 to -3.79); 21% versus 1% lost more than 15%; diabetes remission in 61% versus 12% (OR 12.03, 5.17-28.03) and normoglycaemia in 33% versus 4%. Five serious adverse events occurred in four control participants and none in the intervention group. Surrogate outcomes at 12 months only.

  6. Association of an intensive lifestyle intervention with remission of type 2 diabetes.
    Gregg EW et al. (Look AHEAD), 2012 · PMID 23288372 · mixed

    Ancillary analysis of the Look AHEAD RCT, n=4503 US adults with type 2 diabetes and BMI 25 or higher. Intensive lifestyle produced net weight loss of -7.9% at year 1 and -3.9% at year 4. Any (partial or complete) remission was 11.5% at year 1 and 7.3% at year 4 versus 2.0% with support and education; continuous remission for 4 years was 3.5% versus 0.5%. The authors describe the absolute remission rates as modest.

  7. Cardiovascular effects of intensive lifestyle intervention in type 2 diabetes.
    Look AHEAD Research Group (Wing RR et al.), 2013 · PMID 23796131 · null

    RCT, n=5145 overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes, stopped early for futility at median 9.6 years. Weight loss was 8.6% versus 0.7% at 1 year and 6.0% versus 3.5% at study end, with greater HbA1c reduction in the intervention group, but the cardiovascular composite occurred at 1.83 versus 1.92 events per 100 person-years (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83-1.09, P=0.51): no reduction in cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke or hospitalisation for angina.

  8. Effect of a long-term behavioural weight loss intervention on nephropathy in overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes: a secondary analysis of the Look AHEAD randomised clinical trial.
    Look AHEAD Research Group, 2014 · PMID 25127483 · positive

    Secondary analysis of Look AHEAD, 2423 intervention and 2408 control participants, median 8.0 years. Incidence of very-high-risk chronic kidney disease was 0.63 per 100 person-years with intensive lifestyle versus 0.91 with support and education (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.55-0.87, P=0.0016), partly attributable to reductions in weight, HbA1c and systolic blood pressure. Important caveat stated by the investigators: albuminuria and eGFR were two among many secondary outcomes and were combined post hoc into this endpoint, in a trial terminated early because its cardiovascular primary outcome was null.

  9. Impact of remission from type 2 diabetes on long-term health outcomes: findings from the Look AHEAD study.
    Gregg EW et al., 2024 · PMID 38233592 · positive

    Observational analysis within the Look AHEAD RCT (4402 participants for cardiovascular disease, 4132 for chronic kidney disease; mean age 59, diabetes duration 6 years, BMI 35.8). Participants with any evidence of remission during follow-up had a 33% lower rate of chronic kidney disease (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52-0.87) and a 40% lower rate of the composite cardiovascular measure (HR 0.60, 0.47-0.79), adjusted for HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, cardiovascular history, diabetes duration and trial arm. This is an association between an achieved state and later outcomes, not a randomised comparison, and the authors note it may be driven by accompanying improvements in weight, fitness, HbA1c and LDL cholesterol.
