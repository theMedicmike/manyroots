Conventional medicine

Acetaminophen (paracetamol)

Not supported by evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is the one that looks worst under the microscope. Large, high-quality reviews found it works no better than a dummy pill for low back pain, and for hip or knee arthritis the improvement is real but far too small for most people to actually feel. It is still one of the gentler options at normal doses, so some people use it, but it is fair to say the research does not support relying on it for long-term musculoskeletal pain.

Safety

At standard doses acetaminophen is generally well tolerated: pooled randomised data show overall adverse event rates indistinguishable from placebo (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.11). The main established hazard is hepatotoxicity, and randomised trials found abnormal liver function tests nearly four times more common than with placebo (RR 3.79, 95% CI 1.94 to 7.39), though the clinical significance of those transient enzyme changes is uncertain. Observational cohorts show dose-response signals for mortality, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal and renal harm at the upper end of standard analgesic dosing. The most common real-world danger is accidental overdose from stacking products: many combination cold, flu, sleep and opioid preparations contain acetaminophen, and totals add up quickly. Do not exceed the daily maximum on the label, and count every source.

Who should be careful: Should not be used by people with severe hepatic impairment or active decompensated liver disease. People with chronic liver disease, chronic heavy alcohol use, low body weight, malnutrition, fasting states, or chronic kidney disease need a reduced maximum daily dose and should confirm it with a clinician. Avoid stacking any second product containing acetaminophen or paracetamol, including opioid combination tablets and over-the-counter cold and flu remedies.

Interactions: Regular acetaminophen can raise INR in people taking warfarin, requiring closer monitoring. Chronic alcohol use and enzyme-inducing drugs such as carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin and isoniazid increase the risk of liver injury at a given dose. Because acetaminophen is present in so many combination products, the most important 'interaction' to check is duplication with another acetaminophen-containing medicine.

What the evidence says

Evidence for acetaminophen in chronic musculoskeletal pain is adequate and largely null. A BMJ systematic review of 13 randomised placebo-controlled trials rated as high quality found acetaminophen ineffective for low back pain — pain intensity weighted mean difference -0.5 (95% CI -2.9 to 1.9) on a 0-100 scale, disability 0.4 (95% CI -1.7 to 2.5) — and for hip or knee osteoarthritis a statistically significant but not clinically important pain effect of -3.7 (95% CI -5.5 to -1.9). A subsequent Cochrane review of 10 trials (3541 participants) confirmed this with high-quality evidence: pain improved by 3.23 points (95% CI 1.02 to 5.43) on a 0-100 scale against a minimal clinically important difference of 9, and physical function by 2.9 points (95% CI 0.95 to 4.89) against a threshold of 10, with no difference between lower and higher doses. A Cochrane review specific to low back pain found high-quality evidence of no difference from placebo in acute low back pain at 1, 2, 4 and 12 weeks, and rated evidence for chronic low back pain as very low quality because the only relevant trial has been retracted. Overall adverse event rates match placebo (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.11), but abnormal liver function tests were nearly four times more common (RR 3.79, 95% CI 1.94 to 7.39), and observational cohort data show dose-response signals for mortality, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal and renal outcomes.

Doses studied: 1.95 to 4 g/day across trials, most commonly 3 to 4 g/day, with most trials following participants for about three months. Subgroup analysis found no difference in effect between 3.0 g/day or less and 3.9 g/day or more, so the null result is not explained by underdosing.

Why this grade: This is the clearest case in the group of adequate evidence returning a null or clinically meaningless result. Two independent high-quality syntheses — a BMJ meta-analysis and a Cochrane review — both rated their evidence 'high quality' and both concluded acetaminophen is ineffective for low back pain and provides only minimal, sub-threshold benefit in hip and knee osteoarthritis. In the Cochrane review the pain improvement was 3.23 points on a 0-100 scale against a minimal clinically important difference of 9. Both author groups explicitly called for reconsideration of guidelines recommending it first-line, and the ACR downgraded it to a conditional recommendation. The label 'not_supported' refers to efficacy as a standalone analgesic for this condition; it does not mean the drug is dangerous at standard doses, and its low absolute harm profile is why it still appears in guidelines at all.

  1. Efficacy and safety of paracetamol for spinal pain and osteoarthritis: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised placebo controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisMachado GC, Maher CG, Ferreira PH, et al., 2015 (BMJ) · PMID 25828856 · null

    12 reports covering 13 randomised trials. High-quality evidence that paracetamol is ineffective for low back pain: pain -0.5 (95% CI -2.9 to 1.9), disability 0.4 (95% CI -1.7 to 2.5), quality of life 0.4 (95% CI -0.9 to 1.7) on 0-100 scales. For hip or knee osteoarthritis, a significant but not clinically important effect on pain (-3.7, 95% CI -5.5 to -1.9) and disability (-2.9, 95% CI -4.9 to -0.9). Patients on paracetamol were nearly four times more likely to have abnormal liver function tests (RR 3.8, 95% CI 1.9 to 7.4). Authors called for reconsideration of guideline recommendations.

  2. Paracetamol versus placebo for knee and hip osteoarthritis.
    Meta-analysisLeopoldino AO, Machado GC, Ferreira PH, et al., 2019 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 30801133 · mixed

    10 randomised placebo-controlled trials, 3541 participants, doses 1.95-4 g/day, most followed for three months. High-quality evidence of no clinically important improvement: pain 3.23 points better (95% CI 1.02 to 5.43) on a 0-100 scale against a minimal clinically important difference of 9, and physical function 2.9 points better (95% CI 0.95 to 4.89) against a threshold of 10. Adverse events were similar (325 vs 328 per 1000; RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.11) but abnormal liver function was more common (RR 3.79, 95% CI 1.94 to 7.39). Effects did not differ by dose. The authors state their results call for reconsideration of first-line recommendations.

  3. Paracetamol for low back pain.
    Meta-analysisSaragiotto BT, Machado GC, Ferreira ML, Pinheiro MB, Abdel Shaheed C, Maher CG, 2016 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 27271789 · null

    3 trials, 1825 participants. High-quality evidence of no difference between paracetamol 4 g/day and placebo in acute low back pain at 1, 2, 4 and 12 weeks for pain and disability, and no effect on quality of life, function, global impression of recovery or sleep quality. For chronic low back pain, only very low-quality evidence from a single trial that has since been retracted; no trials were found in subacute low back pain.

  4. Paracetamol: not as safe as we thought? A systematic literature review of observational studies.
    Systematic reviewRoberts E, Delgado Nunes V, Buckner S, et al., 2016 (Ann Rheum Dis) · PMID 25732175 · negative

    8 cohort studies at standard analgesic doses in general adult populations. Dose-response relationships were seen across most endpoints: relative rate of mortality rising to 1.63 (95% CI 1.58 to 1.68), all cardiovascular adverse events rising to a risk ratio of 1.68 (95% CI 1.10 to 2.57), gastrointestinal events or bleeds to 1.49 (95% CI 1.34 to 1.66), and odds of a 30% or greater fall in eGFR to 2.19 (95% CI 1.4 to 3.43). Authors caution that channelling bias may have had an important impact, but that the dose-response pattern suggests considerable toxicity at the upper end of standard dosing.

  5. 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Osteoarthritis of the Hand, Hip, and Knee.
    GuidelineKolasinski SL, Neogi T, Hochberg MC, et al., 2020 (Arthritis Care Res) · PMID 31908149 · mixed

    Acetaminophen received only a conditional recommendation for hand, hip and knee osteoarthritis, alongside duloxetine and tramadol — in contrast to the strong recommendations given to exercise, weight loss, oral NSAIDs and topical NSAIDs for knee OA. This represents a downgrade from acetaminophen's historic position as first-line analgesic.
