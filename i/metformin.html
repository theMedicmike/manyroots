Metformin

Strong evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Metformin is the oldest, cheapest and best-studied diabetes tablet, and it reliably brings blood sugar numbers down in people who already have type 2 diabetes. It can also delay a diabetes diagnosis in people with prediabetes, though a structured diet-and-exercise programme did about twice as well in the biggest trial. Here is the honest part most people are not told: the evidence that it lowers your blood sugar number is strong, but the evidence that it makes you live longer or prevents heart attacks rests almost entirely on one group of 753 overweight patients in a single British trial from 1998. In that group the benefit was real and still visible ten years after the trial ended - fewer heart attacks and fewer deaths - but no other trial has reproduced it, and every later attempt, including a 21-year follow-up and a Cochrane review, has come back null. Read that as an argument about heart attacks and lifespan - not as a reason to think the blood sugar number does not matter. Lowering blood sugar is the one thing here with randomised proof behind it for the complications people fear most: in UKPDS 33 - a trial that used older blood-sugar drugs, a sulphonylurea or insulin, not metformin - holding HbA1c at 7.0% instead of 7.9% cut microvascular complications - the small-vessel damage behind sight loss and kidney failure - by 25% (95% CI 7-40%, p=0.0099, 3,867 newly diagnosed patients over 10 years; PMID 9742976). That is why your team treats the number as a target, and why metformin is a well-tolerated, cheap, guideline-first-line tablet on strong blood-sugar evidence rather than the proven life-extender its reputation suggests. It is generally easy to take apart from stomach upset, but it can quietly lower vitamin B12 over years. If you are prescribed it, do not stop or change the dose on your own - and if your kidney function has changed, that is a conversation with your prescriber about the dose, not a reason to stop by yourself.

Safety

Gastrointestinal upset (nausea, diarrhoea) is the most common reason people stop. Ask your prescriber for a vitamin B12 level if you have taken metformin for more than a few years, particularly with tingling feet or anaemia: long-term use roughly doubled low or borderline-low B12 in DPPOS and each year of use raised the odds 13% (PMID 26900641). The feared harm, lactic acidosis, was not seen in any of 70,490 patient-years of prospective metformin data, with an upper 95% bound of 4.3 cases per 100,000 patient-years (PMID 20393934). Modest weight loss on metformin was maintained at 10 years in DPPOS (PMID 19878986). Nothing on this page is a reason to stop, reduce or skip a prescribed glucose-lowering medicine, or to substitute a supplement or food for one; changes to a prescription belong with the clinician who wrote it.

Who should be careful: Kidney function sets the limits, and the thresholds are specific numbers, not a general ban. Since the FDA's 2016 label change these are expressed as eGFR rather than creatinine: metformin is contraindicated below an eGFR of 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; 30-44 is the band where safety is classed as indeterminate rather than established; and it is regarded as likely safe at 45 and above (PMID 26307607, 32660971). Outcome data line up with those bands - in a systematic review of metformin in reduced renal function, the seven retrospective studies that examined mortality showed lower overall mortality at an eGFR of 45 or above but not below 45, and the eight that evaluated acidosis showed no increase in risk except below an eGFR of 30, where the hazard ratio was 1.97 (95% CI 1.03-3.77); separately, nine small prospective studies in significantly impaired renal function turned up only one case of clinically apparent lactic acidosis (PMID 34009711). Within the 30-44 band the published evidence supports cautious use with a reduced dose and careful follow-up of kidney function rather than an automatic stop (PMID 25536258). The older creatinine-based rule excluded people unnecessarily, particularly Black patients and men, whose creatinine runs higher independently of true kidney function (PMID 32660971). Other labelled cautions are conditions that impair oxygen delivery or drug clearance - significant liver, heart or lung disease (PMID 20393934) - and diarrhoea affects about 10% (PMID 28105986). Do not act on these numbers yourself: ask your prescriber what your latest eGFR is and what it means for your dose. Metformin does not replace insulin and is not a treatment for type 1 diabetes. UKPDS reported an unexplained 96% increase in diabetes-related death when metformin was added early to a sulphonylurea; a larger epidemiological analysis of 4,416 patients in the same paper did not confirm it, and the question has never been fully resolved (PMID 9742977).

Interactions: Combining with sulphonylureas or insulin adds their hypoglycaemia risk; metformin alone caused fewer hypoglycaemic attacks than insulin or sulphonylureas in UKPDS 34 (PMID 9742977). Standard labelling advises pausing metformin around iodinated contrast imaging and with heavy alcohol use because both stress kidney function; this is label guidance rather than trial evidence, and any pause should come from the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

Two classes of evidence must be kept apart. On SURROGATES the record is strong and consistent: pooled across 35 trials, metformin monotherapy lowered HbA1c by 1.12 percentage points versus placebo (95% CI 0.92-1.32), by 0.95 added to oral therapy and by 0.60 added to insulin, with greater reductions at higher doses and no significant increase in side effects (PMID 22275444). One bridge between the two classes has to be stated precisely, because it is what makes the surrogate worth treating at all: glucose lowering itself has randomised evidence for MICROVASCULAR complications. In UKPDS 33 (3,867 newly diagnosed patients, 10 years), an intensive policy holding median HbA1c at 7.0% versus 7.9% cut any diabetes-related endpoint by 12% (95% CI 1-21%, p=0.029), driven mainly by a 25% reduction in microvascular endpoints including the need for retinal photocoagulation (7-40%, p=0.0099); all-cause mortality was 6% lower and not significant, and macrovascular disease was not reduced (PMID 9742976). That intensive arm used a sulphonylurea or insulin, not metformin, so this is evidence that the NUMBER is worth lowering - it is not evidence that metformin lowers hard outcomes. On progression to a diabetes DIAGNOSIS, the Diabetes Prevention Program (3,234 adults with prediabetes, mean 2.8 years) found metformin 850 mg twice daily reduced incidence by 31% (17-43%) while the intensive lifestyle programme reduced it by 58% (48-66%) and was significantly more effective, with 13.9 people needing metformin for 3 years to prevent one case versus 6.9 for lifestyle (PMID 11832527); at 10 years cumulative incidence was 18% lower with metformin and 34% lower with lifestyle (PMID 19878986), and the 2019 Cochrane review reproduced the pattern - metformin beat diet and exercise with or without placebo (RR 0.50, 0.38-0.65; moderate quality) but did not beat intensive diet and exercise (RR 0.80, 0.47-1.37) and added nothing on top of it (RR 0.55, 0.10-2.92; very low quality), with mortality and vascular outcome data sparse or missing (PMID 31794067). On HARD OUTCOMES the record is much weaker than metformin's reputation: UKPDS 34 (753 overweight, newly diagnosed patients, median 10.7 years, median HbA1c 7.4% vs 8.0%) reported 32% fewer diabetes-related endpoints and 36% lower all-cause mortality (PMID 9742977), but this has never been reproduced - the closest thing to durability comes from the same cohort rather than a new one: in 10 years of post-trial monitoring (UKPDS 80, PMID 18784090), with the between-group HbA1c difference lost within the first year, the metformin group retained significant risk reductions for any diabetes-related endpoint (21%, P=0.01), myocardial infarction (33%, P=0.005) and death from any cause (27%, P=0.002), a legacy effect within the original UKPDS 34 randomisation of 753 overweight patients and not an independent replication - and a meta-analysis of 13 trials isolating metformin against diet, lifestyle or placebo found no significant effect on all-cause mortality (RR 0.96, 0.84-1.09), cardiovascular death (0.97, 0.80-1.16) or myocardial infarction (0.89, 0.75-1.06), with UKPDS contributing 52-71% of the weight (PMID 28770324); 21-year DPP/DPPOS follow-up found no reduction in all-cause (HR 0.99, 0.79-1.25), cancer (1.04) or cardiovascular (1.08) mortality (PMID 34697033); 25-year follow-up found lifestyle, but not metformin (HR 0.91, 0.78-1.07), lowered later multimorbidity (PMID 42295772); and a 2020 Cochrane review of 18 RCTs (n=10,680) concluded there is no clear evidence that metformin monotherapy influences patient-important outcomes, rating every one very low certainty (PMID 32501595). Harms are well characterised: long-term use roughly doubled low-or-borderline B12 at 5 years (19.1% vs 9.5%; OR 1.13 per year of use) (PMID 26900641), while 347 comparative studies covering 70,490 patient-years recorded no cases of lactic acidosis, the harm it is most famous for (PMID 20393934).

Doses studied: 850 mg twice daily in the DPP/DPPOS prevention trials (PMID 11832527, 19878986, 26900641, 34697033). In treatment trials metformin was titrated to glucose targets; a dose-comparison meta-analysis found significantly greater HbA1c reduction with higher versus lower doses without a significant increase in side effects (PMID 22275444; specific milligram ranges not reported in the abstract). Dosing is set by a prescriber and is not something to adjust independently.

Why this grade: Graded strong on the rubric's guideline clause, not on outcome data. Metformin is described as the recommended first-line glucose-lowering drug for type 2 diabetes in the peer-reviewed sources reviewed here (PMID 22275444, 32501595, 28105986), and multiple RCTs plus a Cochrane review agree it lowers HbA1c and delays progression from prediabetes to diagnosed diabetes. The hard-outcome file is the weakest part of the record and is stated as such throughout: the mortality benefit rests on one arm of UKPDS (and persisted in that same arm's 10-year post-trial follow-up, UKPDS 80, PMID 18784090) and has not been reproduced in a pooled placebo-controlled meta-analysis (PMID 28770324), in 21-year DPP mortality follow-up (PMID 34697033), or in 25-year multimorbidity follow-up (PMID 42295772), and a 2020 Cochrane review found no clear evidence of any effect on patient-important outcomes (PMID 32501595). HbA1c, fasting glucose and weight are surrogate markers; a lower number is not a prevented heart attack, stroke, amputation, kidney failure or death. For prevention, metformin is clearly beaten by intensive lifestyle change (PMID 11832527, 31794067).

Sources (14)
  1. Reduction in the incidence of type 2 diabetes with lifestyle intervention or metformin.
    Knowler WC et al. (Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group), 2002 · PMID 11832527 · positive

    Prediabetes, n=3,234, mean 2.8 years: diabetes incidence 11.0, 7.8 and 4.8 cases per 100 person-years on placebo, metformin and lifestyle. Metformin reduced incidence 31% (95% CI 17-43%); lifestyle reduced it 58% (48-66%) and was significantly more effective than metformin. To prevent one case over 3 years, 13.9 people would need metformin versus 6.9 for the lifestyle programme.

  2. Effect of intensive blood-glucose control with metformin on complications in overweight patients with type 2 diabetes (UKPDS 34).
    UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) Group, 1998 · PMID 9742977 · positive

    Established T2D, 753 overweight newly diagnosed patients, median 10.7 years: median HbA1c 7.4% with metformin vs 8.0% conventional. Risk reductions of 32% (13-47%) for any diabetes-related endpoint, 42% (9-63%) for diabetes-related death and 36% (9-55%) for all-cause mortality. Caution: in a supplementary trial, early addition of metformin to a sulphonylurea was associated with a 96% (2-275%) increase in diabetes-related death, though an epidemiological analysis of 4,416 patients in the same paper did not confirm it.

  3. 10-year follow-up of intensive glucose control in type 2 diabetes.
    Holman RR et al. (UKPDS 80), 2008 · PMID 18784090 · positive

    HARD OUTCOME, POSITIVE - BUT NOT A REPLICATION. Post-trial monitoring of the UKPDS cohort for 10 further years, analysed by intention to treat according to the original randomisation. Between-group HbA1c differences were lost after the first year, yet in the metformin group significant risk reductions persisted for any diabetes-related endpoint (21%, P=0.01), myocardial infarction (33%, P=0.005) and death from any cause (27%, P=0.002). This is a legacy effect within the same overweight patients randomised in UKPDS 34, not an independent trial, and it remains the strongest surviving hard-outcome datapoint for metformin.

  4. Intensive blood-glucose control with sulphonylureas or insulin compared with conventional treatment and risk of complications in patients with type 2 diabetes (UKPDS 33).
    UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) Group, 1998 · PMID 9742976 · mixed

    WHY THE SURROGATE IS WORTH TREATING - and its limit. 3,867 newly diagnosed patients over 10 years; intensive policy achieved median HbA1c 7.0% vs 7.9% conventional. Any diabetes-related endpoint fell 12% (95% CI 1-21%, p=0.029), driven mainly by a 25% reduction in microvascular endpoints including the need for retinal photocoagulation (7-40%, p=0.0099). Diabetes-related death fell 10% (NS) and all-cause mortality 6% (NS); macrovascular disease was not reduced. Intensive treatment caused more hypoglycaemia and more weight gain. Note the intensive arm used a sulphonylurea or insulin, NOT metformin: this is randomised evidence that lowering the glucose number reduces small-vessel complications, not evidence about metformin specifically.

  5. Quantifying the effect of metformin treatment and dose on glycemic control.
    Hirst JA et al., 2012 · PMID 22275444 · positive

    SURROGATE endpoint only. Meta-analysis of 35 trials: metformin monotherapy lowered HbA1c by 1.12 percentage points vs placebo (95% CI 0.92-1.32); added to oral therapy 0.95 (0.77-1.13); added to insulin 0.60 (0.30-0.91). Higher doses gave significantly greater reduction with no significant increase in side effects. The paper states metformin is the first-line oral medication recommended for glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes.

  6. Metformin for prevention or delay of type 2 diabetes mellitus and its associated complications in persons at increased risk for the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Madsen KS et al. (Cochrane), 2019 · PMID 31794067 · mixed

    20 RCTs, 6,774 people at increased risk (one trial contributed 48%). Versus diet and exercise with or without placebo, metformin reduced diabetes incidence (RR 0.50, 0.38-0.65; moderate-quality). Versus intensive diet and exercise it did not (RR 0.80, 0.47-1.37; moderate-quality), and adding metformin to intensive lifestyle gave no advantage (RR 0.55, 0.10-2.92; very low quality). All-cause mortality versus diet and exercise was RR 1.11 (0.41-3.01). Data on mortality, macrovascular and microvascular complications and quality of life were sparse or missing.

  7. Impact of metformin on cardiovascular disease: a meta-analysis of randomised trials among people with type 2 diabetes.
    Griffin SJ et al., 2017 · PMID 28770324 · null

    13 trials (2,079 people allocated metformin) isolating metformin against diet, lifestyle or placebo in T2D: all-cause mortality RR 0.96 (0.84-1.09), cardiovascular death 0.97 (0.80-1.16), myocardial infarction 0.89 (0.75-1.06), stroke 1.04 (0.73-1.48), peripheral vascular disease 0.81 (0.50-1.31). None reached statistical significance; UKPDS contributed 52.3-70.5% of the weight. The authors conclude uncertainty remains about whether metformin reduces cardiovascular risk.

  8. Effect of Metformin and Lifestyle Interventions on Mortality in the Diabetes Prevention Program and Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study.
    Lee CG et al., 2021 · PMID 34697033 · null

    HARD OUTCOME, NULL. 3,234 adults at high risk for T2D followed a median of 21 years; 453 died (cancer leading cause, n=170; cardiovascular disease n=131). Versus placebo, metformin did not influence all-cause mortality (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.79-1.25), cancer mortality (1.04, 0.72-1.52) or cardiovascular mortality (1.08, 0.70-1.66). Lifestyle modification was also null for all three. Authors conclude that although both strategies prevented diabetes, neither reduced mortality.

  9. Metformin monotherapy for adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Gnesin F et al. (Cochrane), 2020 · PMID 32501595 · null

    HARD OUTCOMES, NULL/UNCERTAIN. 18 RCTs with multiple study arms (n=10,680), treatment duration 1 to 10.7 years; no trial at low risk of bias in all domains. Across comparisons with insulin, sulphonylureas, thiazolidinediones, DPP-4 inhibitors, a GLP-1 analogue, meglitinide and no intervention, there was no substantial difference in all-cause mortality, serious adverse events, cardiovascular mortality, non-fatal myocardial infarction or non-fatal stroke, with very low-certainty evidence throughout. Authors' conclusion: no clear evidence whether metformin monotherapy influences patient-important outcomes. The review nonetheless notes metformin remains the recommended first-line glucose-lowering drug.

  10. Long-term Metformin Use and Vitamin B12 Deficiency in the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study.
    Aroda VR et al., 2016 · PMID 26900641 · negative

    HARM. DPP/DPPOS, metformin 850 mg twice daily vs placebo: low B12 (203 pg/mL or less) 4.3% vs 2.3% at 5 years (P=0.02) but not significantly different at 13 years (7.4 vs 5.4%). Combined low and borderline-low B12 19.1% vs 9.5% at 5 years and 20.3% vs 15.6% at 13 years. Each year of metformin use raised the odds of B12 deficiency 13% (OR 1.13, 1.06-1.20). Anaemia was more prevalent on metformin; neuropathy was more prevalent in metformin users with low B12. Authors recommend routine B12 testing be considered.

  11. Potential Impact of Prescribing Metformin According to eGFR Rather Than Serum Creatinine.
    Tuot DS et al., 2015 · PMID 26307607 · mixed

    SAFETY THRESHOLDS. Analysis of 3,902 US adults with diabetes (NHANES 1999-2010) comparing creatinine-based metformin eligibility with eGFR categories: likely safe at eGFR 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or above, indeterminate at 30-44, contraindicated below 30. Using eGFR or creatinine clearance instead of serum creatinine expanded the population for whom metformin is likely safe, particularly among non-Hispanic Black adults and men, and also identified a larger group in the indeterminate 30-44 band. This is the source of the three eGFR bands quoted in the safety section.

  12. The FDA Metformin Label Change and Racial and Sex Disparities in Metformin Prescription among Patients with CKD.
    Shin JI et al., 2020 · PMID 32660971 · positive

    LABEL CHANGE AND EQUITY. Confirms the FDA changed metformin contraindications in 2016 from serum-creatinine thresholds to eGFR thresholds, because race and sex affect creatinine independently of GFR. In 15,946 primary care patients, Black patients with eGFR 30-44 were prescribed metformin less often than White patients before the change (adjusted prevalence ratio 0.65, 95% CI 0.52-0.82); the gap narrowed after it (aPR 0.90, 0.74-1.09; p for interaction 0.04), with the same pattern for men versus women replicated across 36 cohorts (1,051,723 patients). Evidence that the old creatinine rule excluded people unnecessarily.

  13. Safety and effectiveness of metformin in patients with reduced renal function: A systematic review.
    Orloff J et al., 2021 · PMID 34009711 · mixed

    OUTCOME EVIDENCE BEHIND THE eGFR BANDS. Nine small prospective studies in significantly impaired renal function identified only one case of clinically apparent lactic acidosis. Among 13 larger retrospective studies, the seven that examined mortality showed reduced overall mortality at eGFR 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 or above but not below 45, and the eight that evaluated acidosis showed no increase in acidosis risk except below eGFR 30, where the hazard ratio was 1.97 (95% CI 1.03-3.77). Authors conclude metformin should not be used below eGFR 30, consistent with FDA guidance, and that its risk-benefit profile near 30 needs further research.

  14. Metformin in patients with type 2 diabetes and kidney disease: a systematic review.
    Inzucchi SE, Lipska KJ et al., 2014 · PMID 25536258 · mixed

    DOSE AND MONITORING, NOT AUTOMATIC STOP. Systematic review of 65 articles. In mild to moderate chronic kidney disease (eGFR 30-60 mL/min/1.73 m2), metformin drug levels generally stay within the therapeutic range and lactate is not substantially raised. Lactic acidosis incidence in metformin users ran roughly 3 to 10 per 100,000 person-years, generally indistinguishable from the background rate in diabetes, and no randomised trial has tested metformin safety in significantly impaired kidney function. Conclusion: available evidence supports cautious expansion of use in mild to moderate CKD defined by eGFR, with appropriate dosage reductions and careful follow-up of kidney function.
