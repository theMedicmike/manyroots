Herbs

Magnolia bark (honokiol/magnolol)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Magnolia bark has been used in Chinese and Japanese medicine for hundreds of years for sleep and anxiety, and in mice its active compound honokiol clearly works on the same brain receptor that sleeping pills target. But nobody has run a proper trial of magnolia bark on its own for insomnia in people. The one randomised study that compared it against a dummy pill and actually measured sleep found no benefit for sleep quality or how quickly people fell asleep. Two larger studies in menopausal women did report less insomnia when magnolia was added to a supplement, but magnesium was added at the same time, so you cannot tell which one did anything. People have used this for a very long time and nobody has properly tested it for sleep.

Safety

Safety data for magnolia bark are better developed than efficacy data for sleep. A toxicology review found concentrated magnolia bark extract non-mutagenic and non-genotoxic, with an OECD subchronic no-adverse-effect level above 240 mg/kg body weight per day, and intervention trials of up to one year reported no adverse effects; several food safety authorities have assessed magnolol and honokiol as safe. The 6-week randomised pilot reported no significant adverse events, and the two menopause trials reported adverse-event rates around 1%. However, because magnolol and honokiol are glucuronidated, interactions with medications or other botanicals cannot be excluded. Safety in pregnancy, breastfeeding and children has not been established. Note that most human safety data come from concentrated extract used as a food or oral-care additive, not from sleep dosing - which has never been formally studied.

Who should be careful: Not established as safe in pregnancy, lactation or children. Caution is warranted in anyone already taking a benzodiazepine or other prescribed sedative - a 2017 review documented online reports of exactly that combination being sought out, though no peer-reviewed dependence cases exist.

Interactions: Plausible additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, z-drugs, opioids and other CNS depressants, given that honokiol acts at the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor in animals and that effect is abolished by flumazenil. Magnolol and honokiol are glucuronidated, so pharmacokinetic interactions with drugs sharing that pathway cannot be ruled out. Both also act at cannabinoid CB1/CB2 receptors, adding a further theoretical interaction surface.

What the evidence says

No placebo-controlled randomised trial of magnolia bark with a sleep primary endpoint in adults with insomnia was found. The closest blinded human evidence is a randomised, parallel-group, placebo-controlled pilot of Relora (Magnolia officinalis plus Phellodendron amurense), 250 mg three times daily for 6 weeks in healthy overweight premenopausal women (40 intent-to-treat, 26 completers): it reduced transient STATE anxiety versus placebo but explicitly did not significantly change sleep quality or sleep latency, nor salivary cortisol, salivary amylase, appetite or body morphology (Kalman 2008). Two randomised trials in menopausal women point the other way but cannot isolate the herb: a 24-week multicentre study in 89 women found that adding 60 mg magnolia bark extract plus 50 mg magnesium to a soy isoflavone/lactobacilli/calcium/vitamin D3 formulation reduced insomnia, anxiety, mood depression, irritability and vasomotor symptoms significantly more than calcium plus vitamin D3 from week 4 (Mucci 2006), and a 12-week study across 91 centres randomised 634 women to the base formulation with or without magnolia and found the magnolia-containing arm significantly more active on insomnia, irritability, anxiety, depressed mood, asthenia and loss of libido, with adverse-event rates of 1.0% and 1.2% (Agosta 2011). In both, magnesium accompanies the magnolia, no placebo control was used, blinding is not described, and the two share an author and a commercial product line. Mechanistic support is real but preclinical: honokiol at 10 and 20 mg/kg i.p. in mice significantly shortened non-REM sleep latency and increased NREM sleep amount without changing REM amount or EEG power density, excited sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic neurons, and had its somnogenic effect completely abolished by the benzodiazepine-site antagonist flumazenil (Qu 2012); separately, magnolol behaves as a CB2-selective partial agonist (EC50 3.28 microM) and honokiol as a CB1 full agonist and CB2 antagonist, with the metabolite tetrahydromagnolol 19-fold more potent than magnolol at CB2 (EC50 0.170 microM) (Rempel 2012). These mechanisms make a sedative effect biologically plausible, but plausibility is not clinical evidence.

Doses studied: No sleep dose established. The one randomised placebo-controlled trial that measured sleep used Relora (Magnolia officinalis plus Phellodendron amurense) 250 mg three times daily (750 mg/day) for 6 weeks and found no sleep effect (Kalman 2008). Menopause combination products used 60 mg magnolia bark extract daily alongside magnesium and four to five other ingredients (Mucci 2006; Agosta 2011). Animal work used honokiol 10-20 mg/kg intraperitoneally in mice, which does not translate to a human oral dose.

Why this grade: Magnolia officinalis bark (houpo) has documented use in Chinese and Japanese traditional medicine for sleep and anxiety going back centuries, and honokiol has a well-characterised sedative mechanism in animals acting at the benzodiazepine site of the GABA-A receptor. But a fresh PubMed search found no placebo-controlled randomised trial of magnolia bark with a sleep primary endpoint in adults with insomnia - searching magnolia bark against sleep and insomnia returns only two human records in total. The one randomised placebo-controlled trial that measured sleep found no significant effect on sleep quality or latency. Two randomised trials in menopausal women (n = 89 and n = 634) did find that adding a magnolia-containing component to a base supplement improved insomnia, but neither isolates magnolia: magnesium is co-administered, neither used a placebo control or reported blinding, the population was menopausal symptom sufferers rather than people with insomnia, and both share an author and a commercial product line. 'Traditional' is the accurate grade; the single null pilot is too small and too indirect to justify 'not_supported'.

  1. Effect of a proprietary Magnolia and Phellodendron extract on stress levels in healthy women: a pilot, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialKalman DS, Feldman S, Feldman R, Schwartz HI, Krieger DR, Garrison R, 2008 · PMID 18426577 · null

    Randomised parallel-group placebo-controlled pilot in healthy overweight premenopausal women (40 intent-to-treat, 26 completers) given Relora 250 mg three times daily for 6 weeks: it reduced temporary, transitory STATE anxiety versus placebo, but sleep quality and sleep latency were NOT significantly changed versus placebo, nor were salivary cortisol, salivary amylase, appetite or body morphology, and it was not effective on TRAIT anxiety. There were no significant adverse events.

  2. Randomized controlled study on clinical efficacy of isoflavones plus Lactobacillus sporogenes, associated or not with a natural anxiolytic agent in menopause.
    Randomised trialAgosta C, Atlante M, Benvenuti C, 2011 · PMID 21311416 · positive

    Multicentre randomised parallel-group study across 91 gynaecological centres in 634 menopausal women (mean age 53.1) comparing isoflavones 60 mg plus Lactobacillus sporogenes, calcium and vitamin D3 with or without added magnolia bark extract, one tablet nightly for 12 weeks. Both arms improved from baseline and were similar on hot flushing, nocturnal sweating, palpitations and vaginal dryness, but the magnolia-containing arm was significantly more active on insomnia, irritability, anxiety, depressed mood, asthenia and loss of libido. Adverse-event rates were 1.0% and 1.2%. No placebo arm, blinding not described, and the magnolia-containing product also carries magnesium, so magnolia's individual contribution cannot be isolated.

  3. Soy isoflavones, lactobacilli, Magnolia bark extract, vitamin D3 and calcium. Controlled clinical study in menopause.
    Randomised trialMucci M, Carraro C, Mancino P, et al., 2006 · PMID 16957676 · mixed

    Randomised controlled multicentre study in 89 menopausal women (mean age 53.8) comparing a six-component formulation containing 60 mg Magnolia bark extract plus 50 mg magnesium against calcium plus vitamin D3 for 24 weeks: insomnia, anxiety, mood depression, irritability and vasomotor symptoms all decreased significantly more with the magnolia-containing product from week 4 onwards. Magnolia's individual contribution cannot be isolated, and the comparator was an active supplement rather than placebo.

  4. Honokiol promotes non-rapid eye movement sleep via the benzodiazepine site of the GABA(A) receptor in mice.
    Comparative studyQu WM, Yue XF, Sun Y, et al., 2012 · PMID 22537192 · positive

    In mice, honokiol at 10 and 20 mg/kg i.p. significantly shortened non-REM sleep latency and increased NREM sleep amount, with no effect on REM sleep amount or EEG power density; it increased c-Fos expression in and excited sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic neurons. Pretreatment with the benzodiazepine-site antagonist flumazenil abolished both effects, identifying the GABA-A benzodiazepine site as the mechanism. Animal study only, with no human endpoint.

  5. Magnolia Extract, Magnolol, and Metabolites: Activation of Cannabinoid CB2 Receptors and Blockade of the Related GPR55.
    Journal articleRempel V, Fuchs A, Hinz S, et al., 2012 (ACS Med Chem Lett 4:41-5) · PMID 24900561 · positive

    Receptor pharmacology showing magnolol is a CB2-selective partial agonist (EC50 3.28 microM) while honokiol is less potent, acting as a full CB1 agonist with antagonist properties at CB2; the major metabolite tetrahydromagnolol is 19-fold more potent than magnolol at CB2 (EC50 0.170 microM) and antagonises GPR55 (KB 13.3 microM). The paper notes magnolia bark's traditional use for anxiety and sleeping disorders, but this is in vitro work with no clinical endpoint.

  6. Safety and Toxicology of Magnolol and Honokiol.
    Narrative reviewSarrica A, Kirika N, Romeo M, Salmona M, Diomede L, 2018 · PMID 29925102 · null

    Safety and toxicology review: concentrated magnolia bark extract showed no mutagenic or genotoxic potential in vitro or in vivo, and an OECD-guideline subchronic study established a no-adverse-effect level above 240 mg/kg body weight per day. Intervention trials using concentrated extract for up to one year reported no adverse effects, and multiple food safety authorities have evaluated magnolol and honokiol as safe. However, both undergo glucuronidation, so interaction with pharmaceutical actives or other herbal constituents cannot be excluded.

  7. Is there a potential of misuse for Magnolia officinalis compounds/metabolites?
    Narrative reviewSchifano F, Guarino V, Papanti DG, Baccarin J, Orsolini L, Corkery JM, 2017 · PMID 28517911 · mixed

    Literature review found no peer-reviewed reports of magnolia abuse, misuse, dependence or addiction, but online sources identified three apparent groups of users attracted to its GABA-ergic and cannabimimetic activity: people with a psychiatric history already treated with benzodiazepines seeking magnolia as a 'natural sedative', polydrug users combining it with other herbs, and people naive to misuse who perceive it as a natural dietary supplement.
