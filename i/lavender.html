Herbs

Lavender (oral Silexan and aromatherapy)

Moderate evidence · for anxiety & stress Moderate evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Lavender comes in two very different forms here: a standardized oral capsule (Silexan, studied mostly in Europe) and the familiar scent used in aromatherapy. The capsule has several good-sized studies behind it in which people reported feeling noticeably calmer, though almost all of that research was run by the company that makes it, which is a real reason for caution. The scent may help people feel calmer in stressful moments, but those studies are generally small and less rigorous.

Safety

Oral Silexan's most characteristic side effect in trials was eructation (lavender-flavored burping), alongside mild, transient gastrointestinal complaints; the pooled five-trial dataset found no significant between-group differences in adverse events, serious adverse events, or withdrawal due to adverse events. A dedicated randomized driving-simulator study found no adverse effect on driving performance at single doses up to 320 mg or 80 mg/day for seven days, supporting the absence of sedation. Inhaled lavender is generally well tolerated, though safety reporting in aromatherapy studies is poor.

Who should be careful: Known allergy to lavender. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding at supplemental oral doses. Case reports have linked repeated use of topical lavender-containing products to prepubertal gynecomastia and premature thelarche, resolving on discontinuation; however, a 2019 systematic review of all four published case reports (11 cases total) judged the reporting insufficient to establish causality and found little to no evidence supporting the link, and a cross-sectional study of 556 children found no difference in endocrine-disorder risk between exposed and unexposed children with zero cases of prepubertal gynecomastia. The question is unresolved rather than settled in either direction; caution with prolonged heavy topical use in prepubertal children is reasonable but is not evidence-based.

Interactions: A randomized crossover drug-cocktail study in 16 healthy volunteers found Silexan 160 mg/day had no clinically relevant inhibitory or inducing effect on CYP1A2, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, or 3A4, and no drug interactions emerged in the trial program. Additive drowsiness with sedatives remains a theoretical caution for lavender products generally.

What the evidence says

Evidence for oral lavender oil (Silexan 80-160 mg/day) in anxiety is comparatively substantial: a 10-week, four-arm RCT in 539 adults with GAD found HAM-A reductions of 14.1 and 12.8 points for Silexan 160 and 80 mg/day versus 9.5 for placebo (both p<0.01), while paroxetine 20 mg showed only a trend (p=0.10) in the full analysis set. A 6-week double-blind trial found Silexan comparable to lorazepam (HAM-A -11.3±6.7 vs -11.6±6.6 points, about 45-46% from a baseline of 25±4 in both arms), with no placebo arm. A pooled individual-patient analysis of three placebo-controlled subthreshold-anxiety trials (n=697) found a 3.83-point HAM-A advantage (95% CI 1.28-6.37), and a 2023 meta-analysis of all five placebo-controlled trials (n=1,213) reported a responder rate ratio of 1.34 and a CGI much/very-much-improved rate ratio of 1.51 — both analyses include authors employed by the manufacturer, and the 2017 pooled analysis explicitly states its results cannot be generalized to other lavender oil products. Two independent syntheses corroborate the oral finding: a 2019 systematic review pooling 1,173 participants found HAM-A MD -2.90 (95% CI -4.86 to -0.95) for Silexan and Hedges' g -0.73 for inhalation, and a 2022 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 herbal anxiety trials ranked Silexan significant at MD -3.84 (95% CrI -6.31 to -1.34). That same 2019 review cautioned that the majority of its 65 included RCTs carried a high overall risk of bias, that inhalation results were heterogeneous, and that lavender inhalation did not significantly reduce systolic blood pressure. A fresh screen of the 24 randomized Silexan-titled reports indexed in PubMed found no published null RCT of oral Silexan in anxiety — but in a program run almost entirely by one sponsor, that absence is a publication-bias concern rather than a reassurance.

Doses studied: Oral Silexan 80-160 mg once daily for 6-10 weeks (the pooled placebo-controlled program used 1 x 80 mg/day for 10 weeks); aromatherapy protocols vary widely, from single inhalation or massage sessions to several weeks.

Why this grade: The grade attaches to oral Silexan, not to aromatherapy. For Silexan, multiple randomized trials (including a 539-participant four-arm trial) and four meta-analyses consistently favor lavender over placebo, with effects in the range of active comparators, and one fully independent network meta-analysis replicates the effect (MD -3.84, 95% CrI -6.31 to -1.34). Graded moderate rather than strong because nearly the entire oral-trial program was conducted or funded by a single manufacturer whose employees co-author the trials and the pooled analyses, the only independent systematic review judged the majority of its 65 included RCTs at high overall risk of bias, and no null trial exists in a single-sponsor program — which is a publication-bias concern rather than evidence of consistency. Aromatherapy evidence is larger in volume but of low methodological quality and would grade lower on its own.

  1. Lavender oil preparation Silexan is effective in generalized anxiety disorder--a randomized, double-blind comparison to placebo and paroxetine.
    Randomised trialKasper S et al., 2014 · PMID 24456909 · positive

    In 539 adults with GAD treated 10 weeks, HAM-A fell 14.1±9.3 (Silexan 160 mg/d) and 12.8±8.7 (80 mg/d) versus 9.5±9.0 points on placebo (both p<0.01); paroxetine 20 mg fell 11.3±8.0 and showed only a trend (p=0.10) in the full analysis set. Adverse-event incidence densities with Silexan (0.006-0.008 AEs/d) were comparable to placebo (0.008) and lower than paroxetine (0.011). Two co-authors are employed by the manufacturer, Dr. Willmar Schwabe.

  2. A multi-center, double-blind, randomised study of the Lavender oil preparation Silexan in comparison to Lorazepam for generalized anxiety disorder.
    Randomised trialWoelk H & Schlafke S, 2010 (epub Dec 2009) · PMID 19962288 · positive

    Over 6 weeks in adults with GAD, HAM-A decreased 11.3±6.7 points (45%) on Silexan versus 11.6±6.6 points (46%) on lorazepam, from a baseline of 25±4 in both groups, with comparable changes on somatic and psychic subscores and no sedative effect. There was no placebo arm, so this establishes similarity to a benzodiazepine, not efficacy over placebo.

  3. Efficacy of Silexan in subthreshold anxiety: meta-analysis of randomised, placebo-controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisMoller HJ et al., 2019 (online 2017) · PMID 29150713 · positive

    Pooled individual-patient data from 3 placebo-controlled trials in subthreshold anxiety (n=697 assessed for efficacy): Silexan 80 mg/day was superior to placebo on HAM-A total score over 10 weeks (mean difference 3.83 points, 95% CI 1.28-6.37), with adverse-event incidence comparable to placebo (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.85-1.33). Two authors are employed by Dr. Willmar Schwabe, and the authors state the results cannot be generalised to other lavender oil products.

  4. Efficacy of Silexan in patients with anxiety disorders: a meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisDold M et al., 2023 · PMID 36717399 · positive

    Meta-analysis of all five completed double-blind, placebo-controlled Silexan trials (n=1,213) across subthreshold anxiety, mixed anxiety and depressive disorder, and GAD, all at 1 x 80 mg/day for ten weeks: Silexan was significantly superior to placebo on HAM-A total and sub-scores, with a >=50% HAM-A responder rate ratio of 1.34 and a CGI much/very-much-improved rate ratio of 1.51, and no significant between-group differences in adverse events, serious adverse events, or withdrawal due to adverse events. Includes a manufacturer-employed co-author.

  5. Effects of lavender on anxiety: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisDonelli D et al., 2019 · PMID 31655395 · mixed

    Independent, unfunded systematic review: 65 RCTs (7,993 participants) plus 25 non-randomized studies in the qualitative synthesis, 37 RCTs (3,964 participants) in the quantitative synthesis. Oral Silexan 80 mg/day for at least 6 weeks reduced HAM-A versus control (MD -2.90, 95% CI -4.86 to -0.95, p=0.004, 1,173 participants) and inhalation reduced anxiety scores (Hedges' g -0.73, 95% CI -1.00 to -0.46, 1,682 participants), but the majority of included RCTs had a high overall risk of bias, inhalation evidence was heterogeneous, and lavender inhalation showed no significant effect on systolic blood pressure.

  6. Medicinal herbs for the treatment of anxiety: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisZhang W et al., 2022 · PMID 35378276 · positive

    Independent Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 RCTs comparing 12 medicinal herbs: Silexan showed a significant effect on anxiety (HAM-A MD -3.84, 95% CrI -6.31 to -1.34) and possible benefit for depression (SMD -0.37, 95% CI -0.53 to -0.20) and insomnia (SMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.76 to -0.21). The authors nonetheless describe all results as preliminary because of unconvincing sample sizes and the potential effectiveness of placebos.

For insomnia

Lavender comes in two forms with real research behind them: a swallowed capsule of lavender oil (Silexan, studied mostly in Germany and Austria) and the familiar scent used at bedtime. The capsule has good evidence for easing anxiety, and people in those studies slept better mainly because they felt less anxious, not because lavender knocks you out. The scent route shows encouraging results in many small studies, though the studies are of uneven quality, and the most careful scent study in women with diagnosed insomnia found no clear benefit over placebo; both forms are gentle, with burping being the main complaint from the capsules.

Safety

Tolerability of oral Silexan in trials was comparable to placebo (adverse events 33.7% vs 35.7% in the Kasper 2015 trial), with eructation (burping) and mild transient gastrointestinal complaints the characteristic side effects reported in the clinical-trial literature. Inhaled aromatherapy trials report few adverse events; one cancer-population meta-analysis noted a report of headache and sneezing. Separately, case reports have linked repeated topical application of products containing lavender (and tea tree) oils to prepubertal gynecomastia in boys, which resolved after discontinuation, with laboratory evidence of estrogenic and antiandrogenic activity (Henley 2007, N Engl J Med); this concerns topical cosmetic use in children rather than the oral or inhaled routes studied for sleep, but families should be aware of it.

Who should be careful: Oral Silexan has been studied in adults and (per real-world data) adolescents with anxiety; it has not been established for use in pregnancy. Aromatherapy should be used cautiously by people with asthma or fragrance sensitivity.

Interactions: Published clinical trial reviews of Silexan report no identified drug interactions, and no sedation, dependence, abuse potential, or withdrawal symptoms; it is still sensible to tell a clinician before combining any lavender product with sedative medicines.

What the evidence says

Evidence for lavender in sleep comes from two distinct routes and is positive but indirect and mixed. Oral Silexan (80 mg/day of a standardized Lavandula angustifolia oil): in a randomized double-blind trial of 170 outpatients with anxiety-related restlessness and disturbed sleep, 10 weeks of Silexan reduced Hamilton Anxiety total scores by 12.0 versus 9.3 points for placebo (p = 0.03) with 48.8% versus 33.3% responders (p = 0.04), with the treatment effect more pronounced than placebo on all outcome measures including the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (Kasper 2015); a Bayesian network meta-analysis likewise found Silexan benefitted insomnia outcomes (SMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.76 to -0.21) (Zhang 2022). Critically, a meta-analysis and mediation analysis across the randomized placebo-controlled Silexan trials found that more than 98% of the sleep effect was mediated by the anxiolytic effect, with only a marginal direct effect on sleep (Seifritz 2022), so the sleep benefit appears to travel through reduced anxiety. Aromatherapy: a meta-analysis of 30 studies in adults and elderly people found an overall effect size of 0.74 on sleep quality, though effects were larger in quasi-experimental studies (1.24) and with massage delivery (1.30) than in more rigorous designs, indicating design-sensitivity (Her 2021); a network meta-analysis of 11 trials in 690 critically ill patients found inhaled blends containing lavender improved sleep quality but rated the overall certainty of evidence as low (Fang 2024); and the one double-blind RCT in a clinically diagnosed insomnia population (35 postmenopausal women, 29 days of inhalation versus sunflower-oil placebo) found no significant between-group difference on its primary sleep-quality outcome (PSQI, P = 0.22) (Dos Reis Lucena 2021). No trials establish oral lavender as efficacious in insomnia disorder specifically.

Doses studied: Oral: Silexan 80 mg once daily for 10 weeks (anxiety populations). Aromatherapy: inhaled or massage-delivered lavender essential oil, typically over 2-4 weeks; regimens varied widely across trials.

Why this grade: Multiple randomized trials and meta-analyses exist and are overall positive: oral Silexan improved sleep alongside anxiety in placebo-controlled trials, and aromatherapy meta-analyses show moderate-to-large pooled effects on sleep quality. But the evidence is qualified: a mediation analysis found the Silexan sleep effect is over 98% carried by its anti-anxiety effect rather than direct sedation, Silexan trials enrolled anxiety patients rather than people with insomnia disorder, the aromatherapy literature has high heterogeneity with certainty of evidence rated low, and the one double-blind aromatherapy RCT in a diagnosed-insomnia population was null on its primary outcome. Positive but indirect and methodologically limited evidence fits moderate, not strong.

    Network meta-analysisZhang W et al., 2022 (Pharmacol Res) · PMID 35378276 · positive

    In a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 trials of 12 medicinal herbs, Silexan displayed a significant effect on anxiety (MD -3.84, 95% CrI -6.31 to -1.34) and possibly benefitted insomnia (SMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.76 to -0.21), with good tolerability across included trials.

  4. Effect of aromatherapy on sleep quality of adults and elderly people: A systematic literature review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisHer J & Cho MK, 2021 (Complement Ther Med) · PMID 34166869 · positive

    Across 30 included studies, the overall effect size of aromatherapy on sleep quality was 0.74 (statistically significant); effects were larger in quasi-experimental studies (1.24) and with massage delivery (1.30) than in more rigorous designs, indicating the pooled estimate is sensitive to study quality.

  5. Effect of inhaled aromatherapy on sleep quality in critically ill patients: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisFang CS et al., 2024 (J Clin Nurs) · PMID 39041491 · mixed

    Network meta-analysis of 11 trials (690 critically ill patients) found a lavender-containing essential-oil blend (lavender, Matricaria recutita, neroli 6:2:0.5) produced the largest improvement in sleep quality versus usual care, but the overall certainty of evidence was rated low.

  6. Lavender essential oil on postmenopausal women with insomnia: Double-blind randomized trial.
    Randomised trialDos Reis Lucena L et al., 2021 (Complement Ther Med) · PMID 33905827 · null

    In a double-blind RCT of 35 postmenopausal women with a clinical diagnosis of insomnia, 29 days of inhaled Lavandula angustifolia essential oil produced no significant between-group difference versus sunflower-oil placebo on the primary outcome (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, P = 0.22); both groups improved over time with sleep hygiene guidance, and only within-group gains (e.g., sleep onset latency P = 0.001; polysomnographic sleep efficiency P = 0.002) favored lavender.
