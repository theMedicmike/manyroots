Practices

Tai chi and qigong

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Moderate evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For chronic pain

Tai chi is slow, weight-bearing, balance-focused movement, and it holds up well in trials: for knee arthritis it performed about as well as a standard course of physiotherapy, and for fibromyalgia it matched or beat aerobic exercise. Arthritis guidelines recommend it. The studies are mostly small and nobody can be blinded to whether they are doing tai chi, so treat the exact size of the benefit as uncertain. Qigong, its close relative, has far less evidence behind it - one trial in older adults found it no better than doing nothing.

Safety

Among the gentlest options studied. No serious adverse events related to the interventions were reported in either the 204-participant knee osteoarthritis trial or the 226-participant fibromyalgia trial. Tai chi is low-impact, performed standing at self-selected effort, and adherence in trials was better for tai chi than for aerobic exercise. Transient muscle soreness when starting is common. Qigong's safety record is less thoroughly characterised simply because fewer trials have studied it.

Who should be careful: Because it is done standing, people with severe balance impairment, acute vertigo, or a recent fall or fracture should begin seated or with a chair and supervision rather than in a standard class. Deep or sustained stance depth may need modifying for severe weight-bearing knee or hip pain. Uncontrolled cardiac disease, uncontrolled hypertension or symptomatic orthostatic hypotension warrant clinician clearance, as with any exercise.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Sedatives, opioids, antihypertensives, alpha-blockers and other medications affecting balance or blood pressure raise fall risk during standing practice - a reason to start with support rather than a reason to avoid it.

What the evidence says

An updated systematic review of 15 randomised trials in chronic musculoskeletal pain (80% osteoarthritis, 13% back pain, 7% headache) found moderate-quality evidence that tai chi beat no treatment or usual care in the short term for pain (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -0.85 to -0.48) and disability (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -0.85 to -0.46), while evidence for all other outcomes was low or very low quality with little information on long-term effects, which the authors said hindered reliable clinical recommendations. In a 204-participant randomised trial in knee osteoarthritis, 12 weeks of tai chi and a standard course of physical therapy both substantially reduced WOMAC scores (tai chi 167 points, 95% CI 145 to 190; physical therapy 143 points, 95% CI 119 to 167) with no significant between-group difference (24 points, 95% CI -10 to 58) and benefits held to 52 weeks, though tai chi did produce significantly greater improvements in depression and in the physical component of quality of life. In a 226-participant fibromyalgia trial, tai chi improved the revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire more than aerobic exercise at 24 weeks (difference 5.5 points, 95% CI 0.6 to 10.4, P = .03), with the largest gap when matched for intensity and duration (16.2 points, 95% CI 8.7 to 23.6). Countering this, a 176-person trial in adults aged 65 and over with chronic low back pain found qigong no better than no intervention for pain, and a 2025 network and dose-response meta-analysis of 13 trials (558 participants) in knee osteoarthritis found only a small-to-moderate pooled pain effect for mind-body exercise overall (Hedges' g -0.35; tai chi -0.41, yoga -0.38, qigong -0.26), with 8 of 13 studies at high risk of bias and confidence in all comparisons rated extremely low.

Doses studied: Trials commonly used classic Yang-style tai chi in supervised classes, twice weekly for 12 weeks in the knee-osteoarthritis trial and once or twice weekly for 12 or 24 weeks in the fibromyalgia trial. In the 226-participant fibromyalgia trial, 24 weeks of tai chi outperformed 12 weeks (difference 9.6 FIQR points, 95% CI 2.6 to 16.6) but twice-weekly was no better than once-weekly - duration mattered more than frequency. The 2025 dose-response meta-analysis found the clinically significant dose for mind-body exercise as a class (not tai chi specifically) fell at roughly 250-610 MET-minutes.

Why this grade: The 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline makes a strong recommendation for tai chi in hand, hip and knee osteoarthritis, and a 15-trial meta-analysis found moderate-quality evidence of short-term benefit versus no treatment or usual care (SMD -0.66 for both pain and disability). But the trials are small and necessarily unblinded, evidence for all other outcomes was low or very low quality with little long-term data, head-to-head trials show tai chi matching rather than beating physiotherapy, and a 2025 network meta-analysis rated confidence in every mind-body comparison as extremely low. Qigong, bundled into this entry by name, has a much thinner base and one 176-person trial was null. Graded on the lower reading.

For insomnia

Tai chi is one of the few gentle movement practices with decent sleep research behind it, including studies that measured sleep with wrist monitors rather than just asking people how they slept. The improvements are real but small - a few extra minutes of solid sleep, less time awake in the night - and when 37 trials were pooled the sleep benefit shrank to something quite modest. Tai chi did no better than ordinary exercise, and it did not match CBT-I for actually resolving insomnia in one head-to-head study. Qigong is a separate matter: most of the qigong-for-insomnia studies you can find are plans for trials rather than finished ones, and where it has been pooled it came out at the bottom.

Safety

Tai chi and qigong are low-impact, weight-bearing, and among the safest interventions in this category; the trials cited did not report serious adverse events, though adverse-event reporting in this literature is sparse and should not be read as proof of zero risk - and for qigong in insomnia specifically, most of what exists is registered trial protocols, so safety data are close to absent. The realistic risks are musculoskeletal: knee or hip strain from deep stances, and falls in people with significant balance impairment, particularly when practicing unsupervised or on uneven ground. Standing qigong forms held for long periods can cause light-headedness. Because the sleep benefit was no larger than that of conventional exercise, was small in the largest pooled analysis, and did not produce insomnia remission where CBT did, tai chi is best framed as a well-tolerated adjunct or an accessible alternative for people who will not or cannot do CBT-I - not as a replacement for it.

Who should be careful: Modify or supervise for people with acute joint injury, severe osteoarthritis of the knee or hip, significant balance disorder, orthostatic hypotension, or recent lower-limb surgery. People at high fall risk should start with a seated or supported version and a qualified instructor. Not a substitute for evaluation of untreated sleep apnea or restless legs syndrome.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Practical caution: exercising vigorously close to bedtime can delay sleep onset in some people, and the trials cited scheduled supervised sessions during the day rather than immediately before bed.

What the evidence says

Evidence for tai chi in insomnia is moderate and largely subjective-outcome-driven, with objective gains that are consistent but small. In a 320-participant randomized trial in adults 60 and older with chronic insomnia (mean insomnia duration 124.4 months), 12 weeks of tai chi improved actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency by 3.4 percentage points (95% CI 1.6 to 5.1) and reduced wake after sleep onset by 13.3 minutes (95% CI -21.3 to -5.2) versus passive control, with effects maintained at 24 months - but tai chi was not different from conventional exercise, and the authors describe the absolute improvements as modest. A 90-person noninferiority trial in breast cancer survivors found Tai Chi Chih statistically noninferior to CBT-I, with insomnia treatment response of 46.7% versus 43.7% at 15 months; polysomnography did not improve in either arm. The contrary findings are substantial. A 123-person three-arm trial found CBT outperformed Tai Chi Chih and sleep education control on clinician-ascertained insomnia remission (p<0.01); Tai Chi Chih improved sleep quality, fatigue and depressive symptoms versus control but did not produce insomnia remission, and polysomnographic measures did not change. More importantly, a 2025 Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis of 37 RCTs (2,843 older adults) found that although tai chi improved anxiety (SMD -0.47) and depressive symptoms (SMD -0.41), its effect on sleep quality, while statistically significant, was small in magnitude (SMD -0.12, 95% CrI -0.20 to -0.04) with large heterogeneity - considerably more modest than the -4.57 PSQI points reported in a 2026 network meta-analysis of 22 insomnia-specific RCTs (1,348 participants), where only 4 of 22 trials were at low risk of bias and all tai chi estimates were low-certainty. For qigong specifically the evidence is thinner still: a 2023 meta-analysis of 20 traditional-Chinese-exercise trials found tai chi had greater effects on sleep quality than qigong and found no significant improvement in several subgroups (stroke, breast cancer, college students, episodic migraine), and a 2026 network meta-analysis of 31 RCTs (2,457 participants) ranked qigong alone last of eight interventions on PSQI - below conventional control - with no intervention significantly increasing total sleep time.

Doses studied: Tai chi regimens in the cited trials: 12 weeks of supervised training (the 320-person actigraphy trial); 2-hour group sessions weekly over 4 months (Irwin 2014); 25 weeks of Tai Chi Chih, comprising 16 weeks of teaching followed by practice and assessment 9 weeks later (Irwin 2008); and 3 months of Tai Chi Chih (Irwin 2017). A 2025 Bayesian dose-response meta-analysis of 37 RCTs in older adults proposes an optimal therapeutic target of 500-700 MET-minutes per week, though the sleep effect it modelled was small. No comparable verified dose-response data exist for qigong in insomnia.

Why this grade: This moderate grade is earned by TAI CHI, not by qigong. Tai chi has real RCT evidence for insomnia, including a 320-participant assessor-masked trial using actigraphy (not just self-report) with benefits sustained at 24 months, and a 90-person noninferiority trial against CBT-I in cancer survivors. But the absolute gains are modest (about 3 percentage points of sleep efficiency, about 13 minutes less wake after sleep onset), the largest pooled analysis of tai chi in older adults found the sleep effect statistically significant but very small (SMD -0.12), polysomnographic sleep did not change in two trials, one comparative trial found tai chi did not produce insomnia remission while CBT did, network certainty is low, and tai chi was no better than plain conventional exercise. Qigong specifically is far weaker: its chronic-insomnia literature is dominated by registered trial protocols rather than completed results, one meta-analysis found tai chi outperformed qigong on sleep quality, and in a 31-trial network meta-analysis qigong alone ranked last of eight nodes on sleep quality, below conventional control. Positive but modest and mixed puts tai chi at moderate; qigong alone would not reach it.

    Randomised trialSiu PM, Yu AP, Tam BT, Chin EC, Yu DS, Chung KF, Hui SS, Woo J, Fong DY, Lee PH, Wei GX, Irwin MR, 2021 (JAMA Network Open) · PMID 33587135 · positive

  2. Optimal Tai Chi dose for improving anxiety, depression, and sleep quality in older adults: A Bayesian meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisChen S, Luo L, Yuan Y, 2025 (Clinical Rehabilitation) · PMID 40625025 · mixed

    37 randomized controlled trials, 2,843 older adults (1,424 in tai chi arms). Tai chi improved anxiety (SMD -0.47, 95% CrI -0.65 to -0.30) and depressive symptoms (SMD -0.41, 95% CrI -0.50 to -0.33). Insomnia treatment response at 15 months was 43.7% with CBT-I and 46.7% with Tai Chi Chih; noninferiority of Tai Chi Chih was established at months 3, 6 and 15 (p=0.02, p<0.01, p=0.02).   8. Improving sleep quality in older adults with moderate sleep complaints: A randomized controlled trial of Tai Chi Chih.
    Randomised trialIrwin MR, Olmstead R, Motivala SJ, 2008 (Sleep) · PMID 18652095 · positive

Among those with a baseline PSQI of 5 or greater, more Tai Chi Chih participants achieved a treatment response (PSQI below 5) than health education participants (p<0.05), with improvements in rated sleep quality, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep duration and sleep disturbance. Note this was a sample with moderate sleep complaints, not diagnosed insomnia disorder.
