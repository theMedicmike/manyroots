Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)

Strong evidence · for depression & low mood

For depression & low mood

ECT means passing a brief, carefully controlled electrical current through the brain to trigger a short seizure, done under general anaesthetic with a muscle relaxant, usually two or three times a week. For severe depression, especially when someone is not eating, is psychotic, or has not responded to medication, it is one of the most reliably effective treatments in all of psychiatry. The trade-off is memory: most measurable problems clear within about two weeks, but some people are left with genuine gaps in memories from around the treatment period, and relapse in the following year is common unless treatment continues afterwards. Its reputation comes largely from how it was done before anaesthesia and from film, not from how it is done now, but the memory cost is not a myth and deserves a frank conversation.

Safety

ECT requires general anaesthesia and a muscle relaxant and is given in a hospital or clinic, not an office. Immediate effects commonly include headache, jaw and muscle ache, nausea and short-lived confusion on waking. The cognitive cost is the honest centre of the risk conversation: on standardised testing most deficits are confined to the first 3 days and resolve by about two weeks, but the largest prospective study found retrograde autobiographical memory loss still measurable at 6 months, worse with bilateral placement and with obsolete sine-wave devices, and some people describe permanent gaps in personal memories from around the treatment period. Serious medical risk is low: a matched cohort of 10,016 admissions found no increase in serious medical events and significantly fewer suicide deaths with ECT. Relapse within a year is high (about 51%) unless continuation treatment follows the course, so the plan for afterwards matters as much as the course itself. Stigma around ECT largely reflects pre-anaesthesia practice and film portrayal rather than current technique, which uses anaesthesia, muscle relaxation, monitored seizure dosing and, increasingly, right unilateral placement to limit memory effects; that history is a reason for informed consent to be careful, not a reason to omit ECT from the options.

Who should be careful: Most guidelines list no absolute contraindications, but risk is elevated with raised intracranial pressure or a space-occupying intracranial lesion, recent myocardial infarction or unstable arrhythmia, recent stroke or intracranial aneurysm, severe pulmonary disease, and high anaesthetic risk (ASA class 4-5); each requires anaesthetic and medical assessment rather than automatic exclusion. Pregnancy is not a contraindication. Age, cognitive impairment, lower premorbid intellectual function and female sex predict greater cognitive effect and argue for right unilateral placement and careful dosing. Different in bipolar depression, which is outside this node: ECT is effective there and is sometimes preferred, but antimanic cover and specialist oversight are needed. Capacity and genuinely informed consent, including a specific discussion of memory risk, are central; involuntary administration is governed by jurisdiction-specific law.

Interactions: Benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants raise the seizure threshold and can blunt efficacy, so doses are usually reduced or timed around treatments. Lithium is associated with increased risk of delirium and prolonged seizures and is often held or reduced. Theophylline and bupropion may prolong seizures. Anaesthetic agents, and drugs affecting blood pressure or heart rate, are managed by the anaesthetist. Disclose every medicine, including over-the-counter and herbal products, to the ECT and anaesthesia team before the course.

What the evidence says

A systematic review and meta-analysis found real ECT significantly more effective than simulated ECT (6 trials, 256 patients, standardised effect size -0.91, 95% CI -1.27 to -0.54) and more effective than pharmacotherapy (18 trials, 1144 participants, SES -0.80, 95% CI -1.29 to -0.29), with bilateral more effective than unilateral placement (22 trials, 1408 participants, SES -0.32, 95% CI -0.46 to -0.19). A later network meta-analysis of 113 brain-stimulation trials (262 arms, 6750 patients with major depressive disorder or bipolar depression) put bitemporal ECT first for response against sham (OR 8.91, 95% CI 2.57-30.91) and high-dose right unilateral ECT second (7.27, 1.90-27.78), though the confidence intervals are very wide and the authors described the evidence as typically of low or unclear risk of bias (94 of 113 trials). Cognitive effects on standardised testing are largely time-limited: pooling 84 studies and 2981 patients, 72% of 24 cognitive variables showed significant decline at 0-3 days post-ECT (effect sizes -1.10 to -0.21), no negative effect sizes remained after 15 days, and 57% of variables exceeded baseline; the reviewers noted no standardised retrograde amnesia tests existed in the literature they searched, so that analysis cannot speak to autobiographical memory loss. The largest prospective community study (751 referred, 347 evaluated across 7 facilities) found adverse cognitive effects still detectable 6 months after the course, with bilateral placement producing more severe and persisting retrograde amnesia than right unilateral and sine-wave stimulation producing sustained reaction-time slowing. Durability is the central weakness: across 32 studies, 51.1% (95% CI 44.7-57.4) of ECT responders relapsed within 12 months on continuation pharmacotherapy, 37.7% within the first 6 months, though continuation antidepressants halved relapse risk versus placebo (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.39-0.62, NNT 3.3). On medical safety, a propensity-matched cohort of 10,016 psychiatric admissions found serious medical events at 0.25 per person-year with ECT versus 0.33 without (cause-specific HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61-1.00) and significantly fewer suicide deaths in the exposed group.

Doses studied: Delivered under general anaesthesia with a muscle relaxant, typically two to three times per week; the modern comparative trial used three sessions per week over an initial 3-week phase. Two technical parameters drive the efficacy-versus-memory trade-off and are set by the treating team: electrode placement (bilateral is more effective but causes more severe and more persistent retrograde amnesia than right unilateral) and stimulus dose and waveform (higher dose is more effective; obsolete sine-wave stimulation caused pronounced and lasting reaction-time slowing compared with brief-pulse). A course is finite, and continuation treatment afterwards is what determines whether the gain holds.

Why this grade: ECT holds guideline-supported status for severe, psychotic, catatonic or treatment-resistant depression, and meta-analysis shows a large acute advantage over simulated (sham) ECT (SES -0.91) and over pharmacotherapy (SES -0.80, 18 trials, 1144 participants); a later 113-trial, 6750-patient network meta-analysis of brain stimulation ranked bitemporal ECT highest for response against sham (OR 8.91, 95% CI 2.57-30.91). That satisfies the strong criteria. The grade covers acute efficacy only, and three caveats are part of the honest record rather than footnotes to it: the direct sham-controlled trial base is small (6 trials, 256 patients) and decades old, and the newer network estimates carry very wide confidence intervals; durability is poor, with 51.1% relapsing within 12 months even on continuation pharmacotherapy; and a modern open-label head-to-head found IV ketamine noninferior with ECT numerically lower on response (41.2% vs 55.4%) and markedly worse on short-term memory testing. Cognitive adverse effects are real, measurable and, in the largest prospective study, still detectable at 6 months with bilateral placement.

Sources (7)
  1. Efficacy and safety of electroconvulsive therapy in depressive disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    UK ECT Review Group, 2003 (Lancet) · PMID 12642045 · positive

    Real ECT was significantly more effective than simulated ECT (6 trials, 256 patients, standardised effect size -0.91, 95% CI -1.27 to -0.54) and more effective than pharmacotherapy (18 trials, 1144 participants, SES -0.80, 95% CI -1.29 to -0.29). Bilateral ECT was more effective than unilateral (22 trials, 1408 participants, SES -0.32, 95% CI -0.46 to -0.19), and high-dose more effective than low-dose. This remains the anchor efficacy evidence, and its sham-controlled component is small and old.

  2. Comparative efficacy and acceptability of non-surgical brain stimulation for the acute treatment of major depressive episodes in adults: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Mutz, Vipulananthan, Carter, et al., 2019 (BMJ) · PMID 30917990 · positive

    Across 113 trials and 262 treatment arms randomising 6750 patients with major depressive disorder or bipolar depression, 10 of 18 strategies beat sham for response, led by bitemporal ECT (OR 8.91, 95% CI 2.57-30.91) and high-dose right unilateral ECT (7.27, 1.90-27.78); both also came out ahead in active-versus-active contrasts. All strategies were at least as acceptable as sham. The authors described the evidence as typically of low or unclear risk of bias (94 of 113 trials, 83%) with precision of summary estimates varying considerably, which is visible in the very wide intervals around the ECT estimates.

  3. Objective cognitive performance associated with electroconvulsive therapy for depression: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Semkovska & McLoughlin, 2010 (Biological Psychiatry) · PMID 20673880 · mixed

    Across 84 studies, 2981 patients and 24 cognitive variables, significant decreases appeared 0-3 days after ECT in 72% of variables (effect sizes -1.10, 95% CI -1.53 to -0.67, to -0.21, 95% CI -0.40 to 0.01). By 4-15 days all but one confidence interval included zero or showed positive effects, and after 15 days no negative effect sizes remained, with 57% of variables improved beyond baseline. Critically, the authors identified no standardised retrograde amnesia tests in the literature, so this analysis cannot speak to autobiographical memory loss.

  4. The cognitive effects of electroconvulsive therapy in community settings.
    Sackeim, Prudic, Fuller, et al., 2006 (Neuropsychopharmacology) · PMID 16936712 · negative

    In the first large prospective study of ECT cognitive outcomes (751 referred, 347 evaluated across 7 New York metropolitan facilities), adverse cognitive effects were detected 6 months after the acute course. Bilateral ECT produced more severe and persisting retrograde amnesia on the Columbia University Autobiographical Memory Interview than right unilateral ECT, and sine-wave stimulation caused pronounced reaction-time slowing both immediately and at 6 months. Advancing age, lower premorbid intellectual function and female sex predicted greater deficits, and outcomes varied significantly across facilities.

  5. Relapse following successful electroconvulsive therapy for major depression: a meta-analysis.
    Jelovac, Kolshus & McLoughlin, 2013 (Neuropsychopharmacology) · PMID 23774532 · negative

    Across 32 studies with up to 2 years of follow-up, 51.1% (95% CI 44.7-57.4%) of patients who responded to ECT relapsed within 12 months despite continuation pharmacotherapy, with 37.7% (30.7-45.2%) relapsing in the first 6 months; continuation ECT gave a similar 6-month relapse rate (37.2%, 23.4-53.5%). Continuation antidepressants halved relapse risk versus placebo (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.39-0.62, NNT 3.3). The authors concluded maintenance of well-being after successful ECT needs to be improved.

  6. Ketamine versus ECT for Nonpsychotic Treatment-Resistant Major Depression.
    Anand, Mathew, Sanacora, et al., 2023 (NEJM; ELEKT-D) · PMID 37224232 · negative

    In 403 patients referred to ECT clinics for non-psychotic treatment-resistant depression, response occurred in 41.2% of the ECT group versus 55.4% of the ketamine group, with ketamine meeting noninferiority. ECT was associated with a mean decrease in Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised delayed recall T-score of 9.7 (SE 1.2) at 3 weeks versus 0.9 (SE 1.1) with ketamine, recovering gradually during follow-up, plus musculoskeletal adverse effects. The trial was open-label and excluded psychotic depression.

  7. Risk of serious medical events in patients with depression treated with electroconvulsive therapy: a propensity score-matched, retrospective cohort study.
    Kaster, Vigod, Gomes, et al., 2021 (Lancet Psychiatry) · PMID 34265274 · positive

    Among 10,016 propensity-matched psychiatric hospitalisation records in Ontario (5008 per group), serious medical events occurred at 0.25 per person-year with ECT exposure versus 0.33 per person-year without (cause-specific hazard ratio 0.78, 95% CI 0.61-1.00). Suicide death was significantly lower in the exposed group (5 or fewer of 5008) than the unexposed group (11 of 5008, 0.2%; p<0.03), and no specific serious medical event was elevated.
