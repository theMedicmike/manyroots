Herbs

Wild lettuce (Lactuca virosa)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Wild lettuce, and the dried milky sap called lactucarium, was used as a sedative and painkiller in the 1800s and was sometimes described as a gentler alternative to opium. The honest answer is that people have used it for a very long time and nobody has ever properly tested it: there is not a single human trial of wild lettuce for sleep. The lettuce sleep studies you may have seen used garden lettuce seed, a different plant. What has been documented in people is harm rather than benefit - eight patients in one Iranian hospital were treated for wild lettuce poisoning, one of them in intensive care.

Safety

Wild lettuce is not an inert herb, and the human literature that exists for it is about harm rather than benefit. A published case series describes eight patients admitted to an Iranian hospital with wild lettuce toxicity after eating the plant; all recovered, but one required 48 hours in intensive care, and management consisted of conservative treatment, vital sign monitoring, control of intake and output, and reduction of agitation. Abuse of the plant has also been reported in the clinical literature, and it appears in reviews of psychoactive plants used as new psychoactive substances, where the authors note that its metabolism is largely uninvestigated and data from intoxication and fatality cases are scarce. The historical medical literature explicitly distinguishes Lactuca virosa as the wild and poisonous lettuce, separate from the edible garden species, and records that its latex was treated as an intoxicant. Beyond that, no modern repeated-dose toxicology, no human dosing study, no pregnancy or lactation data, and no paediatric data were found. Allergy is documented at genus level: Lactuca species carry sesquiterpene lactones that cause contact allergy, with positive patch-test reactions in 11% and 22% of Compositae-allergic patients in two Danish series (studied in the edible species), and the standard sesquiterpene lactone screening mix performs poorly at detecting it. People sensitised to the Compositae/Asteraceae family (ragweed, chamomile, feverfew, chrysanthemum, artichoke) are routinely warned about ingesting plants from this family.

Who should be careful: Treat as a plant with documented human toxicity, not as merely unstudied. Wild lettuce ingestion has put people in hospital and one patient in intensive care, so it should not be self-dosed at concentrated-extract or plant-material strengths, and any use warrants clinician oversight. Avoid in anyone with known Compositae/Asteraceae allergy given the documented contact and possible systemic allergic reactions to Lactuca sesquiterpene lactones. In the complete absence of pregnancy, breastfeeding, paediatric and modern toxicology data, and given the plant's historical and continuing classification as the poisonous lettuce species, avoid in those groups entirely, and do not combine with alcohol or prescribed sedatives.

Interactions: No human drug-interaction data exist. Any additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids or other CNS depressants is speculative, extrapolated from reduced locomotor activity in mice given isolated compounds. The nineteenth-century framing of lactucarium as an opium substitute has never been substantiated pharmacologically; the historical record itself states the active principle was never identified, and modern forensic reviews note its metabolism remains largely uncharacterised, so interactions cannot be predicted from known pathways.

What the evidence says

No human trial of Lactuca virosa for insomnia, sleep or any other condition was identified. The historical record documents that in nineteenth-century medical practice lactucarium, the desiccated latex of the poisonous species Lactuca virosa and of garden lettuce, was regarded as an intoxicant and used as a sedative and analgesic considered weaker than opium but free of its side effects; the same review records that attempts to isolate the responsible constituent failed and the active component was never identified. The most relevant modern pharmacology is a mouse study of lactucin, lactucopicrin and 11-beta,13-dihydrolactucin, the characteristic bitter sesquiterpene lactones of Lactuca virosa and Cichorium intybus: at 15 and 30 mg/kg they produced hot-plate analgesia comparable to ibuprofen 30 mg/kg, at 30 mg/kg tail-flick analgesia comparable to ibuprofen 60 mg/kg, and lactucin and lactucopicrin, but not dihydrolactucin, reduced spontaneous locomotor activity, a sedation proxy rather than a sleep measure. Human sleep trials in this plant genus used lettuce seed rather than wild lettuce: a double-blind placebo-controlled pilot in 50 breast cancer patients found lettuce seed syrup 5 mL twice daily for 4 weeks reduced total Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score versus placebo (p=0.014), with significant subscale reductions in subjective sleep quality (p=0.002), sleep duration (p=0.038), habitual sleep efficacy (p=0.029) and sleep disturbance (p=0.032), though the abstract does not state which Lactuca species was used. A second double-dummy trial of an intranasal saffron, Lactuca sativa seed and violet oil preparation in 50 patients with primary chronic insomnia found Insomnia Severity Index scores fell significantly from baseline in both the intervention group (p=0.001) and the placebo group (p<0.01), with only hypnotic-drug use falling differentially (p<0.001). The human literature specific to Lactuca virosa is toxicological: a case series of eight hospitalised patients with wild lettuce toxicity, one requiring 48 hours of intensive care.

Doses studied: No human dose of Lactuca virosa has been studied for insomnia or any other condition. Mouse data used isolated sesquiterpene lactones at 15-30 mg/kg. The human doses sometimes quoted in this area belong to lettuce seed preparations from the edible species rather than wild lettuce: lettuce seed syrup 5 mL twice daily for 4 weeks, and an intranasal Lactuca sativa-containing oil, two drops per nostril twice daily for 8 weeks.

Why this grade: Wild lettuce has no human efficacy trial of any kind for insomnia, or for any other condition, in the literature retrieved. Its reputation rests on nineteenth-century use of lactucarium, the dried latex, as a sedative and analgesic said to be weaker than opium; that same historical record documents that repeated attempts to identify the active substance failed. The only supporting pharmacology is a mouse study of isolated sesquiterpene lactones showing reduced spontaneous locomotor activity, which is a sedation proxy rather than a sleep measure. The human sleep trials people often cite in this area used lettuce seed from the edible garden species, not wild lettuce. A 2015 systematic review of insomnia herbals found no monopreparation trial of any Lactuca species. What does exist in humans for this specific plant is a toxicity case series, which is a reason for caution rather than a reason to upgrade.

Sources (9)
  1. Wild lettuce (Lactuca virosa) toxicity.
    Journal articleBesharat S, Besharat M, Jabbari A, 2009 · PMID 21686920 · negative

    Case series of eight patients admitted to a general hospital affiliated to Golestan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, with manifestations of wild lettuce toxicity after eating the plant, which grows in northern Iran where some residents consume it unaware of its adverse effects. All eight recovered, although one required 48 hours in the intensive care unit, and no chronic complications were reported; treatment was conservative, with vital sign monitoring, control of intake and output and reduction of patient agitation. Direct evidence of human harm from the same plant sold as a sleep aid.

  2. [Lettuce, lactuca sp., as a medicinal plant in polish publications of the 19th century].
    Journal articleTrojanowska A, 2005 · PMID 17153150 · mixed

    Historical review of 19th-century Polish medical literature: lactucarium, the dried latex of the wild and poisonous species Lactuca virosa and of garden lettuce, was considered an intoxicant and used as a sedative and analgesic described as weaker than opium but free of its side-effects; the review also records that studies undertaken to find the substance responsible failed, and the component responsible for the plant's properties was never identified.

  3. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisLeach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · null

    Systematic review of 14 RCTs in 1602 insomnia participants found orally administered monopreparation RCT evidence for only valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling; no Lactuca species appeared, and no herbal medicine differed significantly from placebo on any of thirteen efficacy measures.

  4. Analgesic and sedative activities of lactucin and some lactucin-like guaianolides in mice.
    Journal articleWesolowska A, Nikiforuk A, Michalska K, Kisiel W, Chojnacka-Wojcik E, 2006 · PMID 16621374 · positive

    Lactucin and lactucopicrin, characteristic bitter sesquiterpene lactones of Lactuca virosa, produced hot-plate analgesia in mice at 15 and 30 mg/kg comparable to ibuprofen 30 mg/kg and tail-flick analgesia at 30 mg/kg comparable to ibuprofen 60 mg/kg, with lactucopicrin the most potent; lactucin and lactucopicrin, but not 11-beta,13-dihydrolactucin, also showed sedative properties in the spontaneous locomotor activity test. Isolated compounds in mice, with locomotor activity as the only sedation measure and no sleep endpoint.

  5. Efficacy of lettuce seed syrup on insomnia in patients with breast cancer: a pilot double blind randomized placebo controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialMosavat SH, Mirzaei HR, Mofid B, et al., 2021 · PMID 34455726 · positive

    Double-blind placebo-controlled pilot in 50 breast cancer patients using lettuce seed syrup 5 mL twice daily for 4 weeks: total PSQI fell significantly versus placebo (p=0.014), with reductions in subjective sleep quality (p=0.002), sleep duration (p=0.038), habitual sleep efficacy (p=0.029) and sleep disturbance (p=0.032). The abstract does not name the Lactuca species; it is a Persian-medicine lettuce-seed preparation and is not Lactuca virosa, so it does not transfer to wild lettuce.

  6. Evaluation of sedative effects of an intranasal dosage form containing saffron, lettuce seeds and sweet violet in primary chronic insomnia: A randomized, double-dummy, double-blind placebo controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialTaherzadeh Z, Khaluyan H, Iranshahy M, et al., 2020 · PMID 32736046 · mixed

    In 50 patients with primary chronic insomnia, an intranasal oil of saffron, LACTUCA SATIVA seed and Viola odorata versus sesame-oil placebo over 8 weeks reduced Insomnia Severity Index scores significantly from baseline in the intervention group (p=0.001) but also in the placebo group (p<0.01); only the reduction in hypnotic drug use was significant in the intervention group alone (p<0.001). Different species from wild lettuce, multi-herb formula, non-oral route, and a substantial placebo response.

  7. Biomedical analysis of New Psychoactive Substances (NPS) of natural origin.
    Narrative reviewLo Faro AF, Di Trana A, La Maida N, Tagliabracci A, Giorgetti R, Busardo FP, 2019 · PMID 31704129 · mixed

    Review of psychoactive plants used as new psychoactive substances, listing Lactuca virosa alongside kratom, khat and Salvia divinorum among the plants whose active principles are investigated; the authors note that scarce data exist on characterisation and quantification of these parent drugs in biological matrices from intoxication and fatality cases, and that the metabolism of most of these natural active principles has not been fully investigated.

  8. [Abuse of lactuca virosa].
    Journal articleSpadari M, Pommier P, Canioni D, Arditti J, David JM, Valli M, 2003 · PMID 12762295 · negative

    French-language case report and letter in Presse Medicale documenting abuse of Lactuca virosa; the PubMed record carries no abstract, so no outcome figures can be quoted, but the article is indexed under Substance-Related Disorders, Heroin Dependence and Plants, Toxic. Recorded here as documentation that misuse of this plant has been reported in the clinical literature.

  9. Lettuce contact allergy.
    Narrative reviewPaulsen E, Andersen KE, 2015 · PMID 26289653 · negative

    Review of Lactuca contact allergy: lettuce is weakly allergenic but aimed patch testing in Compositae-allergic patients showed positive lettuce reactions in 11% and 22% in two Danish studies; the sesquiterpene lactone mix is a poor screening agent because prevalence is significantly higher in non-occupationally sensitised patients, and contact urticaria and protein contact dermatitis can present as dermatitis.
