Herbs

Reishi mushroom (Ganoderma lucidum)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

Reishi has been used in Chinese medicine for centuries as a calming, 'tranquilizing' mushroom, and it is marketed heavily for sleep today. But when we searched for human trials testing reishi for sleep, we could not find a single one — every study behind the sleep claims is in mice or rats. Two things are worth knowing before you try it: in a large survey of people actually taking reishi, about 3 in 100 reported insomnia as a side effect rather than a benefit, and there are published reports of serious liver injury, including one death.

Safety

Reishi is generally well tolerated in the human trials that exist, but those trials studied cancer, neurasthenia and fibromyalgia — not sleep — and were short. The 2012 Cochrane review reported only scattered minor adverse events with no significant haematological or hepatological toxicity across included studies. Against that, the 1374-person user survey found 9.1% reporting adverse events: dry mouth 5%, constipation 4%, insomnia 3%, pruritus 3%, vertigo 3%. Serious liver injury is documented and includes a death: a 2007 case report describes FATAL fulminant hepatitis associated with Lingzhi mushroom powder, following an earlier Hong Kong hepatotoxicity case first described in 2004, and a 2023 case report describes acute hepatitis with significant transaminitis in a 47-year-old man after consuming reishi powder with alcohol, which resolved fully within two weeks on N-acetylcysteine and IV fluids. A clinically important detail from the fatal series: both patients had tolerated traditionally boiled Lingzhi without incident and became ill only after switching to the POWDER preparation for 1-2 months, so preparation form may matter. Long-term safety at supplement doses is not established.

Who should be careful: Avoid, or use only under clinical supervision, with existing liver disease, heavy or regular alcohol use, or concurrent hepatotoxic medication, given published hepatotoxicity including a fatal case. Discuss with a clinician before use if immunosuppressed, on immunosuppressive therapy, or scheduled for surgery. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are unstudied. Stop and seek medical attention for jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain, or unexplained fatigue. Notably, because insomnia was itself reported as an adverse effect by 3% of surveyed users, anyone taking reishi specifically for sleep should watch for the opposite of the intended effect.

Interactions: Formal human interaction studies are lacking. Both reported liver-injury cases involved co-ingestion — one with alcohol, one with other therapeutic agents — so combining reishi with alcohol, hepatotoxic drugs, or multiple concurrent medications warrants real caution, and the authors of the fatal case specifically called for monitoring when Lingzhi is combined with other drugs. Because reishi extracts have documented immunomodulatory effects (the Cochrane review found increases in CD3, CD4 and CD8 percentages of 3.91%, 3.05% and 2.02% respectively), people on immunosuppressants or with autoimmune disease should consult a clinician. Anticoagulant interaction is often claimed commercially but was not substantiated by any trial retrieved here.

What the evidence says

No randomized controlled trial of Ganoderma lucidum with a sleep endpoint in humans was identified; the entire PubMed intersection of Ganoderma lucidum and sleep comprises 16 records, none of them human trials. The nearest human evidence is Tang et al. (2005), a 132-patient randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a G. lucidum polysaccharide extract (Ganopoly) 1800 mg three times daily for 8 weeks in neurasthenia, which reported a 15.5% reduction in Clinical Global Impression severity versus 4.9% for placebo and 51.6% (32/62) versus 24.6% (15/61) rated more than minimally improved (p=0.002) — but sleep was not among the assessed outcomes, which were limited to CGI and visual analogue scales for fatigue and well-being. A 2015 RCT in 64 women with fibromyalgia using 6 g/day for 6 weeks improved aerobic endurance, lower-body flexibility and velocity (p<0.05) but measured no sleep outcome despite naming non-restorative sleep as a defining disease feature, and the 2012 Cochrane review of 5 RCTs in cancer found insufficient evidence for first-line use with methodological quality described as generally unsatisfying. In a cross-sectional survey of 1374 Chinese cancer patients using reishi, more than half reported improvement in nausea (55%), fatigue (52%), appetite (51%) and depression (50%), while 125 (9.1%) reported adverse events including insomnia in 3%. The supportive sleep findings are entirely preclinical: in mice, 25-100 mg/kg of an acidic alcohol extract for 28 days shortened sleep latency and prolonged pentobarbital sleeping time via a gut-microbiota-dependent, serotonin-involved pathway that vanished after antibiotic microbiota depletion, and in a rat sporadic Alzheimer's model 720 mg/kg spore extract for 14 days increased total, REM and non-REM sleep time on EEG.

Doses studied: No sleep dose has been established in humans, because no human sleep trial exists. Doses used in the human trials cited here, none of which measured sleep, were Ganopoly polysaccharide extract 1800 mg three times daily (5.4 g/day) for 8 weeks in neurasthenia, and 6 g/day for 6 weeks in fibromyalgia. Animal sleep studies used 25-100 mg/kg (mice) and 720 mg/kg (rats), which do not translate to human supplement dosing.

Why this grade: Reishi is sold widely as a sleep and calming supplement, but a complete review of the PubMed literature on Ganoderma lucidum and sleep — 16 records in total, every one examined — contains no human trial with a sleep outcome of any kind. The nearest human RCT (Tang 2005) studied neurasthenia and measured fatigue, well-being and clinical global impression, not sleep. Other human RCTs in fibromyalgia and cancer did not measure sleep either, and the largest human dataset touching sleep is a cross-sectional survey in which insomnia appeared as an ADVERSE EFFECT in 3% of users. The genuinely suggestive sleep data are rodent studies with plausible mechanisms. Traditional use is well documented; adequate human sleep trials do not exist, which makes 'traditional' the correct grade and 'early' an overstatement.

Sources (8)
  1. A randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled study of a Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharide extract in neurasthenia.
    Tang W, Gao Y, Chen G, Gao H, Dai X, Ye J, Chan E, Huang M, Zhou S, 2005 · PMID 15857210 · positive

    132 patients with neurasthenia; Ganopoly 1800 mg three times daily for 8 weeks. CGI severity fell 15.5% versus 4.9% for placebo; sense of fatigue fell 28.3% versus 20.1%; 51.6% (32/62) versus 24.6% (15/61) rated more than minimally improved (p=0.002). Critically, SLEEP WAS NOT AN OUTCOME MEASURE — efficacy assessment was limited to CGI and visual analogue scales for fatigue and well-being.

  2. Symptom improvements and adverse effects with Reishi mushroom use: A Cross-Sectional survey of cancer patients.
    Li X, Sun L, Chimonas S, Li SQ, Feng P, Yang Y, Mao JJ, 2024 · PMID 39635077 · mixed

    Survey of 1374 cancer patients using reishi. More than half reported improvement in nausea (55%), fatigue (52%), appetite (51%) and depression (50%). However 125 participants (9.1%) reported adverse events, and INSOMNIA was among them at 3%, alongside dry mouth (5%), constipation (4%), pruritus (3%) and vertigo (3%). Sleep appeared as a harm, not a benefit. Uncontrolled cross-sectional design with no comparator.

  3. Ganoderma lucidum (Reishi mushroom) for cancer treatment.
    Jin X, Ruiz Beguerie J, Sze DM, Chan GCF, 2012 · PMID 22696372 · null

    Cochrane systematic review of 5 RCTs. Found insufficient evidence to justify G. lucidum as first-line cancer treatment; methodological quality of primary studies was 'generally unsatisfying'. No sleep outcome was assessed in any included trial. One study recorded minimal side effects including nausea and insomnia; no significant haematological or hepatological toxicity was reported across the included trials.

  4. GANODERMA LUCIDUM IMPROVES PHYSICAL FITNESS IN WOMEN WITH FIBROMYALGIA.
    Collado Mateo D, Pazzi F, Domínguez Muñoz FJ, Martín Martínez JP, Olivares PR, Gusi N, Adsuar JC, 2015 · PMID 26545669 · null

    64 women with fibromyalgia took 6 g/day of G. lucidum or Ceratonia siliqua for 6 weeks. G. lucidum significantly improved aerobic endurance, lower-body flexibility and velocity (p<0.05). Although non-restorative sleep is named as a defining feature of fibromyalgia in the paper itself, no sleep outcome was measured — a human trial that had the opportunity to test the sleep claim and did not.

  5. Fatal fulminant hepatitis associated with Ganoderma lucidum (Lingzhi) mushroom powder.
    Wanmuang H, Leopairut J, Kositchaiwat C, Wananukul W, Bunyaratvej S, 2007 · PMID 17621752 · negative

    SAFETY, FATAL. Case report of fatal fulminant hepatitis associated with Lingzhi mushroom powder, following an earlier Hong Kong hepatotoxicity case first described in 2004. Notably, both patients had previously taken traditionally boiled Lingzhi with no toxic effect, and the hepatotoxic episode occurred only after switching to the POWDER preparation for 1-2 months. Both had also taken other therapeutic agents. Authors call for close monitoring of Lingzhi powder, especially in combination with other drugs.

  6. Ganoderma lingzhi (Reishi Mushroom)-Induced Acute Liver Injury in the Setting of Alcohol Use: A Case Report and Review of the Literature.
    Guedikian R, Kim B, Singh G, Alexander R, 2023 · PMID 37885515 · negative

    SAFETY. A 47-year-old man presented with headache and abdominal pain after consuming Reishi mushroom powder together with alcohol, and was found to have acute hepatitis with significant transaminitis. Managed conservatively with N-acetylcysteine and IV fluids, with complete resolution of symptoms and laboratory abnormalities at two-week follow-up. Authors note that limited research exists on systemic effects of mushroom supplements co-ingested with other substances.

  7. Ganoderma lucidum promotes sleep through a gut microbiota-dependent and serotonin-involved pathway in mice.
    Yao C, Wang Z, Jiang H, Yan R, Huang Q, Wang Y, Xie H, Zou Y, Yu Y, Lv L, 2021 · PMID 34211003 · positive

    In mice, 25, 50 and 100 mg/kg of the acidic alcohol extract of G. lucidum mycelia for 28 days shortened sleep latency and prolonged pentobarbital sleeping time, raising hypothalamic 5-hydroxytryptamine and Tph2/Iptr3/Gng13 transcripts. The sleep-promoting effect and the associated faecal metabolite changes disappeared entirely after gut microbiota depletion with antibiotics. Rodent mechanism study; not a human trial.

  8. Ganoderma lucidum spore extract improves sleep disturbances in a rat model of sporadic Alzheimer's disease.
    Qin Y, Zhao Y, Hu X, Chen X, Jiang YP, Jin XJ, Li G, Li ZH, Yang JH, Zhang GL, Cui SY, Zhang YH, 2024 · PMID 38720773 · positive

    720 mg/kg sporoderm-removed G. lucidum spore extract for 14 days improved reductions in total sleep time, REM sleep time and non-REM sleep time on EEG in streptozotocin-induced sporadic Alzheimer's model rats, with inhibition of NF-kB/NLRP3 signalling in medial prefrontal cortex and improved parabrachial GABAergic activity. Animal model only; the dose does not translate to human supplement dosing.
