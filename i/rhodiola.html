Rhodiola (Rhodiola rosea)

Early evidence · for depression & low mood Early evidence · for fatigue & low energy

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For depression & low mood

Rhodiola is an Arctic root traditionally used for fatigue and stress, and a small number of studies suggest it may help with low mood and energy in mild depression, generally with very few side effects. But the most carefully run study - the only US placebo-controlled trial in people with diagnosed depression - found it did no better than a dummy pill, beating placebo by half a point on a scale where the antidepressant comparison drug gained more than three. Reviewers have repeatedly said the research so far is too small and too flawed to draw conclusions from. It is worth watching, but the evidence is not there yet, and it should not be relied on in place of real treatment.

Safety

Rhodiola is generally well tolerated in the short trials that exist. In the US placebo-controlled trial 30.0% of participants on rhodiola reported an adverse event, versus 16.7% on placebo and 63.2% on sertraline; there were no treatment-related serious adverse events, nobody discontinued rhodiola or placebo early, and no clinically meaningful changes in blood pressure, pulse, weight or laboratory values were seen. The Armenian trial reported no serious side-effects at either 340 or 680 mg/day, and a systematic review of 11 RCTs found only a few mild adverse events. Reported effects are typically mild and include dizziness, dry mouth, restlessness or jitteriness, and difficulty sleeping if taken later in the day - rhodiola is stimulating for some people, so morning dosing is the usual approach. The longest trial ran 12 weeks, so nothing is known about long-term use. One practical point: the trials used a single standardised extract with declared rosavin and salidroside content, so a product that does not state its standardisation is not the product that was studied.

Who should be careful: Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding - avoid. Not appropriate as self-directed treatment for bipolar depression: rhodiola is activating and, as with any agent with antidepressant or stimulant-like activity, a risk of triggering hypomania or mania in bipolar disorder is plausible and has not been studied; anyone with a history of mania should be assessed before use. Not a substitute for treatment of moderate-to-severe depression - the best-controlled trial excluded severe depression entirely and found no separation from placebo in the mild-to-moderate patients it did enrol, so it should not be relied on where treatment is genuinely needed. Not appropriate where there is suicidal thinking; the US trial excluded anyone actively suicidal. Caution in people with anxiety disorders or insomnia, since the activating effect can worsen both.

Interactions: Human interaction data are sparse - no trial in this evidence base was designed to detect interactions. Because rhodiola may have mild stimulant properties, caution is warranted with other stimulants, high caffeine intake, and with serotonergic or monoamine-oxidase-inhibiting medications; the US trial required a 14-day washout from MAO inhibitors before starting. Anyone on prescription medication - particularly antidepressants, blood pressure medication, or anything requiring blood-level monitoring - should check with a pharmacist first. Absence of documented interactions here reflects lack of study, not proof of safety.

What the evidence says

A 6-week Armenian phase III trial randomised 89 adults aged 18-70 with mild-to-moderate depression (baseline HAM-D 21-31) to SHR-5 extract at 340 mg/day (n=31), 680 mg/day (n=29) or placebo (n=29); both active groups improved on overall depression, insomnia, emotional instability and somatisation - though not self-esteem - while placebo did not. A 12-week US phase II trial (n=57) randomised participants with mild-to-moderate major depression to rhodiola, sertraline or placebo and found no statistically significant differences between groups on any measure (HAM-D p=0.79, BDI p=0.28, CGI/C p=0.17); HAM-D decline was -5.1 for rhodiola versus -4.6 for placebo and -8.2 for sertraline, which the authors describe as sertraline producing 3.6 points more reduction than placebo while rhodiola produced only 0.5 points more. Rhodiola's advantage was in tolerability (adverse events 30.0% vs 63.2% for sertraline and 16.7% for placebo, p=0.012). A Chinese trial (n=100) reported greater improvement when a Rhodiola capsule was added to sertraline than with sertraline alone, though it had no rhodiola-monotherapy arm and its published abstract reports the between-group result inconsistently. A 2011 systematic review of 11 RCTs found only two in diagnosed mental health conditions and concluded that independent replication is lacking. A 2012 systematic review of 11 fatigue trials found that all included studies exhibited either high or unclear risk of bias. A 2025 systematic review of pharmacological options in mild depression identified rhodiola as showing significant improvement versus placebo, but on the strength of a single study.

Doses studied: In the 6-week Armenian trial, the standardised SHR-5 rhizome extract at a fixed 340 mg/day or 680 mg/day. In the 12-week US placebo-controlled trial, SHR-5 340 mg capsules (standardised to rosavin 3.07% and rhodioloside 1.95%) given in a dose-escalation design - one capsule daily to start, increased stepwise to as many as four capsules daily (roughly 1,360 mg/day) in participants without a 50% reduction in HAM-D score. In the Chinese adjunct trial, a Rhodiola capsule at 0.3 g/day or 0.6 g/day added to sertraline for 12 weeks. Extracts are usually standardised to rosavins and salidroside, but standardisation differs between products and both depression trials used the same single proprietary extract.

Why this grade: The human evidence base for rhodiola in depression is small and unreplicated: essentially two placebo-controlled depression-specific trials plus one small adjunct trial, alongside a set of fatigue and physical-performance studies. Critically, the only US placebo-controlled trial in diagnosed major depressive disorder was null on all three of its outcome measures, and by week 12 rhodiola had produced only 0.5 points more HAM-D reduction than placebo. Both depression trials tested the same proprietary SHR-5 extract, so there is no independent product replication either. Two independent systematic reviews reached the same verdict - that findings are contradictory, that independent replication is lacking, and that every included study carried a high or unclear risk of bias. There is no meta-analysis of rhodiola in depression on which to build a higher grade; the one recent review reporting a significant effect did so on the strength of a single study. Early is the correct grade: preliminary human trials only, with the best-controlled one negative.

Sources (6)
  1. Rhodiola rosea versus sertraline for major depressive disorder: A randomized placebo-controlled trial.
    Randomised trialMao JJ, Xie SX, Zee J, et al., 2015 (Phytomedicine) · PMID 25837277 · null

    57 subjects randomised to 12 weeks of standardised R. rosea SHR-5 extract, sertraline or placebo, dose-escalated from 340 mg to as much as four 340 mg capsules daily. Modest but statistically non-significant reductions were seen in all three arms with no significant between-group difference (HAM-D p=0.79, BDI p=0.28, CGI/C p=0.17). HAM-D decline: sertraline -8.2 (95% CI -12.7 to -3.6), rhodiola -5.1 (95% CI -8.8 to -1.3), placebo -4.6 (95% CI -8.6 to -0.6); the authors note this is 3.6 points more than placebo for sertraline but only 0.5 points more for rhodiola. Odds of improvement vs placebo: sertraline 1.90 (0.44-8.20), rhodiola 1.39 (0.38-5.04). Adverse events: sertraline 63.2%, rhodiola 30.0%, placebo 16.7% (p=0.012), with no treatment-related serious adverse events and no premature discontinuation on rhodiola or placebo. Severe MDD and actively suicidal patients were excluded.

  2. Clinical trial of Rhodiola rosea L. extract SHR-5 in the treatment of mild to moderate depression.
    Randomised trialDarbinyan V, Aslanyan G, Amroyan E, et al., 2007 (Nord J Psychiatry) · PMID 17990195 · positive

    89 adults aged 18-70 meeting DSM-IV criteria for depression with baseline HAM-D 21-31, randomised to SHR-5 340 mg/day (n=31), 680 mg/day (n=29) or placebo (n=29) for 6 weeks. Both active groups improved significantly on overall depression, insomnia, emotional instability and somatisation, but not on self-esteem; the placebo group showed no such improvements. No serious side-effects were reported in any group. Single-site, 6 weeks, tests the same proprietary SHR-5 extract as the null US trial, and never independently replicated.

  3. The effectiveness and efficacy of Rhodiola rosea L.: a systematic review of randomized clinical trials.
    Systematic reviewHung SK, Perry R, Ernst E, 2011 (Phytomedicine) · PMID 21036578 · mixed

    Eleven placebo-controlled RCTs met inclusion criteria; six examined physical performance, four mental performance, and only two involved patients diagnosed with a mental health condition. Only 5 of 10 RCTs reached more than three points on the Jadad scale. Authors concluded that R. rosea may have beneficial effects but that there is a lack of independent replication of the individual studies, and that more research is warranted. Only few mild adverse events were reported.

  4. Rhodiola rosea for physical and mental fatigue: a systematic review.
    Systematic reviewIshaque S, Shamseer L, Bukutu C, Vohra S, 2012 (BMC Complement Altern Med) · PMID 22643043 · mixed

    11 of 206 identified articles met inclusion criteria. Two of six trials of physical fatigue and three of five trials of mental fatigue reported R. rosea to be effective. All of the included studies exhibited either a high risk of bias or reporting flaws that hindered assessment of their true validity. Authors concluded that research regarding efficacy is contradictory and that methodological flaws limit accurate assessment.

  5. Antidepressants effects of Rhodiola capsule combined with sertraline for major depressive disorder: A randomized double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial.
    Randomised trialGao L, Wu C, Liao Y, Wang J, 2020 (J Affect Disord) · PMID 32090788 · positive

    100 patients with mild-to-moderate MDD randomised for 12 weeks to sertraline plus placebo (n=33), sertraline plus Rhodiola capsule 0.6 g/day (n=33), or sertraline plus Rhodiola capsule 0.3 g/day (n=34). Significant post-treatment improvements in HAM-D, BDI and CGI were reported in all arms, with greater declines in the 0.6 g/day group. Two limits on how much weight this can carry: it is an adjunct design with no rhodiola-monotherapy arm, so it cannot establish standalone efficacy; and the published abstract states the between-group result inconsistently across its results and conclusion sections, which is a reporting-quality concern.

  6. Efficacy of Pharmacological Interventions in Milder Depression: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Meta-analysisUrata M, Sakurai H, Ueno F, et al., 2025 (Neuropsychopharmacol Rep) · PMID 40014460 · mixed

    Eight RCTs comprising 1,049 participants met inclusion criteria for mild depression diagnosed by standardised criteria. Eicosapentaenoic acid and Rhodiola rosea each demonstrated significant improvements in depressive symptoms compared with placebo - but the authors state that for these agents 'single studies evaluated their effects'. They conclude that the paucity of high-quality RCTs exclusively targeting mild depression limits definitive conclusions.

For fatigue & low energy

Rhodiola is popular as an 'adaptogen' for tiredness, and a few small studies — in students, in military cadets, and one in adults diagnosed with a stress-related fatigue syndrome — did report less fatigue. But those studies are small and mostly poorly conducted, and the one carefully designed independent trial, in nurses working shifts, actually found people taking rhodiola felt MORE tired than those on the dummy capsule. Honestly, we do not yet know whether it helps, and no one has repeated any single result convincingly.

Safety

Short-term tolerability appears good in the trials conducted so far. The 42-day nursing-student trial found no difference in total adverse events between rhodiola and placebo, the 28-day stress-related-fatigue trial reported no serious side effects attributable to the extract, the 8-week open-label trial reported mostly mild events judged unrelated to the study drug, and the 2010 systematic review of 11 RCTs reported only a few mild adverse events. However, the trials are small and rarely exceed 6-8 weeks, so long-term safety is genuinely unknown. Safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding has not been established. Note also that R. rosea is subject to conservation concerns from over-collection, and that product content and standardization (typically to salidroside and rosavins) vary widely between brands, so one product's trial results may not transfer to another product.

Who should be careful: Because the one well-designed independent trial found fatigue significantly WORSE on rhodiola than on placebo, anyone taking it for fatigue should treat worsening tiredness as a reason to stop rather than to increase the dose. Rhodiola is not a substitute for finding a cause: fatigue lasting weeks should be evaluated for anemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnea, diabetes, depression and other treatable causes before or alongside any supplement.

Interactions: Formal drug-interaction studies are lacking. In a published case series of supplement-associated liver injury from Iceland and the US Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network, rhodiola was named as a possible contributing agent alongside ashwagandha in one case where the patient was taking multiple supplements — a reason to be cautious about stacking rhodiola with other herbal products, and to tell your prescriber everything you are taking.

What the evidence says

The rhodiola fatigue literature is small, contradictory, and conducted mostly in healthy stressed populations. In healthy volunteers, a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 161 military cadets aged 19-21 found a single dose of standardized SHR-5 extract produced an 'antifatigue index' of 1.0385 and 1.0195 (two- and three-capsule doses) versus 0.9046 for placebo (P < 0.001 for both doses, with no significant difference between them), and a 20-day low-dose SHR-5 trial in students during an examination period reported significant improvement in physical fitness, mental fatigue and neuro-motoric tests (p < 0.01) and self-rated general well-being (p < 0.05), though the authors labelled it a pilot whose dose was probably suboptimal. In people with clinically defined fatigue the evidence is thin but not absent: a 60-person randomized double-blind phase III trial in adults meeting Swedish national diagnostic criteria for fatigue syndrome found SHR-5 576 mg/day for 28 days significantly better than placebo on Pines' burnout scale and on several CCPT II attention indices with a significantly altered cortisol response to awakening, while a 100-subject open-label uncontrolled multicentre trial of a different extract (WS 1375, 2 x 200 mg for 8 weeks) in prolonged or chronic fatigue reported improvement that, without a control group, cannot be distinguished from placebo response. Against this, the most rigorously designed and independently conducted trial found harm rather than benefit: in 48 nursing students on shift work taking 364 mg/day for 42 days, change in RAND-36 Vitality favoured PLACEBO by 17.3 points (95% CI -30.6 to -3.9, p = 0.011), cross-validated by the VAS-F which also favoured placebo (1.9, 95% CI 0.4 to 3.5, p = 0.015), and the authors concluded rhodiola worsened fatigue in this group. Two systematic reviews frame the field: a 2012 review of 11 trials found only 2 of 6 physical-fatigue trials and 3 of 5 mental-fatigue trials positive with ALL included studies at high or unclear risk of bias, and a 2010 review of 11 placebo-controlled RCTs found only 5 of 10 scored above 3 on the Jadad scale and concluded there is a lack of independent replication of any single study. A 2025 randomized triple-blind crossover trial in 18 participants found only trivial-to-small effects on mental fatigue, visuo-cognitive processing and perceived exertion, with rhodiola's main signal on strength performance rather than fatigue; no adequate randomized trial has tested rhodiola in ME/CFS, post-viral fatigue or cancer-related fatigue.

Doses studied: Standardized SHR-5 root extract is the most-tested preparation: 364 mg/day (with an optional second capsule, up to 728 mg/day) for 42 days in the negative nursing-student trial; 576 mg/day for 28 days in the stress-related fatigue syndrome trial; two or three capsules as a single dose in the cadet study; and a low repeated-dose regimen over 20 days in the student examination study. A different extract (WS 1375) was given as 2 x 200 mg for 8 weeks in an uncontrolled trial. Doses, extracts and standardization (typically to salidroside and rosavins) differ across trials, which limits comparison between them.

Why this grade: Graded early. The human trials are small (most under 200 participants, several under 50 or 60), short, and mostly conducted in healthy students, cadets and athletes; the one randomized trial in adults with a diagnosed fatigue syndrome enrolled 60 people for 28 days and has not been independently replicated, and the one trial in prolonged or chronic fatigue with 100 subjects was open-label and uncontrolled, so its improvement cannot be separated from placebo response. Two independent systematic reviews concluded the literature is contradictory, that every included trial carried high or unclear risk of bias or reporting flaws, and that no single result has been independently replicated. Critically, the most rigorously designed and independently conducted trial — in nursing students on shift work — found rhodiola significantly WORSE than placebo on both of its fatigue measures. Small unreplicated positives plus one well-conducted negative is exactly 'early', not 'moderate'; it is not graded not_supported because the negative trial is single and the overall trial base is too weak to count as adequate evidence of absence.

  1. Rhodiola rosea for mental and physical fatigue in nursing students: a randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialPunja et al., 2014 · PMID 25268730 · negative

    48 nursing students on shift work randomized to 364 mg/day R. rosea (with an optional second capsule) or identical placebo for 42 days. Change in RAND-36 Vitality favoured placebo by 17.3 points (95% CI -30.6 to -3.9, p = 0.011); the VAS-F also favoured placebo (1.9, 95% CI 0.4 to 3.5, p = 0.015). Total adverse events did not differ. The authors concluded that a 42-day course of R. rosea worsened fatigue compared with placebo, while cautioning about interpretation.

  2. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group study of the standardised extract shr-5 of the roots of Rhodiola rosea in the treatment of subjects with stress-related fatigue.
    Randomised trialOlsson, von Schéele and Panossian, 2009 · PMID 19016404 · positive

    60 adults aged 20-55 selected by the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare diagnostic criteria for fatigue syndrome, randomized to SHR-5 576 mg/day (four tablets) or placebo for 28 days. Both groups improved on Pines' burnout scale, SF-36 mental health, MADRS and several CCPT II attention indices (a substantial placebo effect), but between-group comparison significantly favoured SHR-5 on Pines' burnout scale and on the CCPT II indices omissions, Hit RT SE and variability, with a significantly different pre- versus post-treatment cortisol response to awakening. No serious side effects attributed to the extract. Single trial, 60 participants, 28 days, not independently replicated.

  3. Rhodiola rosea for physical and mental fatigue: a systematic review.
    Systematic reviewIshaque et al., 2012 · PMID 22643043 · mixed

    11 included trials (10 RCTs, 1 CCT) out of 206 articles screened. Only 2 of 6 physical-fatigue trials and 3 of 5 mental-fatigue trials reported R. rosea effective. ALL included studies exhibited either high risk of bias or reporting flaws that hindered assessment of their true validity; the authors called the research contradictory and called for a rigorously designed, well-reported RCT.

  4. The effectiveness and efficacy of Rhodiola rosea L.: a systematic review of randomized clinical trials.
    Systematic reviewHung, Perry and Ernst, 2010 · PMID 21036578 · mixed

    11 placebo-controlled RCTs (6 on physical performance, 4 on mental performance, 2 in diagnosed mental-health conditions). Only 5 of 10 scored more than three points on the Jadad scale. The authors concluded R. rosea may have beneficial effects but that there is a lack of independent replication of the individual studies. Only a few mild adverse events were reported.

  5. A randomized trial of two different doses of a SHR-5 Rhodiola rosea extract versus placebo and control of capacity for mental work.
    Randomised trialShevtsov et al., 2003 · PMID 12725561 · positive

    161 cadets aged 19-21, single dose of standardized SHR-5 extract against a background of fatigue and stress. Antifatigue index 1.0385 (2 capsules) and 1.0195 (3 capsules) versus 0.9046 for placebo, P < 0.001 for both doses, with no significant difference between the two active doses and a possible trend favouring the lower dose on psychometric tests.

  6. A double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study of the stimulating and adaptogenic effect of Rhodiola rosea SHR-5 extract on the fatigue of students caused by stress during an examination period with a repeated low-dose regimen.
    Randomised trialSpasov et al., 2000 · PMID 10839209 · positive

    Pilot double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial in foreign students over a 20-day examination period. Significant improvement in physical fitness, mental fatigue and neuro-motoric tests (p < 0.01) and self-assessed general well-being (p < 0.05); no significant effect on text-correction or neuro-muscular tapping tests. The authors judged the dose probably suboptimal and described the study as a pilot.

  7. Rhodiola rosea in Subjects with Prolonged or Chronic Fatigue Symptoms: Results of an Open-Label Clinical Trial.
    Clinical trialLekomtseva, Zhukova and Wacker, 2017 · PMID 28219059 · positive

    100 subjects with prolonged or chronic fatigue symptoms given a dry ethanolic R. rosea extract (WS 1375) 2 x 200 mg for 8 weeks in an UNCONTROLLED, OPEN-LABEL multicentre trial. The greatest change came after 1 week, with fatigue continuing to decline and statistically significant improvement at week 8; most adverse events were mild and judged unrelated. Because there was no control group and no blinding, this result cannot be separated from placebo response and natural fluctuation, and the authors describe it as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

  8. The Impact of Rhodiola Rosea Extract on Strength Performance in Alternative Bench-Press and Bench-Pull Exercises Under Resting and Mental Fatigue Conditions: A Randomized, Triple-Blinded, Placebo-Controlled, Crossover Trial.
    Randomised trialMarcos-Frutos et al., 2025 · PMID 40289957 · null

    18 healthy participants (7 women), 4 days of rhodiola supplementation, triple-blind crossover with Stroop or control-video conditions. Rhodiola had only trivial-to-small effects on mental fatigue, visuo-cognitive processing and perceived exertion; of 52 comparisons only 17 reached a small effect size (14 favouring rhodiola, 3 favouring placebo), the remainder trivial, with the main ergogenic signal on strength performance rather than fatigue. Two authors are affiliated with a supplement ingredient company's R&D department.
