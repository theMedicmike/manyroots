Conventional medicine

CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure)

Strong evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

If your sleep is broken because you repeatedly stop breathing at night, CPAP is the treatment with the strongest evidence behind it - it reduces apnea events, daytime sleepiness, blood pressure and car crashes, and improves sleep-related quality of life. But it is important to be clear about what it fixes: CPAP treats the breathing problem, not insomnia. Many people have both at once, and in that case CPAP alone often leaves the insomnia largely untouched - the trials suggest doing insomnia therapy (CBT-I) first actually makes people use their CPAP more.

Safety

CPAP itself is physically safe and non-pharmacological; the adverse effects are local and manageable - nasal congestion and dryness, mask leak, skin irritation or pressure marks, aerophagia (swallowed air), dry mouth and claustrophobia. Heated humidification reduces some CPAP-related side effects per the AASM meta-analysis, and nasal interfaces showed better adherence than oronasal masks. The real limitation is use: adherence, not efficacy, is what determines benefit, and behavioural, educational, troubleshooting and telemonitoring support meaningfully improves it. The most important caution for this site's audience is scope: CPAP treats apnea, not insomnia. Starting CPAP in someone with untreated insomnia can be difficult - the mask plus long prescribed time in bed can aggravate the insomnia - and comorbid insomnia reduces both initial acceptance of and later adherence to CPAP, which is why the trials favour treating the insomnia with CBT-I first or concurrently.

Who should be careful: CPAP requires a diagnosis - it should never be started on the assumption of apnea without a sleep study (in-laboratory polysomnography or a validated home sleep apnea test), because a negative or unperformed test means an insomnia problem is being treated with the wrong device. Relative cautions include severe bullous lung disease, recurrent epistaxis, recent craniofacial or transsphenoidal surgery or CSF leak, severe untreated nausea/vomiting or impaired ability to remove the mask, and untreated severe nasal obstruction. People with mainly central rather than obstructive events need a different device strategy.

Interactions: Not a drug, so no pharmacokinetic interactions, but sedatives matter: alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids and other CNS depressants depress respiratory drive and worsen upper-airway collapse and oxygen desaturation in OSA, and should be discussed with the treating clinician rather than added independently. The COMISA trial evidence points the same way - adding a hypnotic (eszopiclone) to behavioural therapy produced no additional benefit at 24 weeks and no improvement in CPAP use, while CBT-I before CPAP improved both insomnia severity and device use. Nasal steroids or saline can help with congestion that limits mask tolerance.

What the evidence says

CPAP is first-line treatment for obstructive sleep apnea, and where untreated OSA is the reason sleep is fragmented and unrefreshing, the evidence is strong: the AASM systematic review identified 336 studies meeting inclusion criteria with 184 providing data suitable for meta-analysis, and found that PAP compared with no treatment produces clinically significant reductions in disease severity, sleepiness and blood pressure, fewer motor vehicle accidents, and improved sleep-related quality of life in adults with OSA. What CPAP does not do is treat insomnia. Roughly 30-50% of patients with OSA report clinically significant insomnia symptoms and 30-40% of patients with chronic insomnia meet diagnostic criteria for OSA - comorbid insomnia and sleep apnea, or COMISA - a combination associated with greater morbidity and with reduced response to treatments that work for either disorder alone. A 2026 network meta-analysis of 23 articles and 13 conference abstracts, with 10 studies and 768 patients eligible for pooling, found that PAP-based combination therapies improved insomnia symptoms more than PAP alone, and that in pairwise analysis only the sequence CBT-I followed by PAP reduced daytime sleepiness. In a 145-patient randomised trial of adults with apnea-hypopnea index >=15 plus comorbid insomnia, delivering four sessions of CBT-I before starting CPAP increased average nightly CPAP use by 61 min (95% CI 9-113, p=0.023, d=0.38) and initial treatment acceptance (99% vs 89%, p=0.034) and improved insomnia severity, but produced no between-group differences in sleep outcomes or daytime impairments by 6 months. A separate 53-patient trial in veterans with PTSD and COMISA found that adding eszopiclone to brief behavioural therapy for insomnia gave no additional benefit over behavioural therapy alone at 24 weeks (PSQI change -5.24 vs -5.45) and no difference in CPAP use - reinforcing that in this population the behavioural component, not added sedation, is what moves insomnia. Clinical reviews of COMISA management converge on the same model: assess for both disorders and treat them concomitantly with CBT-I alongside PAP rather than assuming the device alone will fix the sleep complaint.

Doses studied: Nightly device use; adherence is effectively the dose, and educational, behavioural, troubleshooting and telemonitoring interventions produced clinically significant improvements in adherence in the AASM meta-analysis. In the COMISA randomised trial, CBT-I delivered before CPAP raised average nightly use by 61 minutes versus treatment as usual. Auto-adjusting and bilevel PAP did not differ clinically from standard continuous PAP, and home initiation was equivalent to in-laboratory titration.

Why this grade: Graded strong strictly and only for poor sleep driven by obstructive sleep apnea. The AASM systematic review identified 336 qualifying studies with 184 contributing to meta-analysis and found PAP versus no treatment produced clinically significant reductions in disease severity, sleepiness and blood pressure, fewer motor vehicle accidents, and improved sleep-related quality of life; PAP is first-line guideline therapy for moderate-to-severe OSA. This grade does NOT transfer to insomnia disorder. In comorbid insomnia and sleep apnea, PAP-based combination therapies outperform PAP alone for insomnia symptoms, and the largest COMISA randomised trial found no between-group difference in sleep outcomes or daytime impairment at 6 months. Anyone whose sleep problem is insomnia without apnea should read this entry as not applicable.

  1. Treatment of Adult Obstructive Sleep Apnea With Positive Airway Pressure: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and GRADE Assessment.
    Meta-analysisPatil et al., 2019 · PMID 30736888 · positive

    336 studies met inclusion criteria and 184 provided data suitable for meta-analysis: PAP versus no treatment produced clinically significant reductions in disease severity, sleepiness, blood pressure and motor vehicle accidents, and improved sleep-related quality of life in adults with OSA. Home initiation was equivalent to in-laboratory titration; auto-adjusting and bilevel PAP produced no clinically significant differences versus continuous PAP; educational, behavioural, troubleshooting and telemonitoring interventions clinically significantly improved adherence; nasal interfaces had improved adherence versus oronasal interfaces; heated humidification reduced some CPAP-related side effects.

  2. Bi-directional relationships between co-morbid insomnia and sleep apnea (COMISA).
    Narrative reviewSweetman et al., 2021 · PMID 34229295 · mixed

    Approximately 30-50% of patients with OSA report clinically significant insomnia symptoms, and 30-40% of patients with chronic insomnia fulfil diagnostic criteria for OSA. Compared with either disorder alone, COMISA is associated with greater morbidity, more complex diagnostic and treatment decisions, and reduced response to treatment approaches that are otherwise effective - i.e. treating the apnea alone does not reliably resolve the insomnia.

  3. Cognitive and behavioral therapy for insomnia increases the use of continuous positive airway pressure therapy in obstructive sleep apnea participants with comorbid insomnia: a randomized clinical trial.
    Randomised trialSweetman et al., 2019 · PMID 31403168 · mixed

    145 participants with apnea-hypopnea index >=15 plus comorbid insomnia randomised to four sessions of CBT-I or treatment as usual before starting CPAP: CBT-I gave 61 min greater average nightly CPAP adherence (95% CI 9 to 113, p=0.023, d=0.38), higher initial CPAP acceptance (99% vs 89%, p=0.034) and greater improvement in insomnia severity and dysfunctional sleep-related cognitions by 6 months (both p<0.001), but there were NO between-group differences in sleep outcomes or daytime impairments by 6 months.

  4. Comparative efficacy of positive airway pressure-based combination therapy in comorbid insomnia and sleep apnea: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisZhang et al., 2026 · PMID 42107468 · mixed

    23 articles and 13 conference abstracts included, 10 studies with 768 patients eligible for meta-analysis: PAP-based combination therapies (PAP+medication, CBT-I->PAP, CBT-I+PAP, adaptive servo-ventilation, and triple therapy) significantly improved insomnia symptoms and were associated with greater improvement in insomnia than PAP alone; in pairwise analysis only CBT-I followed by PAP reduced daytime sleepiness, most evident at 4-14 weeks of follow-up. Triple-therapy rankings were probabilistic and the authors cautioned against over-reading them given limited direct evidence.

  5. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Alone or in Combination with Eszopiclone in Comorbid Insomnia and Obstructive Sleep Apnea in Veterans with Posttraumatic Stress Disorder: A Randomized Trial.
    Randomised trialEl-Solh et al., 2025 · PMID 40202618 · null

    53 veterans with PTSD and COMISA randomised to brief behavioural therapy for insomnia plus 2 weeks of eszopiclone 2 mg/day versus behavioural therapy alone: PSQI fell by -5.24 (95% CI -6.55 to -3.94) versus -5.45 (95% CI -6.75 to -4.14) at 24 weeks with no group-by-time interaction, ISI fell -8.32 versus -8.64 with no interaction, and mixed-effect models showed no group-by-time effects for CPAP use. Adding the hypnotic conferred only an early advantage in insomnia remission at 6 weeks.

  6. Sleep Apnea and Insomnia: Emerging Evidence for Effective Clinical Management.
    Narrative reviewOng et al., 2020 · PMID 33309524 · mixed

    Review of COMISA clinical management: recent randomised controlled trials provide evidence on the safety and effectiveness of a concomitant treatment approach using cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia together with positive airway pressure, and the authors recommend a multidisciplinary, patient-centered care model integrating patient characteristics, treatment preferences and treatment accessibility rather than treating the apnea in isolation.
