Conventional medicine

Gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin) off-label for sleep

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Gabapentin and pregabalin are nerve-pain and seizure medicines that get handed out for sleep quite often, but almost nobody has tested them properly in people who actually have insomnia. The trials that do exist mostly took people with occasional bad nights, deliberately shifted their bedtime five hours earlier to disrupt sleep, and showed gabapentin helped them stay asleep in that artificial setup - while the one placebo-controlled trial in people who really did have insomnia found no difference from placebo, and a large review of these drugs in psychiatry called the insomnia results inconclusive. They also carry real downsides - dizziness, falls, withdrawal, misuse, and a dangerous combination with opioids - so the gap between how often they are used for sleep and what has been shown is wide.

Safety

Common effects are dizziness, somnolence, unsteadiness, blurred vision, peripheral oedema, weight gain and cognitive slowing; in older adults these translate into a meaningful falls and fracture risk. Both drugs cause a withdrawal syndrome (anxiety, insomnia, sweating, agitation) if stopped abruptly, so tapering is required - and rebound insomnia on withdrawal is a particular trap when the drug was started for sleep. Misuse and diversion are documented: pregabalin is a Schedule V controlled substance federally in the US (gabapentin is scheduled in some states but not federally), and both drugs are controlled under the Misuse of Drugs Act in the UK. Gabapentin is renally cleared and accumulates in kidney impairment, requiring dose adjustment. Regulators have warned about serious breathing difficulties when gabapentinoids are combined with opioids or used in people with reduced respiratory reserve. Safety data specifically for long-term use as a sleep aid are essentially unstudied, because the trials for that use do not exist.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindication other than hypersensitivity, but avoid or dose-reduce carefully in significant renal impairment, avoid combining with opioids where possible, and use with real caution in older adults, in people with a history of substance misuse, and in anyone with compromised respiratory function or untreated sleep apnea. Do not stop abruptly.

Interactions: Opioids are the most dangerous combination - additive respiratory depression, with reported deaths. Additive sedation and fall risk with benzodiazepines, z-drugs, alcohol, sedating antihistamines, trazodone and muscle relaxants. Antacids containing aluminium or magnesium reduce gabapentin absorption; separate doses by at least two hours.

What the evidence says

Gabapentin and pregabalin are widely prescribed off-label for sleep, but the human trial evidence in chronic insomnia disorder is close to absent. A systematic review and meta-analysis covering 55 double-blind RCTs and 15 open-label studies of gabapentinoids across bipolar disorder, anxiety and insomnia concluded that in insomnia 'results were inconclusive', found only moderate evidence in anxiety states and minimal evidence in bipolar disorder and insomnia, and recommended these drugs be used for those conditions only with strong justification. The two largest positive sleep trials did not enrol people with insomnia disorder: both recruited adults reporting occasional disturbed sleep and induced transient insomnia with a 5-hour sleep-phase advance. In the single-dose trial (n=377), polysomnographic wake after sleep onset was 135.7 min on placebo versus 100.7 min on gabapentin 250 mg and 73.2 min on 500 mg, with total sleep time 311.4, 356.5 and 378.7 min respectively, and no significant difference in latency to persistent sleep. In the 28-day trial (n=256), gabapentin 250 mg reduced wake after sleep onset from 149.1 to 107.0 min on day 1 and from 152.3 to 113.6 min on day 28, with greater total sleep time and no evidence of next-day impairment. Against those sit two more directly relevant results. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of gabapentin titrated to 1,500 mg at bedtime in 21 adults with alcohol dependence and diagnosed insomnia found that insomnia improved in both arms and that gabapentin had no differential effect on sleep by either subjective report or polysomnography - the only placebo-controlled sleep trial in a population that actually had insomnia. And the sole study conducted in primary insomnia is uncontrolled: 18 patients treated for at least four weeks with pre-post comparisons and no placebo arm, reporting increased sleep efficiency and slow-wave sleep and decreased wake after sleep onset. Pregabalin evidence is thinner still: an exploratory 4-week polysomnographic study in 15 adults with epilepsy and subjective sleep disturbance found sleep efficiency improved significantly in both the pregabalin and placebo arms with no significant between-group difference, though pregabalin reduced the number of awakenings (p=0.02) with wake after sleep onset approaching significance (p=0.055). Neither the 2017 AASM pharmacologic guideline, which issued individual recommendations for 14 named agents, nor the 2023 European Insomnia Guideline includes gabapentin or pregabalin among the drugs it recommends - or among those it explicitly recommends against. Reviews of off-label gabapentinoid prescribing separately note very limited evidence outside licensed indications alongside documented diversion and misuse.

Doses studied: Gabapentin 250 mg and 500 mg as a single bedtime dose, and gabapentin 250 mg nightly for 28 days, both in a 5-hour phase-advance transient-insomnia model in people with occasional disturbed sleep. Gabapentin titrated to 1,500 mg at bedtime for 6 weeks in the placebo-controlled trial in alcohol dependence with insomnia, which was null for sleep. Gabapentin for at least 4 weeks in the uncontrolled primary-insomnia study (dose not stated in the abstract). Pregabalin 300 mg/day adjunctively for 4 weeks in 15 adults with epilepsy. No dose has been established in chronic insomnia disorder because no adequate trial in that population exists.

Why this grade: Graded early because there is no adequate randomised trial of gabapentin or pregabalin in adults with chronic insomnia disorder. The two positive polysomnographic trials used a laboratory 5-hour phase-advance transient-insomnia model in people reporting occasional disturbed sleep, not diagnosed insomnia; the only study in primary insomnia is an uncontrolled 18-patient before-and-after study; the one randomised placebo-controlled trial in people who genuinely had insomnia found no differential effect of gabapentin on sleep by either self-report or polysomnography; the pregabalin polysomnographic data come from an exploratory 15-person study in epilepsy where sleep efficiency improved in both arms with no between-group difference; and a systematic review of 55 double-blind RCTs across psychiatric indications concluded that for insomnia 'results were inconclusive'. Not graded not_supported because the evidence for the target condition is absent and inconclusive rather than adequately null, and no guideline recommends against these drugs for sleep - they simply do not appear in the guidelines at all. Not graded higher because none of the supportive trial evidence is in the condition people are actually prescribed these drugs for.

Sources (9)
  1. Gabapentin and pregabalin in bipolar disorder, anxiety states, and insomnia: Systematic review, meta-analysis, and rationale.
    Hong et al., 2021 · PMID 34819636 · null

    55 double-blind RCTs and 15 open-label studies identified across three psychiatric indications. For insomnia specifically, 'results were inconclusive'. The authors concluded there is moderate evidence of efficacy in anxiety states but minimal evidence in bipolar disorder and insomnia, and that gabapentinoids should be used for these disorders only with strong justification, explicitly noting increasing concern about their safety.

  2. A randomized double-blind pilot trial of gabapentin versus placebo to treat alcohol dependence and comorbid insomnia.
    Brower et al., 2008 · PMID 18540923 · null

    21 adults meeting criteria for both alcohol dependence and insomnia randomised to gabapentin (n=10, titrated to 1,500 mg at bedtime) or placebo (n=11) for 6 weeks, with other causes of insomnia ruled out. Gabapentin significantly delayed onset to heavy drinking, but 'insomnia improved in both treatment groups during the medication phase, and gabapentin had no differential effects on sleep as measured by either subjective report or polysomnography'. The only placebo-controlled sleep trial in a population with diagnosed insomnia, and it was null for sleep.

  3. A randomized, double-blind, single-dose, placebo-controlled, multicenter, polysomnographic study of gabapentin in transient insomnia induced by sleep phase advance.
    Rosenberg et al., 2014 · PMID 25317090 · positive

    377 adults reporting occasional disturbed sleep (NOT insomnia disorder), given a single bedtime dose after a 5-hour phase advance: wake after sleep onset 135.7 min (placebo) vs 100.7 min (gabapentin 250 mg) vs 73.2 min (500 mg); total sleep time 311.4 vs 356.5 vs 378.7 min. Latency to persistent sleep was NOT significantly different between groups. Adverse events were infrequent (<5%).

  4. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter, 28-day, polysomnographic study of gabapentin in transient insomnia induced by sleep phase advance.
    Furey et al., 2014 · PMID 25317091 · positive

    256 adults with occasional disturbed sleep, gabapentin 250 mg (n=128) vs placebo (n=128) across 28 days in a 5-hour phase-advance model: polysomnographic wake after sleep onset 107.0 vs 149.1 min on day 1 (p<=0.001) and 113.6 vs 152.3 min on day 28 (p=0.002), with greater total sleep time and no evidence of next-day impairment. The population was an experimental insomnia model, not chronic insomnia disorder.

  5. Treatment effects of gabapentin for primary insomnia.
    Lo et al., 2010 · PMID 20124884 · positive

    The only study performed in patients with primary insomnia: 18 patients treated for at least 4 weeks, with polysomnography, blood tests and neuropsychological testing before and after. Sleep efficiency and slow-wave sleep increased and wake after sleep onset and spontaneous arousal index decreased. Design limitation is decisive - there was no placebo or control arm and results were analysed as within-patient pre-post t-tests, so placebo response and regression to the mean cannot be excluded; the paper drew a published methodological critique letter in the same journal.

  6. Exploratory polysomnographic evaluation of pregabalin on sleep disturbance in patients with epilepsy.
    de Haas et al., 2007 · PMID 17803010 · mixed

    Exploratory 4-week double-blind pilot, 15 patients with well-controlled partial seizures and subjective sleep disturbance (pregabalin 300 mg/day n=8, placebo n=7): mean sleep efficiency improved significantly in BOTH groups with no significant between-group difference; pregabalin reduced the number of awakenings (p=0.02) and wake time after sleep onset approached significance (p=0.055).

  7. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    Sateia et al., 2017 · PMID 27998379 · null

    The guideline deliberately covered individual drugs used for insomnia including agents without an FDA insomnia indication, and issued 14 named recommendations (suvorexant, eszopiclone, zaleplon, zolpidem, triazolam, temazepam, ramelteon, doxepin for; trazodone, tiagabine, diphenhydramine, melatonin, tryptophan, valerian against). Neither gabapentin nor pregabalin appears among the agents for which sufficient evidence existed to establish a recommendation.

  8. The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.
    Riemann et al., 2023 · PMID 38016484 · null

    Current European guideline. Recommended pharmacological options are benzodiazepines (A), benzodiazepine receptor agonists (A), daridorexant (A), low-dose sedating antidepressants (B), orexin receptor antagonists (A) and prolonged-release melatonin in patients >=55 (B); antihistaminergic drugs, antipsychotics, fast-release melatonin, ramelteon and phytotherapeutics are not recommended (A). Gabapentin and pregabalin appear in neither the recommended nor the not-recommended list, confirming that no current guideline endorses gabapentinoids for insomnia.

  9. Gabapentin and pregabalin: do the benefits outweigh the harms?
    Morrison et al., 2017 · PMID 29537399 · negative

    Review of substantially increased gabapentinoid prescribing: patients may not benefit from gabapentin and pregabalin yet remain at risk of adverse drug reactions; greater availability has increased diversion, creating problems within the opioid-misuse population and prison service, and prescribers should be aware of the very limited clinical evidence for use outside licensed indications as well as the capacity of these drugs to do harm.
