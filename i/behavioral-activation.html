Behavioural Activation

Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood

For depression & low mood

Behavioural activation works from the outside in: instead of trying to change your thoughts first, you rebuild contact with activities that used to matter to you, and mood tends to follow. Its strongest selling point is practical - a large trial found it worked about as well as full CBT when delivered by junior mental health workers rather than highly trained therapists, which makes it far easier to actually get. Be honest about the limits, though: the biggest review found its advantage over usual care disappeared once researchers accounted for people who dropped out, nobody has tested it against a dummy treatment, and it has not been shown to help when depression sits alongside a serious physical illness.

Safety

Well tolerated in trials. The COBRA trial reported no trial-related adverse events across 440 participants; the 15 depression-related serious adverse events recorded (3 in the behavioural activation arm, 12 in the CBT arm) were overdoses and self-harm judged related to the underlying depression rather than to treatment. Acceptability deserves a caveat: the Cochrane review found behavioural activation might have lower acceptability by dropout rate than treatment as usual in the short term, though the data did not confirm a difference and the estimate was imprecise (RR 1.64, 95% CI 0.81 to 3.31; 14 RCTs, 2,518 participants). Harms reporting remains a genuine gap: the Cochrane review in adults with non-communicable diseases found that neither included trial reported adverse-effect data at all. The main practical risk is scheduling activity too ambitiously early on, which can reinforce a sense of failure; graded, achievable steps are how the method is delivered in trials.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications. COBRA excluded people receiving other psychological therapy, people with alcohol or drug dependence, people who were acutely suicidal or had attempted suicide within the previous 2 months, people with cognitive impairment, and people with bipolar disorder, psychosis, or psychotic symptoms; the Cochrane review excluded inpatient settings and people selected for a physical comorbidity. The results do not extend to those groups. In bipolar disorder, activity scheduling interacts with sleep and routine in ways not studied here and should only be undertaken alongside mood-stabilising treatment and clinical supervision, since increased activation can be a feature of emerging hypomania.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Many COBRA participants were simultaneously taking antidepressants, and randomisation was stratified by antidepressant use; behavioural activation was non-inferior to CBT in that mixed context. Any change to antidepressant medication should be made with the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

Behavioural activation reduces CBT to its behavioural core - re-engaging with reinforcing activity. A meta-analysis of 26 randomised trials (1,524 subjects) found it superior to control conditions (SMD -0.74, 95% CI -0.91 to -0.56, k=25, N=1,088) and to antidepressant medication (SMD -0.42, 95% CI -0.83 to 0.00, k=4, N=283), with no indication of publication bias, though the authors judged study quality low in the majority of trials and follow-up periods short. The most rigorous synthesis is less flattering: a Cochrane review of 53 trials (5,495 participants) found moderate-certainty evidence of greater short-term efficacy than treatment as usual (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10-1.78; 7 RCTs, 1,533 participants) but stated that this difference was no longer evident in worst-case or intention-to-treat sensitivity analyses; the waiting-list comparison was non-significant and came from a single 26-participant trial (RR 2.14, 95% CI 0.90-5.09); and no efficacy evidence at all was available versus placebo or no treatment. A separate bias-adjustment analysis of psychotherapy for adult depression, where only 23% of trials were at low risk of bias and the adjusted pooled effect fell to g=0.31, named behavioural activation as one of the therapies with too few low-risk trials to establish effectiveness. The strongest single trial is COBRA, a 440-participant randomised non-inferiority trial in UK primary care: at 12 months PHQ-9 scores were effectively identical (CBT 8.4, SD 7.5; behavioural activation 8.4, SD 7.0; mean difference 0.1 points, 95% CI -1.3 to 1.5, p=0.89), well inside the pre-set 1.9-point non-inferiority margin, with behavioural activation delivered by junior mental health workers lacking professional psychological therapy training; the Cochrane pooling likewise found no difference versus CBT (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92-1.07). A component network meta-analysis of 76 internet-CBT trials, including individual participant data from 11,704 people, independently identified behavioural activation as beneficial (incremental mean difference -1.83 PHQ-9 points, 95% CrI -2.90 to -0.80) while flagging relaxation as potentially harmful (+1.20, 95% CrI 0.17 to 2.27). Evidence thins sharply outside standard populations: a Cochrane review of behavioural activation for depression in adults with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, or chronic respiratory conditions found only 2 eligible trials (181 participants), no evidence of a difference in depression symptoms (MD -1.15, 95% CI -2.71 to 0.41, low-certainty), and concluded the evidence was insufficient to draw conclusions. A pilot trial in socially isolated adults aged 65 and over found no benefit at 12 months (adjusted PHQ-9 difference -0.70, 95% CI -2.61 to 1.20), while its accompanying living meta-analysis of 12 trials showed short-term benefit (SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.51 to -0.11) attenuating to a confidence interval crossing zero in the longer term (SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.47 to 0.07).

Doses studied: In the COBRA trial, the per-protocol analysis required at least eight treatment sessions, delivered by junior mental health workers with no professional psychological therapy training; the trial's stated purpose was to test a simpler, less intensively trained delivery model against full CBT. Across the 53 trials in the Cochrane review, session number, format, and setting varied widely, and the review excluded inpatient settings and participants selected for a physical comorbidity.

Why this grade: Downgraded from strong on adversarial review after two omitted sources were located. The definitive Cochrane review (53 trials, 5,495 participants) found only moderate-certainty evidence of short-term benefit over treatment as usual (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10-1.78), and that difference was no longer evident under worst-case or intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis; the waiting-list comparison was non-significant and rested on one 26-person trial; and no efficacy evidence exists at all against placebo or no treatment. Separately, a bias-adjustment analysis of psychotherapies for adult depression names behavioural activation specifically as a therapy for which too few low-risk-of-bias trials exist to establish effectiveness. Set against that, the direction of benefit is consistent across a 26-trial meta-analysis, a 440-participant non-inferiority trial showing equivalence to full CBT at 12 months when delivered by staff without professional psychotherapy training, and an individual-participant component network meta-analysis of 11,704 people. Consistent direction, fragile foundations, plus null findings in comorbid physical illness and in socially isolated older adults: moderate, not strong.

Sources (7)
  1. Behavioural activation therapy for depression in adults.
    Meta-analysisUphoff, Ekers, Robertson, et al. (Cochrane), 2020 · PMID 32628293 · mixed

    Fifty-three randomised trials with 5,495 participants. Moderate-certainty evidence that behavioural activation had greater short-term efficacy than treatment as usual (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.78; 7 RCTs, 1,533 participants), but the authors state this difference was no longer evident in sensitivity analyses using a worst-case or intention-to-treat scenario. Versus waiting list the estimate was non-significant and rested on one 26-participant trial (RR 2.14, 95% CI 0.90 to 5.09; low certainty), and no efficacy evidence at all was available versus placebo or no treatment. No evidence of a difference versus CBT (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.07; 5 RCTs, 601 participants; moderate certainty). Acceptability by dropout might be lower than treatment as usual, though the data did not confirm a difference (RR 1.64, 95% CI 0.81 to 3.31; 14 RCTs, 2,518 participants). Authors state confidence in all findings is limited by the certainty of the evidence.

  2. Cost and Outcome of Behavioural Activation versus Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Depression (COBRA): a randomised, controlled, non-inferiority trial.
    Randomised trialRichards, Ekers, McMillan, et al., 2016 · PMID 27461440 · positive

    440 adults with DSM-IV major depressive disorder randomised 1:1. At 12 months behavioural activation was non-inferior to CBT: modified ITT mean PHQ-9 8.4 (SD 7.0) vs 8.4 (SD 7.5), mean difference 0.1 points (95% CI -1.3 to 1.5, p=0.89); per protocol 7.8 vs 7.9, difference 0.0 (95% CI -1.5 to 1.6, p=0.99). Non-inferiority margin was 1.9 PHQ-9 points. Behavioural activation was delivered by junior mental health workers without professional psychological therapy training; authors conclude effective psychological therapy can be delivered without costly, highly trained professionals.

  3. Behavioural activation for depression; an update of meta-analysis of effectiveness and sub group analysis.
    Meta-analysisEkers, Webster, Van Straten, Cuijpers, Richards & Gilbody, 2014 · PMID 24936656 · positive

    Across 26 randomised trials including 1,524 subjects, behavioural activation was superior to controls (SMD -0.74, 95% CI -0.91 to -0.56, k=25, N=1,088) and to antidepressant medication (SMD -0.42, 95% CI -0.83 to 0.00, k=4, N=283). No indication of publication bias, and subgroup analysis showed limited association between moderators and effect size. Authors state study quality was low in the majority of studies and follow-up periods short.

  4. Was Eysenck right after all? A reassessment of the effects of psychotherapy for adult depression.
    Journal articleCuijpers, Karyotaki, Reijnders & Ebert, 2018 · PMID 29486804 · negative

    Unadjusted effect of psychotherapies versus control for adult depression was g=0.70, with only 23% of studies considered at low risk of bias; after adjusting for risk of bias, publication bias, and exclusion of waiting-list controls the effect across all therapy types dropped to g=0.31. The authors state that for several psychotherapies, explicitly including behavioural activation, insufficient evidence is available that they are effective because too few low-risk studies were available.

  5. Dismantling, optimising, and personalising internet cognitive behavioural therapy for depression: a systematic review and component network meta-analysis using individual participant data.
    Network meta-analysisFurukawa, Suganuma, Ostinelli, et al., 2021 · PMID 33957075 · positive

    Component network meta-analysis of 76 RCTs (48 contributing individual participant data on 11,704 participants; 28 aggregate on 6,474) found suggestive evidence that behavioural activation is beneficial as a component (incremental mean difference -1.83 PHQ-9 points, 95% CrI -2.90 to -0.80), while relaxation may be harmful (+1.20, 95% CrI 0.17 to 2.27). Combining human and automated encouragement reduced dropout (incremental OR 0.32, 95% CrI 0.13-0.93).

  6. Behavioural activation therapy for depression in adults with non-communicable diseases.
    Meta-analysisUphoff, Pires, Barbui, et al. (Cochrane), 2020 · PMID 32841367 · null

    Only 2 randomised trials (181 participants) met inclusion criteria for behavioural activation in adults with diagnosed depression plus cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, or chronic respiratory conditions. No evidence of a difference in depression symptoms (MD -1.15, 95% CI -2.71 to 0.41, low-certainty). Remission estimates were imprecise short term (RR 1.53, 95% CI 0.98-2.38, low certainty), reached significance medium term (RR 1.76, 95% CI 1.01-3.08, moderate certainty), and were reduced long term (RR 1.42, 95% CI 0.91-2.23, moderate certainty). Neither study reported data on adverse effects. Authors concluded the evidence was insufficient to draw conclusions.

  7. Can we mitigate the psychological impacts of social isolation using behavioural activation? Long-term results of the UK BASIL urgent public health COVID-19 pilot randomised controlled trial and living systematic review.
    Meta-analysisLittlewood, McMillan, Chew Graham, et al., 2022 · PMID 36223980 · mixed

    Pilot RCT of 96 adults aged 65+ with long-term conditions (47 behavioural activation, 49 care as usual) found no benefit at 12 months: adjusted mean PHQ-9 difference -0.70 (95% CI -2.61 to 1.20) and loneliness -0.39 (95% CI -1.43 to 0.65). The accompanying living systematic review of 12 trials found short-term reductions in depression (SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.51 to -0.11) but long-term evidence was weaker, with the depression confidence interval crossing zero (SMD -0.20, 95% CI -0.47 to 0.07).
