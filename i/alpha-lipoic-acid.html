Alpha-lipoic acid

Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Alpha-lipoic acid is an antioxidant your body makes in tiny amounts and that is sold in much larger doses. Its strongest evidence is not for blood sugar at all but for the burning and tingling of diabetic nerve pain, where people taking 600 mg a day reported meaningfully less pain than people taking a placebo over five weeks. One thing first, though. If burning, tingling or numbness in your feet is new or getting worse, that needs a clinician before it needs a supplement. Some causes are treatable and easy to miss - low vitamin B12 after years on metformin is a common one - and numbness is how serious foot injuries go unnoticed until they are ulcers. Get your feet examined and read the foot warning on this page. Treating the pain without finding out what is causing it is the wrong order. For blood sugar itself the picture is weaker: many small, short studies pooled together suggest a modest improvement, but the largest two-year trial saw no change in HbA1c. There is also a rare but serious risk of sudden low blood sugar from an immune reaction, so this is not a supplement to take casually.

Safety

Alpha-lipoic acid is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and this matters more here than for most supplements: the documented insulin autoimmune syndrome risk makes glucose less predictable, not more. Nothing on this page is a reason to stop, reduce or delay a prescribed drug; any change belongs to the prescriber. Tolerability is dose-dependent: SYDNEY 2 reported rising nausea, vomiting and vertigo from 600 to 1,800 mg/day (PMID 17065669), and NATHAN 1 recorded more serious adverse events on ALA (38.1%) than placebo (28.0%) over 4 years despite similar discontinuation rates (PMID 21775755). A 6-month dose-ranging study at 300-1,200 mg/day described treatment as well tolerated with minor side effects (PMID 22374556).

Who should be careful: Anyone who develops unexplained low blood sugar should stop ALA and be assessed for insulin autoimmune syndrome (Hirata disease). Say the words to the clinician - 'I take alpha-lipoic acid; could this be insulin autoimmune syndrome?' - and ask for insulin, C-peptide and insulin autoantibodies to be measured on a sample taken while the blood sugar is low, together with a sulfonylurea screen: insulin that is high out of proportion to C-peptide plus positive insulin autoantibodies is what separates this from an insulin-producing tumour, the drug screen is what rules out a sulfonylurea effect, and the whole picture is easily missed (PMID 42143009, PMID 40317087). Sulfhydryl-containing medications and alpha-lipoic acid are recognized triggers, HLA-DR4-related alleles (particularly DRB1*0406) increase susceptibility, and cases needing months of glucocorticoids have been reported (PMID 42143009, PMID 40488315, PMID 39163080). Not studied for safety in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Neuropathy trials included patients with type 1 diabetes, but there is no glycaemic evidence in type 1, and the autoimmune hypoglycaemia risk applies to anyone.

Interactions: Additive glucose lowering with insulin or sulfonylureas is biologically expected given the clamp data (PMID 10468203) and the autoimmune hypoglycaemia reports, so people on those drugs need glucose monitoring. No specific drug-metabolism interactions were identified in this session's search. Products often combine ALA with other agents (biotin, carnosine, zinc, B vitamins), so results from combination trials (for example PMID 27801825, ALA plus carnosine, zinc and B vitamins) cannot be attributed to ALA alone.

What the evidence says

Glycaemic evidence comes almost entirely from pooling small, short trials in mixed 'metabolic disease' populations rather than from any large dedicated trial in type 2 diabetes. Mohammadi 2025 (63 RCTs) reported HbA1c -0.40% (95% CI -0.66 to -0.14), fasting glucose -5.28 mg/dL and HOMA-IR -0.74; Akbari 2018 (24 RCTs) reported standardized effects of -0.54 for fasting glucose and -1.22 for HbA1c, an implausibly large pooled figure that signals heterogeneity rather than a real percentage-point change; and a 4-week glucose-clamp study in 74 people with type 2 diabetes found insulin-stimulated glucose disposal +27% versus placebo at 600-1,800 mg/day with no dose effect (PMID 10468203). Against this, a 2-year placebo-controlled trial of 600 mg/day in 467 patients with diabetes recorded HbA1c holding constant near 8.1-8.2% in both arms and no reduction in clinically significant macular oedema (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.54-1.53), and a 2026 meta-analysis of neuropathy trials found no significant change in HbA1c (PMID 41619689). The neuropathy evidence is a separate question and stronger: in SYDNEY 2 (n=181, 5 weeks) 600 mg/day cut the Total Symptom Score by 4.9 points (51%) versus 2.9 (32%) on placebo with 62% vs 26% responders; a 2023 meta-analysis of 10 oral RCTs (n=1,242) confirmed dose-related symptom relief but no improvement in nerve conduction (PMID 37630823); and the 4-year NATHAN 1 trial (n=460) missed its primary composite endpoint (P=0.105) while improving secondary impairment scores, with serious adverse events in 38.1% vs 28.0%. There are no trials in prediabetes and none measuring progression to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney outcomes or death, so every glycaemic result here is a surrogate. Population summary: type 2 diabetes glycaemia, mixed surrogate data; diabetic neuropathy, moderate symptomatic benefit; prediabetes and healthy people, untested.

Doses studied: Oral 300-1,800 mg/day (600 mg/day most common and judged the best risk-benefit dose in SYDNEY 2), for 4 weeks to 4 years; neuropathy trials also used 600 mg/day intravenously for about 3 weeks. Glycaemic trials mostly ran 4 weeks to 6 months.

Why this grade: For the glycaemic question that defines this condition, the supportive data are pooled small, short, heterogeneous trials in mixed 'metabolic disease' populations, with one meta-analysis reporting an implausibly large standardized HbA1c effect (SMD -1.22) that signals heterogeneity rather than a real percentage-point change. Against that, a 467-patient two-year placebo-controlled trial recorded HbA1c holding flat near 8.1-8.2% in both arms alongside a negative primary outcome, and a 2026 meta-analysis of neuropathy trials found no significant HbA1c change. There are no prediabetes trials and no trial of progression to diabetes or any clinical outcome. Torn between moderate and early, the lower grade applies. The symptomatic diabetic-neuropathy evidence would on its own rate moderate, but it answers a different question and is described separately.

Sources (11)
  1. Effects of alpha-lipoic acid supplementation on cardiometabolic risk factors: A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis.
    Mohammadi et al., 2025 · PMID 41077538 · positive

    63 RCTs in mixed cardiometabolic populations: ALA lowered HbA1c by -0.40% (95% CI -0.66 to -0.14), fasting blood glucose by -5.28 mg/dL (-7.21 to -3.35), HOMA-IR by -0.74 (-1.17 to -0.31), fasting insulin by -1.70 mU/mL and body weight by -0.64 kg; no significant effect on LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol or blood pressure.

  2. The effects of alpha-lipoic acid supplementation on glucose control and lipid profiles among patients with metabolic diseases: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Akbari et al., 2018 · PMID 29990473 · positive

    24 RCTs in patients with metabolic diseases: standardized mean differences of -0.54 (95% CI -0.89 to -0.19) for fasting glucose, -1.22 (-2.01 to -0.44) for HbA1c and -0.76 for HOMA-IR; these are standardized effects pooled from heterogeneous small trials, not HbA1c percentage points, and the size of the HbA1c figure reflects heterogeneity rather than a plausible real-world change.

  3. Alpha-lipoic acid for the prevention of diabetic macular edema.
    Haritoglou et al., 2011 · PMID 21811051 · null

    467 patients with diabetes (about 36% insulin-dependent) randomized to 600 mg/day oral ALA or placebo for 2 years: mean HbA1c of 8.1-8.2% remained constant over time in both groups; clinically significant macular oedema occurred in 26/235 vs 30/232 (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.54-1.53, P=0.71) and the authors reported the study as providing a negative result on its primary endpoint.

  4. Effectiveness of alpha lipoic acid supplementation on biochemical, clinical, and inflammatory parameters in patients with diabetic polyneuropathy: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Vergara Salinas et al., 2026 · PMID 41619689 · mixed

    15 studies (12 pooled) in diabetic polyneuropathy: 19 of 23 outcomes favoured ALA, with symptom benefit concentrated at 600 mg/day (Total Symptom Score paresthesia SMD -1.04, 95% CI -1.24 to -0.84; numbness SMD -0.23, -0.44 to -0.01), but four outcomes showed no significant change, including HbA1c, sural sensory nerve action potential and peroneal motor nerve conduction velocity; the authors call the impact on long-term glycaemic control inconclusive.

  5. Oral treatment with alpha-lipoic acid improves symptomatic diabetic polyneuropathy: the SYDNEY 2 trial.
    Ziegler et al., 2006 · PMID 17065669 · positive

    181 diabetic patients with symptomatic polyneuropathy randomized to oral ALA 600, 1,200 or 1,800 mg/day or placebo for 5 weeks: Total Symptom Score fell 4.9 points (51%) with 600 mg vs 2.9 points (32%) with placebo (all ALA arms P<0.05 vs placebo); responder rates (>=50% reduction) 62%, 50%, 56% vs 26%; dose-dependent increase in nausea, vomiting and vertigo; 600 mg judged the optimum risk-to-benefit dose.

  6. Efficacy and safety of antioxidant treatment with alpha-lipoic acid over 4 years in diabetic polyneuropathy: the NATHAN 1 trial.
    Ziegler et al., 2011 · PMID 21775755 · mixed

    460 patients with mild-to-moderate diabetic polyneuropathy randomized to 600 mg/day oral ALA or placebo for 4 years: the primary composite endpoint (NIS-LL plus seven neurophysiologic tests) was not significantly different (P=0.105); secondary NIS (P=0.028) and NIS-LL (P=0.05) favoured ALA; serious adverse events occurred in 38.1% on ALA vs 28.0% on placebo, with similar tolerability and discontinuations.

  7. Autoimmune hypoglycemia due to alpha-lipoic acid: Report of two cases.
    Subramaniam & Tom, 2025 · PMID 40488315 · negative

    Two Indian patients developed insulin autoimmune syndrome (spontaneous hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia from insulin autoantibodies) after taking ALA; one had rapid-onset severe hypoglycaemia requiring oral steroids tapered over several months, the other remitted with dietary modification alone; the authors describe ALA as increasingly recognized as a potential trigger.

  8. Insulin autoimmune syndrome (Hirata Disease): An updated review of epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and management.
    Nahar et al., 2026 · PMID 42143009 · negative

    Review of insulin autoimmune syndrome: high-capacity, low-affinity insulin autoantibodies bind endogenous insulin after meals and release it unpredictably, producing postprandial hyperglycaemia followed by delayed hypoglycaemia that often mimics insulinoma or factitious hypoglycaemia; sulfhydryl-containing medications and alpha-lipoic acid are named as common triggers and HLA-DR4-related alleles, particularly DRB1*0406, increase susceptibility; diagnosis is missed because of immunoassay interference, disproportionately elevated insulin and negative insulinoma imaging, and the authors advise early consideration in patients with discordant insulin-C-peptide results or recent exposure to high-risk agents; most cases improve on withdrawal of the trigger, with persistent hypoglycaemia sometimes needing glucocorticoids, acarbose or diazoxide.

  9. Insulin autoimmune syndrome in a patient with vitiligo: a case report and review of literature.
    Heydari et al., 2025 · PMID 40317087 · negative

    Worked example of the diagnostic sequence this entry tells readers to ask for: a 47-year-old woman with recurrent neuroglycopenic episodes was evaluated during hypoglycaemia (glucose 35 mg/dL) and found to have insulin >2000 microIU/mL with C-peptide 23 ng/mL, a negative sulfonylurea screen and imaging excluding insulinoma, with high insulin autoantibodies establishing insulin autoimmune syndrome; treated with small, frequent, low-carbohydrate meals. Confirms that the sulfonylurea screen, not the insulin/C-peptide pair, is what excludes a sulfonylurea effect.

  10. Long-term Metformin Use and Vitamin B12 Deficiency in the Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study.
    Aroda et al., 2016 · PMID 26900641 · negative

    Context citation, not ALA evidence. DPP/DPPOS randomized secondary analysis: low B12 (<=203 pg/mL) occurred in 4.3% of the metformin group vs 2.3% of placebo at 5 years (P=.02) but not significantly at 13 years (7.4 vs 5.4%, P=.12); combined low and borderline-low B12 (<=298 pg/mL) was more common on metformin at 5 years (19.1 vs 9.5%, P<.01) and at 13 years (20.3 vs 15.6%, P=.02); each year of metformin use raised the odds of B12 deficiency (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06-1.20), and neuropathy prevalence was higher in metformin users with low B12. The authors advise considering routine B12 testing in metformin-treated patients. Supports routing new or worsening foot symptoms to a clinician rather than to a supplement.

  11. Preventing foot ulcers in patients with diabetes.
    Singh et al., 2005 · PMID 15644549 · negative

    Context citation, not ALA evidence. Systematic review: foot-ulcer prevalence in diabetes is 4-10%, annual incidence 1.0-4.1% and lifetime incidence may reach 25%; these ulcers frequently become infected and are 'the usual first step to lower extremity amputation'. Prevention begins with screening for loss of protective sensation (brief history plus Semmes-Weinstein monofilament in primary care), and patient education about foot care plus periodic foot examinations are effective preventive interventions. Substantiates telling a reader with numbness to get feet examined rather than treat the symptom.
