Biofeedback and relaxation training

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

Learning to relax on purpose — with a device that shows you your body's signals, or through structured relaxation training — has been studied for migraine for decades, and people who learn it usually do better than people put on a waiting list. What is less clear is whether it beats other reasonable treatments, or simply beats doing nothing and expecting nothing. Recent, stricter reviews are considerably more cautious than the older ones. It is very low risk, and the skills tend to stick once learned.

Safety

Biofeedback and relaxation training are among the lowest-risk interventions in this field; the migraine trials reviewed reported no serious adverse events, and harms are more often under-reported than actually observed — the 2025 evidence review flagged incomplete data collection as a general limitation of this literature, so absence of reported harm is not the same as demonstrated absence of harm. The practical burdens are real: these take 7-12 supervised sessions plus daily home practice, adherence is a genuine problem (only 62% completed an online program in one trial), and app-based versions had technology barriers reported by roughly a third of users. No physical risk, no drug exposure, and skills learned appear to persist at 14-17 month follow-up.

Who should be careful: No absolute medical contraindications identified in the trial literature. As with any self-management approach, a new, sudden, or changing headache pattern needs medical evaluation before being managed behaviourally. People with significant untreated psychiatric illness should be assessed by a clinician rather than relying on self-directed relaxation programs; the trials studied structured, taught programs, not unsupervised app use.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Studied as an addition to, not a replacement for, usual medical care; in the trials that compared it head-to-head, it did not outperform pharmacotherapy or CBT.

What the evidence says

All evidence is PREVENTIVE; biofeedback and relaxation have not been tested as acute abortive treatments. A meta-analysis of 55 studies (RCTs plus uncontrolled pre-post trials) found a medium effect for biofeedback in migraine (d=0.58, 95% CI 0.52-0.64), stable over an average 17-month follow-up, with headache frequency and perceived self-efficacy showing the strongest improvement and home training augmenting the effect; a companion review of 94 studies rated biofeedback 'efficacious' (level 4) for migraine and 'efficacious and specific' (level 5) for tension-type headache, where EMG feedback showed effects over placebo and relaxation. The more recent picture is weaker. A 2025 meta-analysis (9 studies, 558 participants versus waiting list; 3 studies, 278 participants versus active treatment) found biofeedback significantly reduced headache frequency and severity versus waiting-list control but showed NO significant difference versus active treatments such as pharmacotherapy or CBT. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis conducted with an expert panel (50 adult trials, 6024 adults) concluded that relaxation training may reduce migraine/headache attack frequency at LOW strength of evidence, and that for biofeedback specifically the evidence was INSUFFICIENT to permit conclusions; most trials were at high risk of bias, primarily from measurement bias and incomplete data. Two randomized trials were null: an 8-week app-based heart-rate-variability biofeedback trial (n=52) showed no difference in migraine-specific quality of life versus waiting list (estimate 0.3, 95% CI -3.1 to 3.6), and an online relaxation-based behavioural training trial (n=368) found a 20-25% drop in attack frequency in BOTH arms with no between-group difference (ES=0.02, p=.71), though self-efficacy improved substantially (ES=0.86, p<.001).

Doses studied: Typically 7-12 supervised weekly sessions (often 60-90 minutes) plus daily home practice; meta-analysis found regimens including home training more effective than those without. Digital versions studied shorter, self-directed sessions (app-based heart-rate-variability biofeedback averaged about 6-7 minutes per session over roughly 36 days).

Why this grade: A large, long-standing literature with meta-analyses reporting a medium effect (d=0.58) that persists at follow-up, and biofeedback significantly outperforms waiting-list controls. But the effect collapses against fair comparators: a 2025 meta-analysis found NO significant difference in headache frequency versus active treatments (pharmacotherapy or CBT); a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 50 adult trials rated relaxation training only LOW strength of evidence and judged the evidence for biofeedback specifically INSUFFICIENT to permit conclusions; and both an app-based heart-rate-variability biofeedback RCT and an online relaxation-based behavioural training RCT were null against controls. The older pooled effect combined randomized trials with uncontrolled pre-post designs that did not control for expectancy. There is a genuine case for a lower grade on the strength of the 'insufficient' rating; it is held at moderate only because the trial base is large and decades old rather than preliminary, and because benefit over no treatment is reasonably established. Preventive only; no acute evidence.

Sources (6)
  1. Efficacy of biofeedback for migraine: a meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisNestoriuc & Martin, 2006 (Pain) · PMID 17084028 · positive

    55 studies, including randomized controlled trials as well as uncontrolled pre-post trials, integrated from 86 identified. Medium effect size across all biofeedback interventions (d=0.58, 95% CI 0.52-0.64), stable over an average 17-month follow-up, and greater than control conditions. Attack frequency and perceived self-efficacy improved most. Blood-volume-pulse feedback yielded higher effect sizes than skin temperature and EMG feedback; regimens with home training outperformed those without.

  2. Biofeedback treatment for headache disorders: a comprehensive efficacy review.
    Meta-analysisNestoriuc et al., 2008 · PMID 18726688 · positive

    94 of 150 screened outcome studies included, spanning RCTs and uncontrolled quasi-experimental designs. Medium-to-large mean effect sizes for biofeedback in adult migraine and tension-type headache, stable over an average 14-month follow-up in both completer and intention-to-treat analyses. Headache frequency improved most. Biofeedback beat waiting-list and headache-monitoring controls in all cases; for tension-type headache, EMG feedback additionally beat placebo and relaxation. Rated efficacious (level 4) for migraine.

  3. Efficacy of biofeedback for migraine: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisPaudel & Sah, 2025 · PMID 40064357 · mixed

    Nine studies (558 participants) compared biofeedback with waiting-list control and three studies (278 participants) with active treatment. Biofeedback significantly reduced headache frequency and severity versus waiting list, but showed NO significant difference in headache frequency when compared with active treatments such as pharmacotherapy or cognitive behavioral therapy.

  4. Behavioral interventions for migraine prevention: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisTreadwell et al., 2025 (Headache) · PMID 39968795 · mixed

    50 adult trials, 6024 adults. Relaxation training may reduce migraine/headache attack frequency (strength of evidence: LOW), as may CBT and mindfulness-based therapies. For biofeedback specifically, along with acceptance and commitment therapy and hypnotherapy, evidence was INSUFFICIENT to permit conclusions. Most trials were at high risk of bias, primarily from possible measurement bias and incomplete data, and consisted of underpowered multicomponent interventions compared against varied control groups.

  5. Heartrate variability biofeedback for migraine using a smartphone application and sensor: A randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialMinen et al., 2021 · PMID 33516964 · null

    52 participants (26 per arm) randomized to 8 weeks of app-based heart-rate-variability biofeedback or waiting list. No significant difference in the primary clinical outcome, Migraine-Specific Quality of Life (estimate 0.3, 95% CI -3.1 to 3.6). High users of the app did report a reduction at day 30 (-12.3 points, p=0.010) while low users did not (p=0.765). 9 of 29 reported technology barriers; 43% said they would recommend it to others.

  6. Short-term effectiveness of an online behavioral training in migraine self-management: a randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialKleiboer et al., 2014 · PMID 25133856 · null

    368 participants randomized to eight online lessons of behavioural training built on physical relaxation and cognitive-behavioural regulation (n=195) or waiting list (n=173). Attack frequency fell 20-25% in BOTH conditions with no between-group difference (ES=0.02, p=.71). Only 62% (120/195) completed the training. Self-efficacy (ES=0.86, p<.001) and internal locus of control did improve significantly with training.
