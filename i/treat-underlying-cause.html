Treating the underlying cause (thyroid, iron, sleep apnea)

Moderate evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

Tiredness that lasts is a symptom, not a diagnosis, so the first useful step is finding out what is driving it. When a real cause turns up (low iron stores, an underactive thyroid, breathing that stops and starts at night), treating that cause is where the evidence for genuine improvement actually sits, though even there the effects are modest and not consistent across studies. It is also honest to say that about half the time no clear cause is found, and that borderline test results are usually not the answer people are hoping for.

Safety

Investigation itself is low-risk; the treatments that follow are not interchangeable and each needs a confirmed indication. Levothyroxine requires prescribing and periodic thyrotropin monitoring: the TRUST trial reported no significant excess of prespecified serious adverse events, but trials in this population monitored specifically for atrial fibrillation, heart failure and fracture, and the two largest syntheses found no symptom benefit to weigh against those risks in subclinical disease. Oral iron commonly causes gastrointestinal side effects that many people find intolerable, and every trial showing a fatigue benefit enrolled only people with documented low iron stores. For intravenous iron, a Cochrane review of 11 trials in 1,074 non-anaemic iron-deficient adults recorded zero severe adverse events, but a 2022 reanalysis across 21 trials and 3,514 participants found a higher risk of mild adverse events on iron than placebo (RR 1.77, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.83). CPAP is physically safe; adherence is the limiting factor, with 22% of the CPAP group in a 391-patient trial stopping treatment and median use of 2 hours 39 minutes per night.

Who should be careful: Do not self-treat. Thyroid hormone should not be started without confirmed thyroid disease, and it has not been shown to help tiredness or thyroid-related symptoms in subclinical hypothyroidism. Iron supplements should not be taken without measured iron studies, and should be avoided by anyone with haemochromatosis or iron overload. Fatigue accompanied by unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, new breathlessness or bleeding, and new fatigue in men aged 70 and over, warrants prompt medical assessment rather than self-management; the same primary-care data did not support investigating women for cancer on the basis of fatigue alone.

Interactions: Thyroid hormone, iron and CPAP should all be prescribed and reviewed by a clinician rather than layered on top of self-selected supplements. Ask a prescriber or pharmacist about timing and interactions before combining any supplement with prescribed thyroid or iron treatment, and tell them everything you are already taking, since several over-the-counter products affect absorption of both.

What the evidence says

Fatigue is a presenting symptom with a wide differential: in an English primary-care cohort of 304,914 people presenting with new-onset fatigue versus 423,671 without, 127 of 237 diseases were more common in men and 151 in women who presented with fatigue, with cancer carrying an absolute excess risk of 3.65% by age 80 in men (7.01% vs 3.36%) but only the thirteenth highest excess risk in women. Yield from investigation is nonetheless modest: in a prospective cohort of 571 primary-care patients followed 1 year, 46.9% received any diagnosis possibly associated with the fatigue and only 8.2% had clear somatic pathology. Where a genuine deficiency is confirmed there is benefit, but it is partial: a systematic review in iron-deficient non-anaemic adults found reduced self-reported fatigue (SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.23, I-squared 0%, 4 trials, 714 participants) with no improvement in maximal oxygen consumption (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.37, 9 trials), and a 12-week trial in 198 non-anaemic menstruating women with ferritin below 50 micrograms/L found fatigue scores fell 47.7% on iron versus 28.8% on placebo (difference -18.9%, 95% CI -34.5 to -3.2, p=0.02) with no effect on quality of life, depression or anxiety; a Cochrane review of intravenous iron (11 trials, 1,074 adults) judged the evidence insufficient to show benefit, while a 2022 reanalysis expanding to 21 trials and 3,514 participants did find improved peak oxygen consumption (MD 1.77 mL/kg/min, 95% CI 0.57 to 2.97) and fatigue, on low-quality evidence. In obstructive sleep apnea, 3 weeks of therapeutic versus placebo CPAP in 59 adults reduced fatigue and increased vigor (p<0.05), with the largest effect in those most fatigued at baseline, while in 391 patients with minimally symptomatic apnea CPAP improved Epworth sleepiness by 2.0 points (95% CI -2.6 to -1.4) but not calculated vascular risk, and 22% stopped treatment. The clearest negative is subclinical hypothyroidism: in 737 adults aged 65 and over, levothyroxine produced no difference in Tiredness score at 1 year (0.4, 95% CI -2.1 to 2.9) against a 9-point minimum clinically important difference, and a meta-analysis of 21 trials in 2,192 adults found no benefit on thyroid-related symptoms (SMD 0.01, 95% CI -0.12 to 0.14) or general quality of life (SMD -0.11, 95% CI -0.25 to 0.03). These figures come from specific populations and should not be pooled with cancer-related, post-viral or ME/CFS fatigue literatures.

Doses studied: Not a dose. Regimens in the cited trials: levothyroxine 50 micrograms daily (25 micrograms if under 50 kg or with coronary heart disease), titrated to thyrotropin, for 1 year; oral ferrous sulfate providing 80 mg elemental iron daily for 12 weeks; therapeutic CPAP nightly for 3 weeks in the fatigue trial, and 6 months of auto-adjusting CPAP in the larger minimally symptomatic trial.

Why this grade: Finding and treating a real cause is the standard clinical approach and the only route to persistent fatigue with any randomised support at all, but the evidence for fatigue as an outcome is modest and internally mixed rather than consistent, so this is graded moderate rather than strong. On the positive side, iron repletion in confirmed iron deficiency reduced self-reported fatigue in a pooled analysis of 4 trials and 714 participants, and therapeutic CPAP beat placebo CPAP on fatigue in a 59-person double-blind trial. Against that, the pooled iron effect did not carry over to objective physical capacity, an intravenous-iron Cochrane review judged the quality-of-life evidence insufficient, and where the abnormality is borderline the evidence is frankly null: two independent syntheses found levothyroxine did nothing for tiredness or thyroid-related symptoms in subclinical hypothyroidism. Roughly half of people presenting to primary care with fatigue receive no diagnosis that explains it. Graded for the strategy of looking for a cause, not for any single test or treatment.

Sources (10)
  1. Underlying disease risk among patients with fatigue: a population-based cohort study in primary care
    White et al., 2024 · PMID 39658074 · positive

    Among 304,914 people presenting to English primary care with new-onset fatigue versus 423,671 without, 127 of 237 diseases were more common in men and 151 in women presenting with fatigue; the strongest associations were depression, respiratory tract infections, insomnia/sleep disturbance and hypo/hyperthyroidism (women only). By age 80 cancer carried an absolute excess risk of 3.65% in men (7.01%, 95% CI 6.54 to 7.51, vs 3.36%, 95% CI 3.08 to 3.67), but in women cancer had only the thirteenth highest excess risk; the authors support prioritising cancer investigation in older men (aged 70 and over) with fatigue but not in women at any age based solely on fatigue.

  2. Diagnoses during follow-up of patients presenting with fatigue in primary care
    Nijrolder et al., 2009 · PMID 19858240 · mixed

    Of 571 adults presenting with a new episode of fatigue and followed for 1 year, 268 (46.9%) received at least one diagnosis possibly associated with the fatigue, mostly symptom diagnoses (musculoskeletal 19.4%, psychological 16.5%); clear somatic pathology was found in only 47 patients (8.2%) and half received no diagnosis explaining the fatigue.

  3. Thyroid Hormone Therapy for Older Adults with Subclinical Hypothyroidism (TRUST)
    Stott et al., 2017 · PMID 28402245 · null

    In 737 adults aged 65 and over with persisting subclinical hypothyroidism, levothyroxine lowered thyrotropin from 6.40 to 3.63 mIU/L but produced no difference at 1 year in Tiredness score (between-group difference 0.4, 95% CI -2.1 to 2.9) or Hypothyroid Symptoms score (0.0, 95% CI -2.0 to 2.1), against a 9-point minimum clinically important difference, and no beneficial effects on secondary outcomes.

  4. Association of Thyroid Hormone Therapy With Quality of Life and Thyroid-Related Symptoms in Patients With Subclinical Hypothyroidism: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
    Feller et al., 2018 · PMID 30285179 · null

    Across 21 randomised trials with 2,192 adults randomised, thyroid hormone therapy normalised thyrotropin but was not associated with benefit in general quality of life (n=796; SMD -0.11, 95% CI -0.25 to 0.03) or thyroid-related symptoms (n=858; SMD 0.01, 95% CI -0.12 to 0.14, I2=0.0%); risk of bias was low and GRADE quality moderate to high, and the authors concluded the findings do not support routine thyroid hormone therapy in subclinical hypothyroidism.

  5. Effect of iron supplementation on fatigue in nonanemic menstruating women with low ferritin: a randomized controlled trial
    Vaucher et al., 2012 · PMID 22777991 · positive

    In 198 non-anaemic women aged 18-53 with unexplained fatigue and ferritin below 50 micrograms/L, 12 weeks of oral ferrous sulfate (80 mg elemental iron daily) reduced fatigue scores by 47.7% versus 28.8% on placebo (difference -18.9%, 95% CI -34.5 to -3.2, p=0.02), with no significant effect on quality of life (p=0.2), depression (p=0.97) or anxiety (p=0.5).

  6. Efficacy of iron supplementation on fatigue and physical capacity in non-anaemic iron-deficient adults: a systematic review of randomised controlled trials
    Houston et al., 2018 · PMID 29626044 · mixed

    Across 18 unique trials enrolling 1,170 patients, iron supplementation reduced self-reported fatigue (SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.23, I-squared 0%, 4 trials, 714 participants) but was not associated with improvement in objective physical capacity, including maximal oxygen consumption (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.15 to 0.37, 9 trials, 235 participants) and timed exercise testing.

  7. Intravenous iron therapy for non-anaemic, iron-deficient adults (Cochrane review)
    Miles et al., 2019 · PMID 31860749 · null

    Across 11 studies with 1,074 participants, intravenous iron produced only a small, clinically insignificant rise in haemoglobin (MD 3.04 g/L, 95% CI 0.65 to 5.42; low-quality evidence), with quality-of-life scores and peak oxygen consumption rated very-low-quality; the incidence of severe adverse events across all studies was zero, and the reviewers concluded current evidence is insufficient to show benefit of intravenous iron for non-anaemic iron deficiency.

  8. Systematic review and meta-analysis of intravenous iron therapy for adults with non-anaemic iron deficiency: an abridged Cochrane review
    Dugan et al., 2022 · PMID 36321348 · mixed

    An updated reanalysis including 21 randomised trials and 3,514 participants found intravenous iron increased peak oxygen consumption versus placebo (MD 1.77 mL/kg/min, 95% CI 0.57 to 2.97) and reported an overall improvement in fatigue (standardised mean difference reported as 0.30, 95% CI -0.52 to -0.09), with no difference in quality of life (MD 0.15, 95% CI -0.01 to 0.31) and a higher risk of mild adverse events (RR 1.77, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.83); evidence quality was low or very low for all outcomes except fatigue.

  9. Effects of continuous positive airway pressure on fatigue and sleepiness in patients with obstructive sleep apnea: data from a randomized controlled trial
    Tomfohr et al., 2011 · PMID 21203367 · positive

    In 59 adults with obstructive sleep apnea randomised double-blind to 3 weeks of therapeutic versus placebo CPAP, therapeutic CPAP significantly reduced fatigue and increased vigor on two instruments (p<0.05), with the largest benefit in those most fatigued at baseline; sleepiness scores improved only in the subset with excessive sleepiness at onset.

  10. Continuous positive airway pressure improves sleepiness but not calculated vascular risk in patients with minimally symptomatic obstructive sleep apnoea: the MOSAIC randomised controlled trial
    Craig et al., 2012 · PMID 23111478 · mixed

    In 391 patients with confirmed but minimally symptomatic obstructive sleep apnoea randomised to 6 months of auto-adjusting CPAP or standard care, CPAP improved subjective daytime sleepiness (adjusted treatment effect on Epworth -2.0, 95% CI -2.6 to -1.4, p<0.0001), objective sleepiness and self-assessed health status, but did not improve 5-year calculated vascular risk or any of its components; 22% of the CPAP group reported stopping treatment and overall median CPAP use was 2 hours 39 minutes per night.
