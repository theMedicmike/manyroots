Ketamine and esketamine for treatment-resistant depression

Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood

For depression & low mood

Ketamine, and its nasal-spray cousin esketamine, work differently from ordinary antidepressants and can lift mood within hours or days rather than weeks, which is why they are used for depression that has not responded to two or more other medicines. The results are real but more mixed than the headlines suggest: only one of the three short-term approval trials actually hit its main target, a small trial that hid ketamine under surgical anaesthesia so nobody could feel it working found no benefit over salt water, and the benefit fades unless treatment continues. These are supervised medical treatments given in a clinic with monitoring afterwards, not something to arrange for yourself online.

Safety

These are supervised medical treatments, not self-administered ones. In the US, esketamine nasal spray is available only through a restricted REMS programme: it is taken in a certified healthcare setting under direct observation with at least two hours of monitoring afterwards, because of sedation, dissociation, transient blood-pressure rises and abuse potential; patients cannot drive until the next day after restful sleep. The most common trial adverse events were dissociation, nausea, dizziness, vertigo, dysgeusia and headache, generally appearing shortly after dosing and resolving by about 1.5 hours; in the flexibly dosed pivotal trial 7% of esketamine patients discontinued for an adverse event versus 0.9% of controls. Effectiveness in older adults is not established: the phase 3 trial restricted to patients 65 and over missed its primary endpoint and showed essentially no separation from placebo in those aged 75 and over. Repeated long-term ketamine exposure raises well-documented concerns about ulcerative cystitis and bladder damage, cognitive effects, and dependence, mostly characterised in heavy recreational use. Antidepressant effects fade without continuation treatment. Ketamine sold direct-to-consumer for unsupervised home use sits outside these monitoring structures and outside the trial evidence described here.

Who should be careful: US labelling contraindicates esketamine in people with aneurysmal vascular disease (including thoracic and abdominal aorta, intracranial and peripheral arterial vessels), arteriovenous malformation, or a history of intracerebral haemorrhage. Caution or avoidance with uncontrolled hypertension or significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, a history of psychosis, and current substance use disorder. Different in bipolar depression, which is outside this node: the pivotal trials excluded bipolar disorder, and mood-switch and psychosis risk mean any use there requires specialist assessment. Not established in pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Interactions: Additive sedation with benzodiazepines, opioids, alcohol and other CNS depressants. Psychostimulants and MAOIs may compound blood-pressure elevation. Esketamine is given alongside an oral antidepressant in the licensed regimen, and prescribers monitor blood pressure before and after each dose. Tell the treating team about every medicine and supplement, including anything sedating.

What the evidence says

In the flexibly dosed phase 3 trial (227 randomised), esketamine nasal spray plus a newly initiated oral antidepressant produced a significantly greater day-28 MADRS improvement than antidepressant plus placebo spray (difference in LS means -4.0, SE 1.69, 95% CI -7.31 to -0.64). The parallel fixed-dose trial (346 randomised) did not meet its primary endpoint: esketamine 84 mg versus placebo gave an LS mean difference of -3.2 (95% CI -6.88 to 0.45, two-sided p=0.088), and because of the pre-specified testing sequence the 56 mg result (-4.1, -7.67 to -0.49, nominal p=0.027) could not be formally tested. The phase 3 trial in patients 65 and over also missed its primary endpoint (median-unbiased treatment difference -3.6, 95% CI -7.20 to 0.07, p=0.059), with essentially no separation in those aged 75 and over (-0.4, 95% CI -10.38 to 9.50). A Cochrane review of 64 studies and 5299 participants found esketamine likely increased remission at 24 hours versus placebo (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.71-4.40; 894 participants, 5 studies, moderate certainty) while ketamine-versus-placebo response at 24 hours rested on very low certainty evidence (OR 3.94, 95% CI 1.54-10.10; 185 participants, 7 studies), with masking adequacy a stated limitation for all outcomes. When 40 patients were randomised to ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or saline during surgical anaesthesia, so that the psychoactive effects could not be detected, there was no evidence of an effect on MADRS at 1-3 days (-5.82, 95% CI -13.3 to 1.64, p=0.13) and only 36.8% guessed their allocation correctly. A pooled comparison put add-on esketamine's MADRS advantage over placebo at 4.09 points (95% CI 2.01-6.17, 3 trials, n=641), roughly double that of second-generation antipsychotic augmentation (2.05, 1.51-2.59; 22 trials, n=8363). In the open-label ELEKT-D trial (403 randomised), IV ketamine was noninferior to ECT, with response in 55.4% versus 41.2% (difference 14.2 percentage points, 95% CI 3.9-24.2). A former FDA reviewer has published seven specific concerns about the approval evidence.

Doses studied: Esketamine nasal spray was studied at 56 mg or 84 mg twice weekly for 4 weeks alongside a newly initiated oral antidepressant, with a lower 28 mg step available in the trial of patients aged 65 and over. Intravenous ketamine in the comparative-effectiveness trial was 0.5 mg per kg of body weight infused over 40 minutes, twice weekly for 3 weeks, and the same 0.5 mg/kg single dose was used in the anaesthesia-masked trial. Both are administered and monitored in a clinical setting; ketamine dosing in trials wore off within days, and continuation regimens rather than single doses are what sustain response.

Why this grade: There is genuine randomised evidence, including a regulator-approved agent, but the picture is mixed rather than uniformly positive and the grade is deliberately held below 'strong'. Of the three short-term phase 3 esketamine trials, only one met its primary endpoint: the flexibly dosed trial did (LS mean MADRS difference -4.0), while the fixed-dose trial did not (-3.2, 95% CI -6.88 to 0.45, p=0.088) and the trial in patients 65 and over did not (-3.6, 95% CI -7.20 to 0.07, p=0.059). Masking is the central methodological problem for a drug that produces obvious dissociation: a Cochrane review of 64 studies rated ketamine-versus-placebo evidence very low certainty and esketamine-versus-placebo remission only moderate certainty, explicitly citing inadequate detail about masking, and a small trial that masked ketamine inside surgical anaesthesia found no separation from saline. Effects are rapid but short-lived, durability data are thin, and the strongest comparative trial (ketamine noninferior to ECT) was open-label. Evidence applies to treatment-resistant depression, not to first-episode or mild depression.

  1. Efficacy and Safety of Flexibly Dosed Esketamine Nasal Spray Combined With a Newly Initiated Oral Antidepressant in Treatment-Resistant Depression: A Randomized Double-Blind Active-Controlled Study.
    Randomised trialPopova, Daly, Trivedi, et al., 2019 (Am J Psychiatry; TRANSFORM-2) · PMID 31109201 · positive

    Of 227 randomised patients with non-response to at least two antidepressants, esketamine (56 or 84 mg twice weekly) plus a new oral antidepressant produced a significantly greater day-28 MADRS improvement than antidepressant plus placebo spray (difference in LS means -4.0, SE 1.69, 95% CI -7.31 to -0.64). Dissociation, nausea, vertigo, dysgeusia and dizziness were more frequent with esketamine; 7% versus 0.9% discontinued for an adverse event, and adverse events generally appeared shortly after dosing and resolved by 1.5 hours.

  2. Efficacy and Safety of Fixed-Dose Esketamine Nasal Spray Combined With a New Oral Antidepressant in Treatment-Resistant Depression: Results of a Randomized, Double-Blind, Active-Controlled Study (TRANSFORM-1).
    Randomised trialFedgchin, Trivedi, Daly, et al., 2019 (Int J Neuropsychopharmacol) · PMID 31290965 · null

    In 346 randomised patients, the primary endpoint was not met: esketamine 84 mg plus antidepressant versus antidepressant plus placebo gave an LS mean MADRS difference of -3.2 (95% CI -6.88 to 0.45, two-sided p=0.088). Under the pre-defined testing sequence the 56 mg arm could not be formally tested, though its nominal difference was -4.1 (-7.67 to -0.49, nominal p=0.027).

  3. Efficacy and Safety of Esketamine Nasal Spray Plus an Oral Antidepressant in Elderly Patients With Treatment-Resistant Depression-TRANSFORM-3.
    Randomised trialOchs-Ross, Daly, Zhang, et al., 2020 (Am J Geriatr Psychiatry; TRANSFORM-3) · PMID 31734084 · null

    In this phase 3 trial restricted to patients aged 65 and over, esketamine plus a new oral antidepressant did not achieve statistical significance on the primary endpoint: median-unbiased treatment difference -3.6 (95% CI -7.20 to 0.07), weighted combination test z=1.89, two-sided p=0.059. Prespecified age analysis showed an adjusted mean difference of -4.9 (95% CI -8.96 to -0.89, nominal p=0.017) at 65-74 years but -0.4 (95% CI -10.38 to 9.50, nominal p=0.930) at 75 and over.

  4. Ketamine and other glutamate receptor modulators for depression in adults with unipolar major depressive disorder.
    Systematic reviewDean, Hurducas, Hawton, et al., 2021 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews) · PMID 34510411 · mixed

    Across 64 studies and 5299 participants, esketamine likely increased remission at 24 hours versus placebo (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.71-4.40; n=894, 5 studies, moderate certainty), while ketamine's response advantage at 24 hours rested on very low certainty evidence (OR 3.94, 95% CI 1.54-10.10; n=185, 7 studies). The reviewers stated that lack of detail regarding masking of treatment reduced certainty in the effect for all outcomes, and that how the findings translate into clinical practice is not entirely clear.

  5. Randomized trial of ketamine masked by surgical anesthesia in patients with depression.
    Journal articleLii, Smith, Flohr, et al., 2023 (Nature Mental Health) · PMID 38188539 · null

    In a single-centre, triple-masked, placebo-controlled trial, 40 adults with major depressive disorder scheduled for routine surgery received a single infusion of ketamine 0.5 mg/kg or saline during usual anaesthesia, so that the acute psychoactive effects could not be perceived. A mixed-effects model showed no evidence of an effect of treatment assignment on MADRS at 1, 2 and 3 days post-infusion (-5.82, 95% CI -13.3 to 1.64, p=0.13); only 36.8% of participants guessed their assignment correctly, and both groups guessed in similar proportions. The authors concluded a single dose had no greater effect than placebo when masking was complete.

  6. Ketamine versus ECT for Nonpsychotic Treatment-Resistant Major Depression.
    Randomised trialAnand, Mathew, Sanacora, et al., 2023 (NEJM; ELEKT-D) · PMID 37224232 · positive

    In an open-label noninferiority trial of 403 patients referred to ECT clinics, response occurred in 55.4% of the ketamine group (0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes twice weekly) versus 41.2% of the ECT group (difference 14.2 percentage points, 95% CI 3.9-24.2, P<0.001 for noninferiority). Ketamine was associated with dissociation and ECT with musculoskeletal adverse effects; the trial was not blinded and excluded psychotic depression.

  7. Treatment Response of Add-On Esketamine Nasal Spray in Resistant Major Depression in Relation to Add-On Second-Generation Antipsychotic Treatment.
    Systematic reviewDold, Bartova, Kasper, 2020 (Int J Neuropsychopharmacol) · PMID 32570275 · positive

    Pooling 3 intranasal esketamine trials (n=641) gave a mean MADRS difference versus placebo of 4.09 points (95% CI 2.01-6.17), compared with 2.05 points (95% CI 1.51-2.59) from 22 second-generation antipsychotic augmentation trials (n=8363), an effect size nearly twice as large. This is an indirect comparison across separate trial sets, not a head-to-head randomisation.

  8. Esketamine for treatment-resistant depression: seven concerns about efficacy and FDA approval.
    Journal articleTurner, 2019 (Lancet Psychiatry) · PMID 31680014 · negative

    A published commentary from a former FDA reviewer setting out seven specific concerns about the efficacy evidence underpinning esketamine's approval, including reliance on a short-term trial programme in which the majority of the acute trials did not meet their primary endpoints. Included here as the principal dissenting appraisal of the approval evidence; no abstract is indexed in PubMed, so no effect estimates are quoted.
