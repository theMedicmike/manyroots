Rifaximin

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Rifaximin is an antibiotic that stays almost entirely inside the gut instead of being absorbed into the body, given as a two-week course rather than long-term. In two large trials, about 41 people in 100 got meaningful relief of their overall IBS symptoms and bloating compared with about 32 in 100 on a dummy pill — a real but modest edge, meaning roughly one extra person benefits for every ten treated. It is only for IBS with diarrhoea or mixed bowel habit, not for constipation-type IBS, symptoms come back within a few months for most people who respond, and its effect on abdominal pain specifically has been inconsistent across pooled analyses.

Safety

Tolerability across the phase 2b and 3 programme was essentially indistinguishable from placebo, with no Clostridioides difficile colitis and no deaths in 1,103 treated patients (Schoenfeld 2014, PMID 24697851), and in a network meta-analysis of licensed IBS-D drugs rifaximin ranked first for safety (PMID 30996042). In a propensity-matched cohort of 669,083 US adults with IBS, rifaximin was not associated with increased all-cause mortality (Mehravar 2026, PMID 41951879) — observational, but reassuring for a drug given in repeated courses. The genuine open questions the trial authors themselves flag are the risk of C. difficile colitis and of antimicrobial resistance with repeated courses over years, which the trials were not long or large enough to settle. Because it is an antibiotic and its effect is temporary — roughly two-thirds of initial responders relapsed within 18 weeks in TARGET 3 — repeat courses are usual, which is exactly the pattern for which long-term safety data are thinnest. Coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease should be ruled out before treating diarrhoea as IBS; the 2021 ACG guideline (PMID 33315591) suggests coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin in this setting. Cost and insurance prior authorisation are, in practice, the most common barriers to use in the United States.

Who should be careful: Hypersensitivity to rifaximin or other rifamycins. It is studied and licensed only for IBS without constipation (IBS-D and mixed subtypes) and has no established role in IBS-C. Caution in severe hepatic impairment, where systemic exposure to this normally minimally absorbed drug is increased.

Interactions: Rifaximin is described in its trials as a nonsystemic, gut-targeted, minimally absorbed antibiotic, so clinically important drug interactions are uncommon. Per its prescribing information it is a P-glycoprotein substrate, and inhibitors such as ciclosporin can raise its systemic levels. Unlike rifampicin, it is not a meaningful inducer of systemic drug metabolism at licensed doses, so it does not carry rifampicin's contraceptive and warfarin interaction problems.

What the evidence says

In the identically designed phase 3 TARGET 1 and TARGET 2 trials, patients with non-constipated IBS given rifaximin 550 mg three times daily for 2 weeks achieved adequate relief of global IBS symptoms in 40.7% versus 31.7% on placebo across both trials combined (p<0.001), and adequate relief of bloating in 40.2% versus 30.3% (p<0.001). A meta-analysis of five RCTs put global symptom improvement at OR 1.57 (95% CI 1.22-2.01), a therapeutic gain of 9.8% with NNT 10.2, and bloating at OR 1.55 (95% CI 1.23-1.96), a gain of 9.9% with NNT 10.1 — a gain the authors themselves describe as modest and similar to other available IBS therapies. Benefit on abdominal pain specifically is not established: a four-trial meta-analysis found abdominal pain OR 1.01 (95% CI 0.98-1.03, non-significant) with abdominal distention non-significant at the treatment endpoint (OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.96-1.49) and separating only at follow-up, and a separate six-trial meta-analysis likewise found no significant difference from control for abdominal pain. Rifaximin is also not the most effective licensed option: in a network meta-analysis of 18 RCTs and 9,844 patients with IBS-D or IBS-M, all drugs beat placebo but alosetron 1 mg twice daily and ramosetron 2.5 micrograms once daily ranked above rifaximin for efficacy, while rifaximin ranked first for safety. Durability is limited: in the TARGET 3 retreatment trial, 1,074 patients (44.1% of those treated) responded to an open-label 2-week course, and 692 of them (64.4%) relapsed during an observation phase of up to 18 weeks; on retreatment 38.1% responded versus 31.5% on placebo (p=0.03), with abdominal pain improved (50.6% vs 42.2%, p=0.018) but stool consistency no different from placebo (51.8% vs 50.0%, p=0.42). Tolerability is its main advantage: pooled across phase 2b and phase 3 trials (1,103 rifaximin, 829 placebo), drug-related adverse events were 12.1% versus 10.7%, serious adverse events 1.5% versus 2.2%, with no cases of Clostridioides difficile colitis and no deaths. The AGA makes a conditional recommendation at moderate certainty; the ACG recommends it for global IBS-with-diarrhoea symptoms.

Doses studied: Rifaximin 550 mg three times daily for 14 days (TARGET 1, TARGET 2 and TARGET 3). The phase 2b programme also studied 275, 550 and 1,100 mg twice daily for 2 weeks and 550 mg twice daily for 4 weeks. Retreatment in TARGET 3 was a further 14-day course at 550 mg three times daily on relapse.

Why this grade: Two large identically designed phase 3 RCTs plus a phase 3 retreatment trial and several meta-analyses show a consistent benefit in non-constipated IBS, and the ACG recommends it for global IBS-with-diarrhoea symptoms — but the therapeutic gain over placebo is only about 9-10% (NNT around 10), abdominal pain specifically did not separate from placebo in two meta-analyses, a network meta-analysis of licensed IBS-D drugs ranked it below alosetron and ramosetron for efficacy, roughly two-thirds of initial responders relapse, and the AGA recommendation is conditional at moderate certainty. Consistent but small, so moderate rather than strong.

  1. Rifaximin therapy for patients with irritable bowel syndrome without constipation.
    Randomised trialPimentel M, Lembo A, Chey WD, et al., 2011 (New England Journal of Medicine) · PMID 21208106 · positive

    In two identically designed phase 3 trials (TARGET 1 and 2), rifaximin 550 mg three times daily for 2 weeks produced adequate relief of global IBS symptoms in 40.8% vs 31.2% (p=0.01) and 40.6% vs 32.2% (p=0.03), combined 40.7% vs 31.7% (p<0.001). Adequate relief of bloating was 39.5% vs 28.7% (p=0.005) and 41.0% vs 31.9% (p=0.02), combined 40.2% vs 30.3% (p<0.001). Adverse event incidence was similar between groups. Funded by Salix Pharmaceuticals.

  2. The efficacy and safety of rifaximin for the irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisMenees SB, Maneerattannaporn M, Kim HM, Chey WD, 2012 (American Journal of Gastroenterology) · PMID 22045120 · positive

    Five eligible RCTs gave an OR of 1.57 (95% CI 1.22-2.01) for global IBS symptom improvement, a therapeutic gain of 9.8% and NNT of 10.2, with mild heterogeneity (I-squared 26%). For bloating, OR 1.55 (95% CI 1.23-1.96), therapeutic gain 9.9%, NNT 10.1. The authors describe the therapeutic gain as modest and similar to other available IBS therapies. Age was the strongest predictor of response, correlating 0.97 with response rate in the placebo groups — a reminder that much of the observed improvement is not drug-specific.

  3. Rifaximin for Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trials.
    Meta-analysisLi J, Zhu W, Liu W, Wu Y, Wu B, 2016 (Medicine) · PMID 26825893 · mixed

    Pooling four RCTs on intention-to-treat, overall symptom relief favoured rifaximin at end of treatment (OR 1.19, 95% CI 1.08-1.32) and follow-up (OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.18-1.58). However, abdominal pain did not differ from placebo (OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.98-1.03, p>0.05), and abdominal distention was non-significant at the treatment endpoint (OR 1.19, 95% CI 0.96-1.49), reaching significance only at follow-up (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.27-2.23).

  4. Effectiveness of Rifaximin on the Outcomes of Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Narrative reviewKhan Z, Khan SK, Reyaz I, et al., 2023 (Cureus) · PMID 37809182 · mixed

    Pooling six RCTs, rifaximin improved abdominal distension and overall symptom relief at both the end of treatment and follow-up, but no significant differences were found between rifaximin and control for abdominal pain, nausea, headache, vomiting, diarrhoea, sinusitis, bronchitis or upper respiratory tract infection. This is the second independent meta-analysis to find no separation from placebo on abdominal pain specifically. Published in a low-tier journal, so it carries less weight than the Menees or Li analyses, but its pain result is concordant with theirs.

  5. Repeat Treatment With Rifaximin Is Safe and Effective in Patients With Diarrhea-Predominant Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    Randomised trialLembo A, Pimentel M, Rao SS, et al., 2016 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 27528177 · mixed

    1,074 patients (44.1% of those given an open-label 2-week rifaximin course) responded; of those responders, 382 (35.6%) did not relapse and 692 (64.4%) did during an observation phase of up to 18 weeks. 636 relapsers were randomised to repeat treatment: 38.1% responded versus 31.5% on placebo (p=0.03) and abdominal pain responders were 50.6% versus 42.2% (p=0.018), but stool consistency response did not differ (51.8% vs 50.0%, p=0.42). Significant improvements were also seen for prevention of recurrence, durable response and bowel movement urgency. Adverse event rates were low and similar between groups. This quantifies both the limited durability and the modest size of the retreatment effect.

  6. Efficacy of pharmacological therapies in patients with IBS with diarrhoea or mixed stool pattern: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisBlack CJ, Burr NE, Camilleri M, et al., 2020 (Gut) · PMID 30996042 · mixed

    Across 18 RCTs and 9,844 patients (seven alosetron, five ramosetron, two rifaximin, four eluxadoline), all licensed drugs were superior to placebo at 12 weeks on the FDA-recommended endpoint, but alosetron 1 mg twice daily ranked first for efficacy on the composite endpoint, global symptoms and stool consistency, and ramosetron 2.5 micrograms once daily ranked first for abdominal pain. Rifaximin 550 mg three times daily ranked first for safety and was the only drug for which constipation was not significantly more common than placebo. Rifaximin is therefore the best-tolerated licensed IBS-D option, not the most effective one.

  7. Safety and tolerability of rifaximin for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome without constipation: a pooled analysis of randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials.
    Journal articleSchoenfeld P, Pimentel M, Chang L, et al., 2014 (Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics) · PMID 24697851 · null

    Pooling phase 2b and phase 3 data (1,103 rifaximin, 829 placebo), drug-related adverse events were 12.1% versus 10.7%, serious adverse events 1.5% versus 2.2%, discontinuation for drug-related adverse events 0.8% versus 0.8%, gastrointestinal adverse events 12.2% versus 12.2% and infection-associated adverse events 8.5% versus 9.5%. There were no cases of Clostridioides difficile colitis and no deaths. The authors state explicitly that future research should define the safety profile, including risk of C. difficile colitis and microbial antibiotic resistance, with repeated courses.

  8. AGA Clinical Practice Guideline on the Pharmacological Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome With Diarrhea.
    GuidelineLembo A, Sultan S, Chang L, et al., 2022 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 35738725 · mixed

    The AGA panel made a conditional — not strong — recommendation for rifaximin in IBS-D at moderate certainty of evidence, alongside eluxadoline and alosetron. The 2021 ACG guideline (PMID 33315591) is somewhat firmer, recommending rifaximin to treat global IBS-with-diarrhoea symptoms.
