Herbs

Boswellia (Indian frankincense)

Early evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Boswellia, also called Indian frankincense, looks good on paper for knee arthritis; when researchers pool the studies together it often comes out near the top of the supplement list. The catch is that nearly every one of those studies was small and run or funded by the company selling the extract, and the clearest independent test involved just 30 people over eight weeks. A newer independent trial of a blend containing Boswellia found no benefit over a dummy pill at all. It is a reasonable thing to raise with a clinician for joint pain, but the confident marketing claims run well ahead of the evidence, and nobody has tested it for longer than three months.

Safety

Boswellia was well tolerated across trials of 30 to 90 days, with adverse events not significantly different from placebo in pooled analyses (PMIDs 40806131, 29622343). The independent 30-patient crossover trial reported minor gastrointestinal adverse drug reactions as the only tolerability issue (PMID 12622457); the 90-day 5-Loxin/Aflapin trial reported essentially unchanged serum, urine and haematological safety parameters (PMID 21060724); the 150-participant BOSMAX trial reported one mild, unrelated adverse event with unchanged vital signs and laboratory parameters (PMID 41220252); and the ATLAS trial found similar adverse-event rates between a Boswellia-containing combination and placebo (PMID 42331134). A 90-day open-label study in 40 healthy adults taking a turmeric-Boswellia-sesame formulation at 2,000 mg/day found no clinically significant change in liver enzymes, bilirubin, renal function markers, lipids or blood counts, with no Hy's law or eDISH signals (PMID 39778015); it is small and uncontrolled, so it supports tolerability rather than proving safety. Because no trial has run beyond 90 days, nothing is known about continuous longer-term use.

Who should be careful: There is no trial evidence in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children, so Boswellia is not established as safe in these groups. Anyone with a history of peptic ulcer, gastritis or reflux should note that mild gastrointestinal upset was the adverse effect most consistently reported in trials. Tell your surgical team about any Boswellia supplement before a planned operation. If a product combines Boswellia with turmeric, the turmeric liver-injury cautions apply to the whole product.

Interactions: Formal drug-interaction studies for Boswellia are absent from the trial literature reviewed here, so the absence of reported interactions is not evidence that none exist. Boswellic acids are studied as 5-lipoxygenase inhibitors, but no clinical study has tested Boswellia alongside anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, immunosuppressants or NSAIDs. A practical hazard is product composition: many trial and retail products are proprietary multi-ingredient blends (with turmeric, celery seed, pine bark, MSM, piperine, vitamins or minerals), and a combination product carries the interaction profile of every component, including turmeric's liver-injury signal and piperine's absorption-enhancing effect. Read the full ingredient panel, and tell a pharmacist what you are taking.

What the evidence says

A 2025 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 39 RCTs (42 studies, 4,599 patients with knee osteoarthritis) ranked Boswellia highest of seven supplements for pain and stiffness, with mean differences versus placebo of 10.58 (95% CI 6.45 to 14.78) on WOMAC pain, 9.47 (6.39 to 12.74) on stiffness, 14.00 (7.74 to 20.21) on function and 17.26 (8.06 to 26.52) on VAS pain, with no excess adverse events. The underlying trials are uniformly small and largely industry-run: the 90-day 5-Loxin and Aflapin trial randomized 60 patients across three arms at the R&D centre that developed the products, the 90-day BOSMAX trial of 150 adults was co-authored by staff of the supplying companies, and the only clearly independent placebo-controlled study is a 30-patient, 8-week crossover trial from 2003. Against this, the independent 12-week ATLAS trial (n=84, 2026) tested a combination containing Boswellia serrata 250 mg and found no difference from placebo in VAS pain (0.16 mm, 95% CI -6.81 to 7.12), concluding the evidence does not support its use. A 2018 systematic review of 11 curcuminoid and Boswellia RCTs (n=1,009) found Boswellia statistically superior to placebo for pain and function but concluded the evidence base 'is not adequate in size or quality to make any meaningful clinical practice recommendations,' noting that no RCT has compared Boswellia against an approved NSAID. A multicentre Belgian trial of 162 patients with hand osteoarthritis tested a fixed Curcuma longa plus Boswellia serrata combination and found a between-group VAS pain difference of only -8.5 mm (95% CI -16.4 to -0.6, p=0.03) with most secondary outcomes not significantly different, and cannot attribute that effect to Boswellia. A 2019 GRADE-based review of dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain made no recommendation for boswellia, and Boswellia does not appear among the therapies recommended in the 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline; all of this evidence is in osteoarthritis, since the chronic low back pain trials test Boswellia only as one component of multi-ingredient formulations.

Doses studied: The enriched proprietary extracts 5-Loxin and Aflapin were tested at 100 mg/day for 30 to 90 days (PMID 21060724 at 90 days; PMID 22022214 at 30 days). The BOSMAX extract trial dosed 150 adults aged 40-75 daily for 90 days (PMID 41220252). The ATLAS combination trial delivered Boswellia serrata extract 250 mg/day for 12 weeks alongside four other ingredients (PMID 42331134). Older whole gum-resin extract trials used daily doses not stated in the abstracts reviewed here. Doses are not comparable across products because boswellic acid content, and specifically the acetyl-11-keto-beta-boswellic acid fraction that the enriched extracts are built around, differs widely between preparations. No trial has extended beyond 90 days.

Why this grade: There is a surface case for grading this moderate: roughly nine to twenty RCTs exist, and a 2025 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 39 trials ranks Boswellia at or near the top of tested supplements for knee osteoarthritis pain and stiffness. Following the instruction to take the lower grade when torn, it is graded early on four grounds. First, essentially every trial is small (arms of 20 to 75) and none has run longer than 90 days. Second, the largest positive trials were conducted or sponsored by the companies that developed the test extracts, so the clearly independent evidence base is effectively one 30-patient crossover trial from 2003 plus one multicentre trial of a fixed Boswellia-plus-turmeric combination that cannot attribute its effect to Boswellia. Third, the most recent and most clearly independent placebo-controlled trial, ATLAS (2026), tested a combination containing Boswellia 250 mg and found no separation from placebo at all. Fourth, the most careful independent synthesis concludes the evidence 'is not adequate in size or quality to make any meaningful clinical practice recommendations'; a 2019 GRADE-based review made no recommendation for boswellia; and Boswellia does not appear among the therapies recommended in the 2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline. The evidence is also osteoarthritis-specific; the chronic low back pain trials test Boswellia only inside multi-ingredient products.

Sources (9)
  1. Comparative Effectiveness of Nutritional Supplements in the Treatment of Knee Osteoarthritis: A Network Meta-Analysis.
    Network meta-analysisZhang Y, Gui Y, Adams R, Farragher J, Itsiopoulos C, Bow K, Cai M, Han J, 2025 · PMID 40806131 · positive

    39 RCTs (42 studies, 4,599 patients with knee osteoarthritis) comparing eggshell membrane, vitamin D, Boswellia, curcumin, ginger, krill oil and collagen against placebo. Boswellia showed significant improvements versus placebo in WOMAC pain (MD 10.58, 95% CI 6.45 to 14.78), stiffness (MD 9.47, 95% CI 6.39 to 12.74), function (MD 14.00, 95% CI 7.74 to 20.21) and VAS pain (MD 17.26, 95% CI 8.06 to 26.52). Bayesian ranking gave Boswellia the highest probability of being most effective for pain and stiffness. No supplement increased adverse events. The authors call for larger trials with standardized dosages and formulations and note that long-term efficacy remains unevaluated.

  2. Efficacy of curcumin and Boswellia for knee osteoarthritis: Systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisBannuru RR, Osani MC, Al-Eid F, Wang C, 2018 · PMID 29622343 · mixed

    11 RCTs, 1,009 patients with knee osteoarthritis. Boswellia formulations were statistically significantly more effective than placebo for pain relief and functional improvement, with no significant safety difference versus placebo. However the authors report that 'study quality was low overall, and most included RCTs were conducted on fewer than 100 participants,' and that no RCT compared boswellia against approved NSAIDs. Their conclusion is explicit: 'The current body of evidence is not adequate in size or quality to make any meaningful clinical practice recommendations.'

  3. Effect of an oral complementary medicine combination for symptomatic knee osteoarthritis: A double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial.
    Randomised trialYuan S, Liu X, Bracken K, Christensen R, Deveza LA, Collins S, et al., 2026 · PMID 42331134 · negative

    The independent 12-week ATLAS trial (University of Sydney, 2024-2025) randomized 84 adults with symptomatic knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 or greater) to placebo or a daily combination of Boswellia serrata extract 250 mg, pine bark extract 100 mg, methylsulfonylmethane 1500 mg, curcumin 500 mg and piperine 5 mg. Both arms improved similarly (least squares mean VAS pain decrease 20.1 mm versus 20.3 mm) with no between-group difference: 0.16 mm (95% CI -6.81 to 7.12). Adverse events were similar (risk difference -3.4%, 95% CI -24.8 to 17.9); four serious adverse events were unrelated to treatment. The authors conclude the combination 'was not superior to placebo' and 'the evidence does not support the use of this combination'.

  4. Clinical Benefits of Boswellia Serrata (BOSMAX®) in Early Knee Osteoarthritis: A Randomized, Placebo-Controlled, Double-Blind Study.
    Journal articleJayaram M, Kim J, Baek KS, Jung H, Kim J, Priya MK, et al., 2025 · PMID 41220252 · positive

    150 adults aged 40-75 with non/mild knee osteoarthritis randomized to BOSMAX Boswellia extract or placebo daily for 90 days. Mean total WOMAC scores decreased significantly at day 30 (-6.28), day 60 (-15.24) and day 90 (-24.55, p<0.05), with significant reductions in WOMAC sub-scores and VAS pain at days 60 and 90 and progressive Lequesne Index improvement (-1.37, -2.93, -4.53). TNF-alpha fell -104.75 ng/L and hs-CRP -5.67 ng/mL at day 90. However, no significant change was observed in SF-36 quality-of-life scores. Only one mild, unrelated adverse event was reported. Four of the ten authors are employed by Daehan Chemtech, Cosmax Bio or Katra Phytochem, the companies supplying the test extract.

  5. Efficacy and tolerability of Boswellia serrata extract in treatment of osteoarthritis of knee--a randomized double blind placebo controlled trial.
    Randomised trialKimmatkar N, Thawani V, Hingorani L, Khiyani R, 2003 · PMID 12622457 · positive

    Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 30 patients with knee osteoarthritis at Indira Gandhi Medical College, Nagpur; 15 patients per starting sequence, 8 weeks per period with washout. All patients during active treatment reported decreased knee pain, increased knee flexion and increased walking distance, with decreased frequency of knee swelling, and the differences versus placebo were reported as statistically significant. There was no radiological change. Boswellia was well tolerated apart from minor gastrointestinal adverse drug reactions. This is the smallest possible evidentiary base: n=30, single centre, 8 weeks per period.

  6. Comparative efficacy and tolerability of 5-Loxin and AflapinAgainst osteoarthritis of the knee: a double blind, randomized, placebo controlled clinical study.
    Randomised trialSengupta K, Krishnaraju AV, Vishal AA, Mishra A, Trimurtulu G, Sarma KVS, et al., 2010 · PMID 21060724 · positive

    90-day double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 60 patients with knee osteoarthritis randomized to 100 mg/day 5-Loxin (n=20), 100 mg/day Aflapin (n=20) or placebo (n=20); 57 completed. Both Boswellia extracts produced statistically significant improvements in visual analog scale, Lequesne's Functional Index and WOMAC scores, with Aflapin showing significant improvement as early as day 7. Serum, urine and haematological safety parameters were essentially unchanged versus placebo. The study was conducted at the Laila Impex R&D Center, which developed the test products; the abstract identifies Aflapin as the subject of Indian Patent Application No. 2229/CHE/2008.

  7. Effect of a combination of C. longa and B. serrata extracts on hand osteoarthritis. Results of a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial.
    Randomised trialHenrotin Y, de Schrijver F, De Vlam K, Devinck M, Joos R, Luyten H, et al., 2025 · PMID 40554037 · mixed

    162 patients meeting ACR criteria for symptomatic hand osteoarthritis, randomized across multiple Belgian centres to a fixed patented Curcuma longa plus Boswellia serrata combination (CBTIL, n=83) or placebo (n=79) for 3 months. Mean VAS pain change from baseline was -24.7 mm (95% CI -30.7 to -18.7) with the combination versus -16.2 mm (95% CI -22.5 to -9.9) with placebo, a between-group difference of -8.5 mm (95% CI -16.4 to -0.6, p=0.03). Patient global assessment, PASS achievement and two SF-36 domains improved, but 'other outcomes did not significantly differ between the two groups.' As a fixed combination of a patented proprietary product, the trial cannot attribute the effect to Boswellia rather than turmeric.

  8. Dietary Ingredients as an Alternative Approach for Mitigating Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: Evidence-Based Recommendations for Practice and Research in the Military.
    Journal articleCrawford C, Boyd C, Paat CF, Meissner K, Lentino C, Teo L, Berry K, Deuster P, 2019 · PMID 30986309 · null

    A steering committee assessed 19 dietary ingredients for chronic musculoskeletal pain using systematic review, meta-analysis where feasible, and GRADE. No recommendation was made for boswellia, placing it with ginger as a supplement, rose hip and s-adenosyl-L-methionine among ingredients where the evidence was judged insufficient to recommend either way; eight other ingredients were conditionally recommended and eight were recommended against. The panel concluded the scientific evidence is 'insufficiently robust to establish definitive clinical practice guidelines.'

  9. 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Osteoarthritis of the Hand, Hip, and Knee.
    GuidelineKolasinski SL, Neogi T, Hochberg MC, Oatis C, Guyatt G, Block J, et al., 2020 · PMID 31908149 · null

    GRADE-based guideline for hand, hip and knee osteoarthritis. Strong recommendations were made for exercise, weight loss, self-management programmes, tai chi, cane use, hand orthoses for first carpometacarpal osteoarthritis, tibiofemoral bracing, topical NSAIDs for knee osteoarthritis, oral NSAIDs, and intra-articular glucocorticoid injections; conditional recommendations covered balance exercise, yoga, cognitive behavioural therapy, acupuncture, thermal modalities, chondroitin sulfate for hand osteoarthritis, topical capsaicin, acetaminophen, duloxetine and tramadol. Boswellia does not appear among the recommended therapies.
