Herbs

Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora / Scutellaria baicalensis)

Early evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Skullcap has been used in North America and Europe as a calming nerve tonic for over two hundred years, and in Chinese medicine even longer. There is now one real placebo-controlled trial of American skullcap in people who actually have insomnia, and it found better sleep quality, faster falling asleep and more total sleep - genuinely promising, but it is one small study that nobody has repeated yet, and the participants still did not reach what researchers call good sleep. Worth knowing too: the two plants sold as skullcap are different, and the Chinese one has a documented case of liver injury behind it, so this is not a simply-harmless herb.

Safety

Species matters for safety as much as for identity. In the 66-participant insomnia crossover trial of American skullcap (S. lateriflora), no participant reported an adverse effect over 56 days of 400 mg/day and physicians judged the product well tolerated - the best short-term human safety data available for this species, though it is a single small trial. Chinese skullcap (S. baicalensis) is a different matter: a published case describes cholestatic hepatitis on a supplement containing it, with biochemical improvement on withdrawal, recurrence on unintentional rechallenge, and a liver biopsy consistent with acute drug-induced liver injury. In the 180-participant S. baicalensis depression trial, tolerability was reported as good, with 66 adverse events across all arms, only 13 judged possibly product-related, and the single severe event in the scutellaria arm judged unrelated. One trial of a S. baicalensis-containing supplement recorded worse self-rated sleep quality and higher sleep-related psychological tension than placebo, so a paradoxical effect on perceived sleep cannot be dismissed. No long-term human safety dataset exists for either species, and no pregnancy, lactation or paediatric data were retrieved; the insomnia trial excluded pregnant and breastfeeding women and anyone under 18 or over 70.

Who should be careful: Avoid Chinese skullcap in people with liver disease or abnormal liver enzymes, and stop immediately if jaundice, dark urine, right-upper-quadrant pain or fatigue develop. Because no pregnancy, breastfeeding or paediatric safety data were found for either species, avoid in those groups. The only insomnia evidence is in adults aged 18-70 with primary insomnia and without significant anxiety or depression, who were free of sleep apnoea, restless legs and other sleep disorders; it does not extend to secondary insomnia, to severe insomnia, or to people already taking prescribed hypnotics.

Interactions: No formal human drug-interaction studies were retrieved for either species. Given the documented hepatotoxicity signal for Chinese skullcap, combining it with other potentially hepatotoxic drugs or supplements, or using it in anyone with existing liver disease, warrants clinician oversight. Additive sedation with alcohol, benzodiazepines and other CNS depressants is plausible rather than demonstrated: skullcap flavonoids (baicalin, baicalein, wogonin) bind the GABA-benzodiazepine receptor in laboratory work, but the insomnia trial specifically excluded people taking benzodiazepines, antidepressants or other relaxing supplements, so the combination has never been studied.

What the evidence says

One randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial has studied a Scutellaria species in insomnia itself. Di Minno and colleagues (2025) gave 66 adults aged 18-70 (mean 44 +/- 15) with mild-to-moderate primary insomnia a S. lateriflora aerial-part hydroethanolic extract 400 mg/day standardised to 10% baicalin, or placebo, for 56 days each with a 28-day washout. PSQI showed significant measurement, treatment and measurement-by-treatment effects (all p<0.001), falling on the supplement between t0 and t1 (beta = 1.88 +/- 0.31, p<0.001) and t1 and t2 (beta = 1.68 +/- 0.31, p=0.0038) while rising on placebo (t1 to t2 beta = 1.15 +/- 0.31, p<0.001), with a between-treatment difference at t2 of beta = 3.77 +/- 0.31 (p<0.001); sleep efficiency and total sleep time improved on supplement only, and no adverse effects were reported. The authors report a carry-over effect indicating the washout was too short, and the PSQI reduction never reached the threshold of 5 below which sleep is considered good. Evidence in Chinese skullcap is weaker and partly contrary: in 43 stressed adults (35 analysed), a S. baicalensis plus hawthorn plus magnesium/chromium supplement increased total sleep time to 452.16 minutes versus 425.18 on placebo at day 15 (p=0.037) but produced significantly worse overall self-rated sleep quality than placebo (6.00 vs 5.19, p=0.027), and a 180-participant RCT of S. baicalensis 20 mg/day in mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms showed a Beck Depression Inventory session effect (p<0.001) with no significant session-by-group interaction, its three-item HAMD sleep improvement occurring only in the saffron arm, though scutellaria did beat placebo on WHO-5 well-being (p=0.016). Earlier human work addressed mood, not sleep: S. lateriflora 350 mg three times daily for two weeks in 43 healthy volunteers did not differ from placebo on the Beck Anxiety Inventory (p=0.191). A 2015 systematic review of 14 insomnia RCTs found no monopreparation trial of any Scutellaria species at that time.

Doses studied: S. lateriflora dried hydroethanolic aerial-part extract 400 mg/day standardised to 10% baicalin, one capsule daily for 56 days - the only dose tested in people with insomnia. S. lateriflora 350 mg three times daily for 2 weeks was used for mood in healthy volunteers. S. baicalensis extract 20 mg/day standardised to 90.04% baicalin (18.08 mg baicalin) for 6 weeks was used for depressive symptoms; no S. baicalensis monopreparation dose has been tested for sleep.

Why this grade: One randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial has tested American skullcap (S. lateriflora) in the actual condition: 66 adults with mild-to-moderate primary insomnia, 400 mg/day for 56 days, positive on the PSQI primary outcome and on sleep latency, sleep efficiency and total sleep time. That single small trial is what lifts this above traditional, and its limits are what hold it at early: a single centre, subjective questionnaires only with no actigraphy or polysomnography, a carry-over effect the authors concede means the 28-day washout was too short for a crossover design, a placebo arm whose sleep got worse over time, baseline imbalance favouring placebo, PSQI never falling below the threshold for good sleep, and study product supplied by its manufacturer. Nothing has been replicated. Evidence in the other species is weaker and partly contrary: a 2025 RCT of a S. baicalensis-containing supplement in stressed adults found longer total sleep time but significantly worse self-rated sleep quality than placebo, and a 2025 RCT of S. baicalensis in depressive symptoms was null on its primary outcome with the sleep-item improvement occurring only in the saffron arm. A documented case of drug-induced liver injury with positive rechallenge involving Chinese skullcap argues against any more generous grade.

  1. Efficacy and Tolerability of a Chemically Characterized Scutellaria lateriflora L. Extract-Based Food Supplement for Sleep Management: A Single-Center, Controlled, Randomized, Crossover, Double-Blind Clinical Trial.
    Randomised trialDi Minno A, Morone MV, Buccato DG, De Lellis LF, Ullah H, Piccinocchi R, et al., 2025 · PMID 40362800 · positive

    Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 66 adults with mild-to-moderate PRIMARY INSOMNIA, S. lateriflora extract 400 mg/day standardised to 10% baicalin for 56 days with 28-day washout. PSQI showed significant measurement, treatment and measurement-by-treatment effects (all p<0.001): scores fell on supplement (t0-t1 beta = 1.88 +/- 0.31, p<0.001; t1-t2 beta = 1.68 +/- 0.31, p=0.0038) and rose on placebo (t1-t2 beta = 1.15 +/- 0.31, p<0.001), difference at t2 beta = 3.77 +/- 0.31, p<0.001. Sleep efficiency rose on supplement only (t1-t2 beta = 5.87 +/- 1.46, p<0.001; between-treatment at t2 beta = 6.48 +/- 1.46, p<0.001) and total sleep time likewise (t1-t2 beta = 0.67 +/- 0.12, p<0.001), with no significant placebo change on either. No adverse effects over 56 days. Limitations the authors state or the data show: a carry-over effect indicating the 28-day washout was insufficient, subjective outcomes only, PSQI not reaching the <5 good-sleep threshold, single centre, and baseline imbalance in which the supplement arm started with worse sleep onset latency (p<0.001) and lower sleep efficiency (p=0.0026) than placebo.

  2. Effects of a Scutellaria baicalensis/Crataegus laevigata, magnesium and chromium supplement on stressed individuals: A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover trial.
    Randomised trialDodd F, Weishaupt R, Katumba PKM, Elcoate R, Wightman E, 2025 · PMID 41194549 · mixed

    Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial, 43 stressed but healthy adults (35 analysed), 15 days on a S. baicalensis root plus hawthorn plus magnesium/chromium supplement. Total sleep time showed a significant Treatment x Day interaction (F(1, 91.68) = 4.52, p = 0.036), longer on supplement than placebo at day 15 (452.16 vs 425.18 minutes, p = 0.037). But overall self-rated sleep quality was significantly WORSE on the supplement than placebo (6.00 vs 5.19 on a scale where higher indicates poorer sleep; F(1, 37.96) = 5.26, p = 0.027), as was psychological tension in relation to sleep (F(1, 44.27) = 4.66, p = 0.036). Multi-ingredient formula, so no effect is attributable to skullcap alone; the paper also notes that for S. baicalensis 'no randomised intervention trials in humans have observed outcomes here' for sleep. An author is employed by the supplement manufacturer.

  3. The Effects of Combined Scutellaria and Saffron Supplementation on Mood Regulation in Participants with Mild-to-Moderate Depressive Symptoms: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Study.
    Randomised trialDormal V, Suchareau M, Copine S, Simar L, Deldicque L, 2025 · PMID 40077679 · mixed

    Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 180 participants, S. baicalensis extract 20 mg/day standardised to 90.04% baicalin (18.08 mg baicalin) for 6 weeks. The primary outcome, Beck Depression Inventory, showed a session effect (p<0.001) but no significant session-by-group interaction, with scores falling in every group including placebo and not differing between groups. The exploratory improvement on the three HAMD sleep-quality items occurred only in the saffron arm (-0.15 +/- 0.45, p=0.033), not the scutellaria arm. Scutellaria did outperform placebo on WHO-5 well-being between weeks 3 and 6 (p=0.016) and reduced HAMD within-arm (p=0.044) where placebo did not. Sleep was a secondary, exploratory endpoint; one author is affiliated with the extract supplier.

  4. American Skullcap (Scutellaria lateriflora): a randomised, double-blind placebo-controlled crossover study of its effects on mood in healthy volunteers.
    Randomised trialBrock C, Whitehouse J, Tewfik I, Towell T, 2013 · PMID 23878109 · mixed

    S. lateriflora 350 mg three times daily for 2 weeks in 43 healthy volunteers: no significant difference from placebo on the Beck Anxiety Inventory (p=0.191), with a significant group effect (p=0.049) indicating carryover; Profile of Mood States Total Mood Disturbance fell significantly from pre-test with skullcap (p<0.001) but not placebo (p=0.072), a within-group rather than between-group result, in a population where 81% were mildly anxious or less. No sleep outcome was measured.

  5. Herbal medicine for insomnia: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisLeach MJ, Page AT, 2015 · PMID 25644982 · null

    Across 14 RCTs and 1602 insomnia participants, only valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling had monopreparation RCT evidence; no Scutellaria species appeared, and no herbal medicine differed significantly from placebo on any of thirteen efficacy measures. Establishes that the skullcap insomnia evidence base was empty as of 2015 and rests entirely on a single 2025 trial.

  6. Herbal hepatoxicity from Chinese skullcap: A case report.
    Journal articleYang L, Aronsohn A, Hart J, Jensen D, 2012 · PMID 22855699 · negative

    An elderly woman developed significant cholestasis and hepatitis on a supplement containing Chinese skullcap (S. baicalensis) and black catechu alongside glucosamine and chondroitin; liver tests improved on withdrawal, injury recurred when she unknowingly resumed the product, and liver biopsy was consistent with acute drug-induced liver injury. Multi-ingredient exposure, so attribution to skullcap alone is not definitive, but the authors note Chinese skullcap has been implicated as a possible hepatotoxic agent elsewhere in the literature.

  7. Herbal medicine for depression, anxiety and insomnia: a review of psychopharmacology and clinical evidence.
    Systematic reviewSarris J, Panossian A, Schweitzer I, Stough C, Scholey A, 2011 · PMID 21601431 · mixed

    Systematic review locating 66 controlled studies across eleven phytomedicines: Scutellaria lateriflora was classed among herbs with only preliminary positive evidence of anxiolytic activity, with the authors cautioning that many of these studies have not been replicated. High-level evidence was reserved for Hypericum perforatum and Piper methysticum, not skullcap.
