Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

Strong evidence · for depression & low mood

For depression & low mood

CBT is a structured talking therapy that works on the loop between what you think, what you do, and how you feel. It is the most heavily researched therapy for depression there is, and across hundreds of trials it helps more than no treatment or routine care, with benefits still visible six to twelve months later. Two honest caveats: a lot of studies that found nothing were never published and many of the rest were not tightly run, so the real effect is meaningfully smaller than the headline numbers suggest - and CBT does not clearly beat other well-delivered talking therapies.

Safety

Generally well tolerated, with no consistent differences in acceptability (all-cause dropout) between psychotherapies and control conditions across 331 trials. Harms are nonetheless under-reported in this literature. In the 440-participant COBRA trial, 15 depression-related serious adverse events occurred (12 in the CBT arm, 3 in the behavioural activation arm), consisting of overdoses and self-harm judged related to the depression itself rather than to treatment; this reflects the ongoing risk carried by people in active depression treatment and the need for monitoring, not evidence that therapy caused the events. Working through distressing material can temporarily increase distress. CBT is not a substitute for emergency care during a suicidal crisis.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications. Note who the trials excluded: COBRA and comparable trials excluded people who were acutely suicidal or had attempted suicide within the previous 2 months, people with alcohol or drug dependence, people with cognitive impairment, and people with bipolar disorder, psychosis, or psychotic symptoms. Findings therefore do not transfer to those groups without clinical judgement. In bipolar depression, psychological therapy is used alongside mood-stabilising treatment, never as a replacement for it, and the unipolar evidence summarised here does not establish benefit in bipolar illness.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Combined CBT plus antidepressant medication outperformed pharmacotherapy alone at short term (g=0.51, 95% CI 0.19-0.84) and long term (g=0.32, 95% CI 0.09-0.55), but was not more effective than CBT alone at either time point, so combination is a defensible option rather than a requirement. Decisions to stop or change antidepressants should be made with the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

The largest synthesis of a single psychotherapy for any mental disorder pooled 409 randomised trials (518 comparisons, 52,702 patients) and found a moderate-to-large effect for CBT versus control conditions such as care-as-usual and waiting list (g=0.79, 95% CI 0.70-0.89), which remained similar in sensitivity analyses and was still significant at 6-12 month follow-up. CBT's advantage over other psychotherapies was small and unstable (g=0.06, 95% CI 0-0.12) and became non-significant in most sensitivity analyses; a separate 331-trial network meta-analysis (34,285 patients) likewise found no significant differences between the major psychotherapies except that non-directive supportive counselling performed worse. Efficacy is not confined to face-to-face delivery, with CBT effective as unguided self-help (g=0.45, 95% CI 0.31-0.60) and in institutional settings (g=0.65, 95% CI 0.21-1.08). The published effect is measurably inflated. Among US NIH-funded trials of psychological treatment for major depression - the literature CBT dominates - 13 of 55 funded grants that began trials (23.6%) never produced a publication, and adding the recovered unpublished data reduced the effect versus control from g=0.52 (95% CI 0.37-0.68, k=20) to g=0.39 (95% CI 0.08-0.70), a 25% drop. A broader bias-adjustment analysis of psychotherapy for adult depression found an unadjusted effect of g=0.70 with only 23% of studies at low risk of bias; after adjusting for risk of bias, publication bias, and exclusion of waiting-list controls, the effect across all therapy types fell to g=0.31 - small, but above the suggested minimal important difference, and notably CBT was not among the therapies (problem-solving, interpersonal, behavioural activation) that the authors said lacked sufficient low-risk evidence. A meta-regression of 70 trials from 1977-2014 further reported that CBT effect sizes have declined steadily since the therapy's introduction on self-report (p<.001), clinician rating (p<.01), and remission rates (p<.01) - a trend the 409-trial analysis did not find when comparing publication-year categories, leaving the trajectory of CBT's effect genuinely contested rather than settled.

Doses studied: Trials most commonly used 8-20 weekly individual or group sessions. The evidence base also covers guided and unguided self-help and internet-delivered formats; unguided self-help CBT retained a smaller but significant effect (g=0.45, 95% CI 0.31-0.60).

Why this grade: Supported by the largest meta-analysis ever conducted on a single psychotherapy for a mental disorder (409 trials, 52,702 patients), with consistent moderate-to-large benefit versus control conditions that survives sensitivity analyses, persists at 6-12 month follow-up, and is reflected in first-line guideline recommendations. Graded strong despite three documented problems that the entry states rather than hides: unpublished trials inflate the pooled psychotherapy effect by roughly 25%; a full bias adjustment (risk of bias, publication bias, and removal of waitlist controls) drops the pooled psychotherapy effect from g=0.70 to g=0.31, with only 23% of trials at low risk of bias; and CBT's superiority over other psychotherapies is small and does not survive most sensitivity analyses. Strong is retained rather than downgraded because CBT specifically was not among the therapies identified as having too few low-risk trials to establish efficacy, and because the bias-adjusted effect remains above the threshold suggested as a minimal important difference. The evidence supports CBT as an effective psychotherapy, not as the uniquely best one.

Sources (6)
  1. Cognitive behavior therapy vs. control conditions, other psychotherapies, pharmacotherapies and combined treatment for depression: a comprehensive meta-analysis including 409 trials with 52,702 patients.
    Cuijpers, Miguel, Harrer, et al., 2023 · PMID 36640411 · positive

    Across 409 trials and 52,702 patients, CBT produced g=0.79 (95% CI 0.70-0.89) versus care-as-usual and waitlist controls, still significant at 6-12 month follow-up. CBT exceeded other psychotherapies by only g=0.06 (95% CI 0-0.12), non-significant in most sensitivity analyses. Combined treatment beat pharmacotherapy alone short term (g=0.51) and long term (g=0.32) but did not beat CBT alone. No reduction in effect size by publication year (<2001 vs 2001-2010 vs >2011).

  2. Does Publication Bias Inflate the Apparent Efficacy of Psychological Treatment for Major Depressive Disorder? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of US National Institutes of Health-Funded Trials.
    Driessen, Hollon, Bockting, Cuijpers & Turner, 2015 · PMID 26422604 · mixed

    Of 55 NIH-funded grants that began trials of psychological treatment for major depression, 13 (23.6%) produced no publication. Adding the unpublished studies (g=0.20, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.51, k=6) to published ones (g=0.52, 95% CI 0.37-0.68, k=20) cut the pooled effect versus control to g=0.39 (95% CI 0.08-0.70), a 25% reduction. Authors note this may still overestimate the true effect because outcome reporting bias could not be quantified.

  3. Was Eysenck right after all? A reassessment of the effects of psychotherapy for adult depression.
    Cuijpers, Karyotaki, Reijnders & Ebert, 2018 · PMID 29486804 · mixed

    The unadjusted effect of psychotherapies versus control for adult depression was g=0.70 (g=0.63 restricted to Western countries), a number-needed-to-treat of 4.18, but only 23% of studies could be considered at low risk of bias. After adjusting for risk of bias, publication bias, and exclusion of waiting-list control groups, the effect across all therapy types dropped to g=0.31. Authors state unadjusted meta-analyses overestimate the effects considerably, and that for problem-solving therapy, interpersonal psychotherapy, and behavioural activation insufficient evidence of effectiveness is available because too few low-risk studies exist.

  4. Psychotherapies for depression: a network meta-analysis covering efficacy, acceptability and long-term outcomes of all main treatment types.
    Cuijpers, Quero, Noma, et al., 2021 · PMID 34002502 · mixed

    Network meta-analysis of 331 trials with 34,285 patients found all major psychotherapies more efficacious than care-as-usual and waitlist (SMDs -0.81 to -0.32), and all except non-directive supportive counselling and psychodynamic therapy more efficacious than pill placebo. Individual psychotherapies did not differ significantly from each other except non-directive supportive counselling, which was less efficacious than all others. Results held when restricted to low-risk-of-bias studies; no consistent acceptability differences.

  5. The effects of cognitive behavioral therapy as an anti-depressive treatment is falling: A meta-analysis.
    Johnsen & Friborg, 2015 · PMID 25961373 · mixed

    Across 70 trials from 1977-2014, average effect was g=1.58 on the BDI (95% CI 1.43-1.74) and 1.69 on the HRSD (95% CI 1.48-1.89), but metaregression showed effects declined linearly and steadily by publication year on self-report (p<.001), clinician rating (p<.01), and remission (p<.01), in both pre/post (p<.01) and controlled trial designs (p=.02). Experienced psychologists achieved better results (g=1.55) than student therapists (g=0.98).

  6. Initial treatment choices to achieve sustained response in major depression: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Furukawa, Shinohara, Sahker, et al., 2021 · PMID 34505365 · positive

    Across 81 RCTs with 13,722 participants followed a mean of 42.2 weeks, psychotherapy kept patients well more often than protocolised pharmacotherapy whether continued into maintenance (OR 1.53, 95% CI 1.00-2.35) or followed by discretionary treatment (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.13-2.44). Against a 29% sustained-response rate on standard care, this equals risk differences of 12-16 percentage points.
