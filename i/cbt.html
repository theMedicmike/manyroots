Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)

Strong evidence · for anxiety & stress Strong evidence · for depression & low mood Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Moderate evidence · for fatigue & low energy Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

CBT is a structured, skills-based talk therapy where you learn to notice and change the thought and behavior patterns that keep worry going. Of all the non-drug options for anxiety it has the deepest research behind it, and in the largest head-to-head trial about 7 in 10 people responded to it. Two honest caveats: many of the underlying studies are of low quality and compared CBT against a waiting list rather than against another real treatment, and the benefit shrinks somewhat past a year.

Safety

CBT is drug-free and generally safe. The most common downsides are temporary increases in distress when confronting feared thoughts or situations, the time and cost commitment, and dropout: in the PTSD trials of the placebo-controlled meta-analysis, dropout was higher with CBT (29.0%) than placebo (17.2%), though no dropout difference was found across the other anxiety disorders. Quality depends heavily on the therapist and on fidelity to the protocol, and effects were stronger in completer samples than in intent-to-treat samples — which is another way of saying that the people who finish do better than the average person who starts.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications. Not a substitute for urgent psychiatric care in people with active suicidal ideation or psychosis, where it should be delivered only as part of comprehensive clinical management.

Interactions: No drug interactions. CBT is commonly combined with medication; decisions about combined treatment should be made with the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

Evidence for CBT in generalized anxiety and anxiety-related disorders is the deepest of any behavioral intervention, though not as clean as its reputation suggests. A meta-analysis of 41 randomized placebo-controlled trials (N=2,843) found a moderate pooled effect on target-disorder symptoms (Hedges' g = 0.56) and a response odds ratio of 2.97 versus placebo, with large effect sizes for GAD, OCD and acute stress disorder. In a 3-arm randomized trial of 226 adults with GAD, twelve weeks of CBT produced a 70.8% response rate versus 33.0% for an active stress-education control (OR 5.00, 95% CI 2.12-11.82; number needed to treat 2.62). A meta-analysis of 41 GAD psychotherapy studies (2,132 patients) found a large pooled effect across 38 comparisons from 28 studies (g = 0.84, 95% CI 0.71-0.97) and on depression (g = 0.71), but reported indications of publication bias and too few comparative trials to judge CBT against other therapies or medication. The countervailing evidence is substantial. Across 69 randomized trials (4,118 outpatients) that were mainly of low quality, effects for GAD versus control ran g = 0.07-0.40 through 12 months and only g = 0.22 (k=10) at 12 or more months. In older adults, CBT beat waitlist and treatment-as-usual on worry but showed only a small, non-significant advantage over active control conditions at end of treatment, with equivalent outcomes at follow-up. And a meta-analysis of 12 GAD trials (N=531) rated 8 of 12 as high risk of bias, judged the overall evidence 'moderate' and not yet robust, and found that psychological placebo alone reduced symptoms significantly more than placement on a waiting list — meaning waitlist-controlled effect sizes overstate what CBT itself contributes.

Doses studied: In the largest GAD trial cited here, CBT was delivered as twelve weekly 120-minute group sessions with 20 minutes of daily homework over 12 weeks. Formats vary widely across the meta-analysed trials; one placebo-controlled synthesis found larger effects for individual than group CBT in social anxiety disorder and PTSD studies, and larger effects in completer than intent-to-treat samples.

Why this grade: Multiple independent meta-analyses of randomized trials — including 41 placebo-controlled trials — show consistent benefit in generalized anxiety and related disorders, CBT beat an active stress-education control in a well-powered 3-arm GAD trial, and CBT is guideline first-line care. Graded strong despite substantial caveats that are stated rather than buried: the underlying trials are mainly rated low quality, most comparisons used waiting lists rather than active controls (and psychological placebo alone outperforms waitlist), the advantage over active comparators is small and non-significant in older adults, and the effect at 12-plus months is small.

For depression & low mood

CBT is a structured talking therapy that works on the loop between what you think, what you do, and how you feel. It is the most heavily researched therapy for depression there is, and across hundreds of trials it helps more than no treatment or routine care, with benefits still visible six to twelve months later. Two honest caveats: a lot of studies that found nothing were never published and many of the rest were not tightly run, so the real effect is meaningfully smaller than the headline numbers suggest - and CBT does not clearly beat other well-delivered talking therapies.

Safety

Generally well tolerated, with no consistent differences in acceptability (all-cause dropout) between psychotherapies and control conditions across 331 trials. Harms are nonetheless under-reported in this literature. In the 440-participant COBRA trial, 15 depression-related serious adverse events occurred (12 in the CBT arm, 3 in the behavioural activation arm), consisting of overdoses and self-harm judged related to the depression itself rather than to treatment; this reflects the ongoing risk carried by people in active depression treatment and the need for monitoring, not evidence that therapy caused the events. Working through distressing material can temporarily increase distress. CBT is not a substitute for emergency care during a suicidal crisis.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications. Note who the trials excluded: COBRA and comparable trials excluded people who were acutely suicidal or had attempted suicide within the previous 2 months, people with alcohol or drug dependence, people with cognitive impairment, and people with bipolar disorder, psychosis, or psychotic symptoms. Findings therefore do not transfer to those groups without clinical judgement. In bipolar depression, psychological therapy is used alongside mood-stabilising treatment, never as a replacement for it, and the unipolar evidence summarised here does not establish benefit in bipolar illness.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Combined CBT plus antidepressant medication outperformed pharmacotherapy alone at short term (g=0.51, 95% CI 0.19-0.84) and long term (g=0.32, 95% CI 0.09-0.55), but was not more effective than CBT alone at either time point, so combination is a defensible option rather than a requirement. Decisions to stop or change antidepressants should be made with the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

The largest synthesis of a single psychotherapy for any mental disorder pooled 409 randomised trials (518 comparisons, 52,702 patients) and found a moderate-to-large effect for CBT versus control conditions such as care-as-usual and waiting list (g=0.79, 95% CI 0.70-0.89), which remained similar in sensitivity analyses and was still significant at 6-12 month follow-up. CBT's advantage over other psychotherapies was small and unstable (g=0.06, 95% CI 0-0.12) and became non-significant in most sensitivity analyses; a separate 331-trial network meta-analysis (34,285 patients) likewise found no significant differences between the major psychotherapies except that non-directive supportive counselling performed worse. Efficacy is not confined to face-to-face delivery, with CBT effective as unguided self-help (g=0.45, 95% CI 0.31-0.60) and in institutional settings (g=0.65, 95% CI 0.21-1.08). The published effect is measurably inflated. Among US NIH-funded trials of psychological treatment for major depression - the literature CBT dominates - 13 of 55 funded grants that began trials (23.6%) never produced a publication, and adding the recovered unpublished data reduced the effect versus control from g=0.52 (95% CI 0.37-0.68, k=20) to g=0.39 (95% CI 0.08-0.70), a 25% drop. A broader bias-adjustment analysis of psychotherapy for adult depression found an unadjusted effect of g=0.70 with only 23% of studies at low risk of bias; after adjusting for risk of bias, publication bias, and exclusion of waiting-list controls, the effect across all therapy types fell to g=0.31 - small, but above the suggested minimal important difference, and notably CBT was not among the therapies (problem-solving, interpersonal, behavioural activation) that the authors said lacked sufficient low-risk evidence. A meta-regression of 70 trials from 1977-2014 further reported that CBT effect sizes have declined steadily since the therapy's introduction on self-report (p<.001), clinician rating (p<.01), and remission rates (p<.01) - a trend the 409-trial analysis did not find when comparing publication-year categories, leaving the trajectory of CBT's effect genuinely contested rather than settled.

Doses studied: Trials most commonly used 8-20 weekly individual or group sessions. The evidence base also covers guided and unguided self-help and internet-delivered formats; unguided self-help CBT retained a smaller but significant effect (g=0.45, 95% CI 0.31-0.60).

Why this grade: Supported by the largest meta-analysis ever conducted on a single psychotherapy for a mental disorder (409 trials, 52,702 patients), with consistent moderate-to-large benefit versus control conditions that survives sensitivity analyses, persists at 6-12 month follow-up, and is reflected in first-line guideline recommendations. Graded strong despite three documented problems that the entry states rather than hides: unpublished trials inflate the pooled psychotherapy effect by roughly 25%; a full bias adjustment (risk of bias, publication bias, and removal of waitlist controls) drops the pooled psychotherapy effect from g=0.70 to g=0.31, with only 23% of trials at low risk of bias; and CBT's superiority over other psychotherapies is small and does not survive most sensitivity analyses. Strong is retained rather than downgraded because CBT specifically was not among the therapies identified as having too few low-risk trials to establish efficacy, and because the bias-adjusted effect remains above the threshold suggested as a minimal important difference. The evidence supports CBT as an effective psychotherapy, not as the uniquely best one.

For chronic pain

Pain-focused talk therapy does not tell you the pain is not real. It teaches practical skills for handling the fear, avoidance and low mood that long-term pain builds up, so pain interferes less with your life. Across many trials the average benefit is genuine but small, and it works best when paired with a structured exercise or physiotherapy programme rather than used alone.

Safety

No serious harms have been established, but the honest position is that harms data are thin rather than reassuring: the Cochrane review found adverse events were inadequately recorded and/or reported across studies and rated the evidence on harms as very low quality for every comparison. The realistic costs are burden and access - weekly sessions over roughly two months plus practice between them, therapist availability and expense. Adherence is a real limit: in the largest back-pain trial, only 53.7% of participants attended 6 or more of the 8 sessions.

Who should be careful: Not a substitute for medical evaluation of a new, changing or red-flag pain problem. People with active suicidal ideation, psychosis, severe untreated depression, or a substance-use crisis need those addressed first or concurrently by a clinician equipped to manage them, not deferred into a pain-coping programme.

Interactions: No drug interactions. The framing matters clinically: presenting CBT as evidence that pain is 'psychological' is both inaccurate and counterproductive - it is skills training for living with real pain, and mis-framing it predicts dropout. It is not a reason to stop or reduce prescribed medication without clinician involvement.

What the evidence says

The 2020 Cochrane review of psychological therapies for chronic non-cancer pain (excluding headache) included 75 studies with 9401 participants at treatment end, most with fibromyalgia, chronic low back pain, rheumatoid arthritis or mixed chronic pain. CBT, with the largest evidence base (59 studies), showed very small benefit over active control for pain (SMD -0.09, 95% CI -0.17 to -0.01; 3235 participants; 23 studies), disability (SMD -0.12, 95% CI -0.20 to -0.04) and distress (SMD -0.09, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.00), and small benefit over treatment-as-usual for pain (SMD -0.22, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.10; 2572 participants; 29 studies), disability (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.45 to -0.19) and distress (SMD -0.34, 95% CI -0.44 to -0.24); effects were largely maintained at follow-up versus usual care but not versus active control. Behavioural therapy (8 studies, 647 participants) and acceptance and commitment therapy (5 studies, 443 participants) showed no evidence of difference from active control, on very low quality evidence. A 2022 network meta-analysis of 97 trials and 13,136 participants with chronic non-specific low back pain found CBT delivered with physiotherapy care produced clinically important improvement in physical function (SMD 1.01, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.44) and pain (SMD 0.92, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.42) immediately post-intervention, while only behavioural therapy plus physiotherapy held a clinically important pain effect to mid-term follow-up. In a 342-participant randomised trial, 57.7% of the CBT group achieved clinically meaningful improvement in function at 26 weeks versus 44.1% with usual care (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.69). Adverse events were inadequately recorded and/or reported across trials.

Doses studied: Formats vary across trials. In the largest chronic low back pain trial, CBT was delivered in eight weekly two-hour groups. In the chronic low back pain network meta-analysis, psychological interventions were most effective when delivered in conjunction with physiotherapy care (mainly structured exercise); behavioural therapy plus physiotherapy was the only combination whose pain effect remained clinically important to mid-term (6-12 month) follow-up, and no trial had investigated the long-term effectiveness of pain education plus physiotherapy care.

Why this grade: A 75-study, 9401-participant Cochrane review confirms real but small benefits: CBT's advantage over active control was SMD -0.09 for pain and over usual care SMD -0.22, with mostly moderate-to-low quality evidence. A 97-trial network meta-analysis concluded psychological interventions are most effective when delivered in conjunction with physiotherapy care, and every clinically important effect it reported came from such a combination. Closely related therapies (behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy) showed no clear benefit over active control on very low quality evidence, and ACP rates the underlying CBT evidence for chronic low back pain as low quality. Genuine but modest and delivery-dependent, so moderate rather than strong.

For fatigue & low energy

This is talking therapy aimed at the habits, thoughts and activity patterns that can keep exhaustion going, not at telling you the tiredness is imaginary. Several good trials show it helps people report less fatigue, especially after cancer treatment and after COVID-19. The honest caveats are that people know which treatment they got, the improvements are mostly in what people say rather than what they can do, and in two long trials the advantage had faded by a year because the comparison groups caught up. In ME/CFS its role is now limited to helping people cope rather than aiming at recovery.

Safety

CBT for fatigue carries low physical risk and adverse events in trials have been uncommon: PACE recorded serious adverse reactions in 3 of 161 CBT participants (2%), similar to the other arms (1-2%), and the long COVID trial recorded 8 adverse events during CBT versus 20 during care as usual, none serious. The more substantial risk is not medical but framing: when therapy is presented as though changed beliefs and behaviour will resolve the illness, people whose illness does not resolve can be left feeling blamed, and patient organisations have consistently raised this. NICE (2021) now states CBT should be offered in ME/CFS only as a supportive intervention, not as a treatment aimed at recovery.

Who should be careful: CBT is not a substitute for finding a medical cause of fatigue; it should follow, not replace, appropriate workup for anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, diabetes, medication effects and other treatable causes. Anyone with active suicidal thinking, untreated severe depression, psychosis or a substance use disorder needs those addressed first by appropriate mental health care rather than a fatigue-focused protocol.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Where CBT is delivered alongside a graded activity component, the cautions that apply to graded exercise apply here too, particularly for anyone with post-exertional malaise.

What the evidence says

A Cochrane review of 15 trials (1,043 participants with chronic fatigue syndrome) found CBT reduced fatigue at post-treatment versus usual care (SMD -0.39, 95% CI -0.60 to -0.19; 6 studies, 373 participants), with clinical response in 40% of CBT participants versus 26% on usual care, though findings at follow-up were inconsistent (Price 2008). In the PACE trial (n=641), CBT lowered fatigue scores by 3.4 points (95% CI 1.8-5.0) versus specialist medical care alone at 52 weeks (White 2011), but that advantage did not hold: at a median of 31 months there was little evidence of any difference between the randomised groups, the pacing and specialist-care groups having improved to a similar level, with 44% of responders having taken additional treatment after the trial (Sharpe 2015). An independent reanalysis using the originally protocol-specified primary outcome found CBT and graded exercise did not significantly outperform the control group after correcting for the trial's own pre-specified comparisons, that recovery rates were low and not significantly different across groups, and that significant effects were almost entirely confined to self-report measures and did not endure beyond two years (Wilshire 2018). The same rise-then-converge pattern appeared independently in multiple sclerosis: CBT reduced fatigue by 6.7 CIS20r points at 16 weeks (95% CI -10.7 to -2.7) but the difference had disappeared by 52 weeks (0.5, 95% CI -3.6 to 4.4), with no clinically relevant effect on participation (van den Akker 2017). Evidence is more encouraging elsewhere: in severe fatigue 3-12 months after COVID-19, CBT reduced fatigue severity by 8.8 points versus care as usual (95% CI -11.9 to -5.8, Cohen's d 0.69, n=114), sustained at 6 months (Kuut 2023); and in severely fatigued disease-free cancer survivors, CBT produced a 13.3-point fatigue advantage over waiting list (95% CI 8.6-18.1), with clinically significant improvement in 54% versus 4% (Gielissen 2006). Waiting-list and usual-care comparators inflate apparent effects in unblinded trials, and this should temper how all of these numbers are read.

Doses studied: The MS trial delivered 12 individual sessions with a psychologist over 16 weeks against three MS-nurse consultations (van den Akker 2017). The long COVID trial provided CBT targeting perpetuating factors of fatigue over 17 weeks (Kuut 2023). The cancer-survivor trial delivered individual therapy with outcomes assessed at 6 months (Gielissen 2006). Trials in this literature typically run three to six months of individual sessions.

Why this grade: Multiple randomised trials and a Cochrane review show CBT reduces self-reported fatigue compared with usual care or waiting list across several populations, which rules out the lower grades. It falls short of strong because the trials are necessarily unblinded with self-reported primary outcomes; because the advantage disappears by long-term follow-up in two independent trials in different diseases (PACE in ME/CFS, TREFAMS-CBT in multiple sclerosis) as comparison groups catch up; because an independent reanalysis using the originally specified protocol found the effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons; and because guidance in ME/CFS has narrowed CBT to a supportive role rather than a treatment aimed at recovery.

    Across 15 studies with 1,043 participants, CBT versus usual care (6 studies, 373 participants) reduced fatigue at post-treatment with SMD -0.39 (95% CI -0.60 to -0.19), and 40% of CBT participants (4 studies, 371 participants) showed clinical response versus 26% on usual care; the review reports this as OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.29-0.76), a figure whose direction is the reverse of the response percentages and is easily misread. Findings at follow-up were inconsistent.

  2. Comparison of adaptive pacing therapy, cognitive behaviour therapy, graded exercise therapy, and specialist medical care for chronic fatigue syndrome (PACE): a randomised trial.
    Randomised trialWhite PD et al., 2011 · PMID 21334061 · positive

    In 641 participants, mean fatigue scores at 52 weeks were 3.4 points lower for CBT than specialist medical care alone (95% CI 1.8-5.0, p=0.0001) and physical function scores 7.1 points higher (95% CI 2.0-12.1, p=0.0068); serious adverse reactions occurred in 3 of 161 CBT participants (2%).

  3. Rehabilitative treatments for chronic fatigue syndrome: long-term follow-up from the PACE trial.
    Randomised trialSharpe M et al., 2015 · PMID 26521770 · null

    At a median 31 months after randomisation (481 of 641 participants responding), there was little evidence of differences in outcomes between the originally randomised treatment groups; participants originally assigned to adaptive pacing or specialist medical care alone had improved to a similar level as CBT and GET, though 210 (44%) had received additional treatment after the trial.

  4. Cognitive behavioral therapy positively affects fatigue in patients with multiple sclerosis: Results of a randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialvan den Akker LE et al., 2017 · PMID 28528567 · mixed

    In 91 patients with severe MS-related fatigue randomised to 12 individual CBT sessions over 16 weeks or three MS-nurse consultations, CBT reduced CIS20r fatigue by 6.7 points at 16 weeks (95% CI -10.7 to -2.7), but the between-group difference had disappeared by 52 weeks (0.5, 95% CI -3.6 to 4.4) and no clinically relevant effect on societal participation was found.

  5. Rethinking the treatment of chronic fatigue syndrome-a reanalysis and evaluation of findings from a recent major trial of graded exercise and CBT.
    Randomised trialWilshire CE et al., 2018 · PMID 29562932 · negative

    Using the originally protocol-specified primary outcome of overall improvement rates, CBT and graded exercise did not significantly outperform the control group after correcting for the number of comparisons specified in the trial protocol; recovery rates were consistently low and not significantly different across groups, and significant effects were almost entirely confined to self-report measures and did not endure beyond two years.

  6. Efficacy of Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy Targeting Severe Fatigue Following Coronavirus Disease 2019: Results of a Randomized Controlled Trial.
    Randomised trialKuut TA et al., 2023 · PMID 37155736 · positive

    In 114 mainly non-hospitalised, self-referred patients severely fatigued 3-12 months after COVID-19, 17 weeks of CBT reduced fatigue severity by 8.8 points versus care as usual across follow-up assessments (95% CI -11.9 to -5.8; Cohen's d 0.69), with the difference present at end of treatment (-9.3, 95% CI -13.3 to -5.3) and sustained at 6-month follow-up (-8.4, 95% CI -13.1 to -3.7); 8 adverse events were recorded during CBT versus 20 during care as usual, none serious.

  7. Effects of cognitive behavior therapy in severely fatigued disease-free cancer patients compared with patients waiting for cognitive behavior therapy: a randomized controlled trial.
    Randomised trialGielissen MFM et al., 2006 · PMID 17050873 · positive

    Among 98 analysed cancer survivors with somatically unexplained fatigue (112 randomised), CBT produced a 13.3-point greater reduction in fatigue severity than waiting list (95% CI 8.6-18.1) and clinically significant improvement in 54% versus 4% of participants at 6 months.

For gut & digestion

CBT for IBS teaches practical skills for handling the stress, worry and avoidance that get tangled up with gut symptoms, and it can be delivered face to face, by phone, or through a website or app. It has the strongest research record of any talking therapy for IBS, and in the largest trials people who did it reported meaningfully calmer guts a year or two later. The effect is real but moderate, self-guided online versions tend to fade sooner than therapist-supported ones, and it does not work for everyone.

Safety

CBT for IBS has a very low physical risk profile; the 558-patient ACTIB trial reported no serious adverse reactions to treatment and similar adverse event rates across all three arms, and the 24-month follow-up recorded 41 adverse events between 12 and 24 months with none related to treatment. The most common downsides are practical: time commitment, homework burden, and temporary increases in discomfort or anxiety when programmes include exposure elements that deliberately reduce avoidance of feared foods, situations or sensations. Therapist-supported delivery outperformed minimally supported web delivery on symptoms over the long term, and a meta-analysis of internet-CBT trials found no effect on anxiety or depression, so a fully self-guided app should not be assumed equivalent to a supported course. CBT is not a diagnostic test and must not delay assessment of alarm features such as unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, nocturnal symptoms or new symptoms after age 50.

Who should be careful: Not a substitute for medical evaluation of undiagnosed abdominal symptoms, and not appropriate as the sole approach where inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or malignancy has not been excluded. People with significant depression, active suicidality, severe untreated anxiety, psychosis or an active eating disorder need direct clinical mental-health care rather than a self-guided digital IBS programme; note also that trial evidence found no advantage of CBT over simple education in patients with high trait anxiety, so that group in particular should not be routed to a self-help app in place of proper assessment. Framing IBS treatment as psychological can also feel dismissive to people whose symptoms have been minimised in the past; that is a real reason some people decline, not a failure on their part.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. CBT has been studied as an addition to usual medical care and is compatible with dietary approaches and IBS medications. Do not stop prescribed medication because a behavioural programme is helping without discussing it with the prescriber.

What the evidence says

A 2025 network meta-analysis of 67 RCTs (7,441 participants) found several CBT formats efficacious against waiting-list control for global IBS symptoms: minimal-contact CBT (RR of symptoms not improving 0.55, 95% CI 0.39-0.76; 2 RCTs, 511 patients; P score 0.78, the highest of the therapies with the largest evidence base), face-to-face CBT (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.53-0.80; 9 trials, 1,150 patients) and internet-based minimal-contact CBT (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.61-0.96; 5 RCTs, 705 patients), though the authors report publication bias, no RCT at low risk of bias, and low or very low certainty throughout. In the 558-patient ACTIB trial the IBS Symptom Severity Score at 12 months was 61.6 points lower with therapist telephone CBT (95% CI 33.8-89.5, p<0.001) and 35.2 points lower with web CBT (95% CI 12.6-57.8, p=0.002) than treatment as usual (205.6); at 24 months the telephone arm retained a 40.5-point advantage (95% CI 15.0-66.0, p=0.002) but the web arm's 12.9-point difference was no longer significant (95% CI -12.9 to 38.8, p=0.33), although both arms kept a significant functional benefit on the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (3.1 points, p<0.001 and 1.9 points, p=0.036) and follow-up was available for only 58% of participants. Against an active control, the 436-patient IBS Outcome Study found moderate-to-substantial symptom improvement in 61.0% of patients receiving 4-session home-based CBT versus 43.5% receiving IBS education by patient rating (55.7% vs 40.4% by blinded gastroenterologist rating) two weeks after treatment, and 58.4% versus 44.8% by gastroenterologist rating at 6 months (P=.05), with the 4-session version at least equivalent to a 10-session version. A moderator analysis of that trial found the CBT advantage was confined to patients with low or typical trait anxiety (71.3% vs 34.9% and 66.0% vs 47.1%) and disappeared in those with high trait anxiety (60.6% vs 59.2%, non-significant). Digital delivery is not uniformly effective: a meta-analysis of 9 internet-CBT RCTs found medium-to-large benefits on symptom severity (SMD -0.633, 95% CI -0.861 to -0.430) and quality of life (SMD 0.582, 95% CI 0.396-0.769) but NO significant difference in anxiety or depression versus controls, and a cluster RCT of 160 Taiwanese female nursing students found short internet-CBT reduced anxiety and depression slightly but had no effect on IBS symptoms. Trials enrolled mixed IBS subtypes and did not generally report subtype-stratified efficacy, so there is no reliable evidence of differential benefit in IBS-D versus IBS-C.

Doses studied: Face-to-face IBS-specific CBT has used 10 weekly 60-minute sessions covering brain-gut interactions, symptom self-monitoring, muscle relaxation, worry control, flexible problem solving and relapse prevention. A minimal-contact version delivering the same content in 4 sessions plus home-study materials was formally equivalent to the 10-session version. The ACTIB trial delivered therapist-led telephone CBT and a web-based programme with minimal therapist support, both alongside treatment as usual. Across 9 internet-delivered CBT trials pooled in a 2022 meta-analysis, programmes ran 5 to 13 sessions over 5 to 10 weeks.

Why this grade: IBS-specific CBT has the largest and most consistent trial base of any behavioural therapy for IBS: network meta-analyses place CBT formats among the best-supported options, and two large, well-conducted RCTs (n=558 and n=436) show benefit against treatment as usual and against an active education control, sustained to 12-24 months. Held at moderate rather than strong because effect sizes against active controls are modest (roughly a 15 percentage-point advantage over education); because the top-ranking format's estimate rests on only 2 RCTs and 511 patients; because the network meta-analysts state that no RCT was at low risk of bias, that publication bias was present, and that certainty was low or very low for every comparison; because the web-delivered arm's SYMPTOM advantage lost statistical significance by 24 months (p=0.33), even though a smaller functional benefit persisted (p=0.036); because a moderator analysis found no CBT advantage over simple education in patients with high trait anxiety; because a meta-analysis of 9 internet-CBT trials found no significant effect on anxiety or depression versus controls; and because at least one internet-CBT trial found no effect on IBS symptoms at all.

  2. Assessing telephone-delivered cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) and web-delivered CBT versus treatment as usual in irritable bowel syndrome (ACTIB): a multicentre randomised trial.

    Three-arm RCT, 558 adults with refractory IBS from 74 general practices and 3 gastroenterology centres.   3. Cognitive behavioural therapy for irritable bowel syndrome: 24-month follow-up of participants in the ACTIB randomised trial.
    Randomised trialEveritt et al., 2019 (24-month follow-up) · PMID 31492643 · mixed

    Naturalistic 24-month follow-up of 323 of 558 ACTIB participants (58%). Telephone CBT retained a 40.5-point IBS-SSS advantage over treatment as usual (95% CI 15.0-66.0, p=0.002), but the web CBT advantage of 12.9 points was NO LONGER statistically significant (95% CI -12.9 to 38.8, p=0.33). Both arms did retain a significant functional benefit on the Work and Social Adjustment Scale (telephone 3.1 points, p<0.001; web 1.9 points, p=0.036). A clinically significant change of at least 50 IBS-SSS points occurred in 71% (telephone CBT), 63% (web CBT) and 46% (usual care).     Randomised trialLackner et al., 2018 · PMID 29702118 · positive

    Meta-analysisKim et al., 2022 · PMID 35687389 · mixed

Medium-to-large effects were found on symptom severity (SMD -0.633, 95% CI -0.861 to -0.430) and quality of life (SMD 0.582, 95% CI 0.396-0.769), with symptom effects persisting at short-term (SMD -0.391) and long-term follow-up (SMD -0.357). The authors state that studies remain insufficient and that more high-quality trials are required.
