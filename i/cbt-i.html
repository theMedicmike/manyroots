CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia)

Strong evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

CBT-I is a structured, skills-based program — usually a handful of sessions with a therapist or an app — that retrains the habits and worried thinking that keep insomnia going. It is the best-proven treatment for chronic insomnia in adults and is what sleep-medicine guidelines say to try first, before sleeping pills. The first few weeks can be tiring because one component temporarily limits time in bed, but improvements tend to last after treatment ends.

Safety

The 20-trial meta-analysis by Trauer et al. reported no adverse outcomes, and long-term tolerability is a key advantage over hypnotic medication. The main documented harm is transient: measurably reduced objective sleep time and impaired vigilance during acute sleep restriction (Kyle 2014), resolving by 3 months.

Who should be careful: The sleep-restriction component acutely increases daytime sleepiness and impairs vigilance during the first weeks — use caution, and clinical supervision, in people who drive professionally, operate machinery, or do safety-critical work, and in conditions where sleep loss is destabilizing (e.g., bipolar disorder, seizure disorders). Untreated sleep apnea or other sleep disorders should be evaluated first.

Interactions: No drug interactions; CBT-I is often used alongside gradual, clinician-supervised tapering of sleep medication rather than abrupt changes.

What the evidence says

Evidence for CBT-I in chronic insomnia is the strongest of any intervention in this set. A meta-analysis of 20 RCTs (n=1,162) found CBT-I improved sleep onset latency by 19.0 minutes (95% CI 14.1-23.9), wake after sleep onset by 26.0 minutes, and sleep efficiency by 9.9%, with gains apparently sustained; total sleep time change (7.6 min) was not statistically significant. A 241-trial component network meta-analysis (n=31,452) estimated a number needed to treat of 3.0 for remission with the most effective package versus psychoeducation, identifying cognitive restructuring, sleep restriction, and stimulus control as key components. The 2021 AASM clinical practice guideline issues a STRONG recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I. Countervailing evidence: a meta-analysis of 15 studies found no reliable improvement on polysomnography-defined sleep parameters, and actigraphy showed reduced total sleep time (Hedges' g = -0.51), so benefits are predominantly in the subjective domain. Acute sleep restriction therapy also measurably reduces objective sleep time and impairs vigilance during the first weeks of treatment.

Doses studied: Typically 4-8 sessions delivered in person, in groups, or digitally, combining at least three of: cognitive therapy, stimulus control, sleep restriction, sleep hygiene, and relaxation; in-person therapist-led delivery performed best in the component network meta-analysis.

Why this grade: Multiple large meta-analyses (including a 241-trial network meta-analysis) show consistent benefit on self-reported sleep, and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine gives multicomponent CBT-I its only STRONG recommendation among behavioral insomnia treatments. Graded strong despite an honest caveat: benefits are much clearer on subjective than on objective (polysomnography/actigraphy) sleep measures.

Sources (5)
  1. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.
    Trauer et al., 2015 (Annals of Internal Medicine) · PMID 26054060 · positive

    Across 20 RCTs (1,162 adults with chronic insomnia), CBT-I improved sleep onset latency by 19.03 min (95% CI 14.12-23.93), wake after sleep onset by 26.00 min (CI 15.48-36.52), and sleep efficiency by 9.91% (CI 8.09-11.73); total sleep time change was 7.61 min (CI -0.51 to 15.74, not significant). No adverse outcomes were reported and gains appeared sustained.

  2. Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline.
    Edinger et al., 2021 (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine) · PMID 33164742 · positive

    GRADE-based clinical practice guideline recommends multicomponent CBT-I for chronic insomnia disorder in adults with STRONG strength — the only strong recommendation among all behavioral/psychological insomnia treatments assessed.

  3. Components and Delivery Formats of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia in Adults: A Systematic Review and Component Network Meta-Analysis.
    Furukawa et al., 2024 (JAMA Psychiatry) · PMID 38231522 · positive

    In 241 trials (31,452 participants), the most efficacious CBT-I package (cognitive restructuring, third-wave components, sleep restriction, stimulus control, delivered in person) increased remission by a risk difference of 0.33 (95% CI 0.23-0.43) versus in-person psychoeducation — number needed to treat 3.0.

  4. The impact of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia on objective sleep parameters: A meta-analysis and systematic review.
    Mitchell et al., 2019 (Sleep Medicine Reviews) · PMID 31377503 · null

    Across 15 studies, meta-analysis found no evidence that CBT-I reliably improves polysomnography-defined sleep parameters; actigraphy showed a small reduction in sleep onset latency (g = -0.28) but a moderate REDUCTION in total sleep time (g = -0.51). Benefits were robust only on subjective diary measures (g 0.50-0.79).

  5. Sleep restriction therapy for insomnia is associated with reduced objective total sleep time, increased daytime somnolence, and objectively impaired vigilance: implications for the clinical management of insomnia disorder.
    Kyle et al., 2014 (Sleep) · PMID 24497651 · negative

    During acute sleep restriction therapy (a core CBT-I component), 16 patients showed PSG total sleep time reduced by 91 min (night 1), 78 min (night 8), and 69 min (night 22) versus baseline (p<0.001), with significantly increased psychomotor vigilance lapses and Epworth sleepiness in weeks 1-3; all measures returned to baseline by 3 months.
