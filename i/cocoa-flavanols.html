Foods

Cocoa and dark chocolate flavanols

Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Cocoa contains flavanols that help blood vessels relax, and pooling 35 studies shows they do lower blood pressure - but only by about 2 points on the top number overall, and around 4 points in people who already have high blood pressure. In people with normal blood pressure the effect essentially disappears, the studies disagree with one another, and the results looked better in trials whose authors worked for the industry. The largest long-term trial, in more than 21,000 older adults, did not find fewer heart attacks and strokes overall, so this is a small nudge rather than a treatment.

Safety

Cocoa products were highly tolerable in the Cochrane review, with gastrointestinal complaints and nausea reported by 1% of participants on cocoa versus 0.4% on control, and COSMOS reported no safety concerns with 500 mg/day cocoa extract over a median 3.6 years. The practical harm comes from the delivery vehicle rather than the flavanols: 50 g/day of dark chocolate adds substantial calories, sugar and saturated fat, and in the prehypertension trial half of participants found that amount hard to eat daily and 20% rejected it as a long-term option. Cocoa also contains caffeine and theobromine, which can disturb sleep or provoke palpitations and reflux in sensitive people. Cocoa is one of the crops known to take up cadmium from soil, which is a general food-safety reason to avoid very large daily intakes of high-cocoa products, particularly for children.

Who should be careful: Delivered as chocolate, this is not an appropriate blood-pressure strategy for anyone who needs to limit calories, sugar or saturated fat, including many people with diabetes or obesity. Avoid with caffeine sensitivity, chocolate-triggered migraine, or reflux aggravated by chocolate. Chocolate and cocoa supplements are toxic to dogs and should be kept away from pets.

Interactions: Caffeine and theobromine in cocoa can add to other stimulants and to drugs whose effects or metabolism involve caffeine. Any blood-pressure-lowering effect is additive with antihypertensive medication. High-dose cocoa extract supplements have been studied for effects on platelet function, so they are worth mentioning to a clinician before use alongside anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs.

What the evidence says

The 2017 Cochrane review pooled 35 trials (40 treatment comparisons, 1,804 mainly healthy adults, 2-18 weeks, mean 9 weeks) and found flavanol-rich cocoa lowered systolic BP by 1.76 mmHg (95% CI -3.09 to -0.43, P=0.009) and diastolic by 1.76 mmHg (95% CI -2.57 to -0.94), rating the evidence moderate quality after downgrading from high for unexplained heterogeneity. The effect concentrated in people with raised pressure: systolic fell about 4 mmHg in the 9 hypertensive comparisons (401 participants) but showed no significant difference in the 23 normotensive comparisons (1,063 participants), with the test for subgroup difference of borderline significance (P=0.08). Two other meta-analyses reproduce that pattern - 15 trial arms from 13 studies gave pooled systolic -3.2 mmHg and diastolic -2.0 mmHg, significant only in hypertensive or prehypertensive subgroups (systolic -5.0 mmHg) and not in normotensives (systolic -1.6 mmHg, P=0.17); and a 2020 meta-analysis in middle-aged and elderly subjects (13 studies, 758 participants) gave systolic -2.77 mmHg (95% CI -5.28 to -0.27, I2=89%) while stating it could not conclude any benefit in normotensive or elevated-blood-pressure participants. Reporting bias is documented by the Cochrane reviewers themselves: a sensitivity analysis that removed trials with authors employed by the sponsoring industry, leaving 33 trials and 1,482 participants, produced a smaller effect size. A randomized trial giving 50 g/day of 70% dark chocolate (750 mg polyphenols) to 36 prehypertensive adults found no significant blood pressure change within or between groups over 6 months, with half of participants finding the dose hard to sustain. The largest and longest test, COSMOS (21,442 older adults, 500 mg cocoa flavanols/day, median 3.6 years), missed its primary endpoint of total cardiovascular events (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78-1.02, P=0.11), although cardiovascular death (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54-0.98) and a per-protocol analysis censoring non-adherence (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.72-0.99) favoured cocoa; a broader 2025 meta-analysis of 145 flavan-3-ol trials (5,205 participants, covering tea and grape extract as well as cocoa, with two industry-employed authors) reported office reductions of 2.8/2.0 mmHg and 24-hour ambulatory reductions of 3.7/2.6 mmHg at moderate strength of evidence.

Doses studied: Cochrane-included trials delivered 30 to 1,218 mg of flavanols per day (mean 670 mg) in 1.4 to 105 g of cocoa product daily for 2 to 18 weeks, compared with flavanol-free or low-flavanol controls. COSMOS used a cocoa extract supplement providing 500 mg flavanols/day, including 80 mg (-)-epicatechin, for a median 3.6 years. The null prehypertension trial used 50 g/day of 70%-cocoa dark chocolate containing 750 mg polyphenols. The 2025 flavan-3-ol meta-analysis pooled interventions delivering a mean 586 mg (95% CI 510-662) total flavan-3-ols per day across cocoa, tea, grape extract and apples.

Why this grade: A Cochrane review of 35 randomized trials (1,804 participants) establishes a real but very small effect - about 1.8 mmHg systolic overall, around 4 mmHg in the hypertensive subgroup, and no significant effect in normotensive participants - which is far too well studied to grade 'early' but far too small and heterogeneous to grade 'strong'. Confidence is further constrained by findings the Cochrane authors report themselves: the evidence was downgraded from high to moderate for unexplained heterogeneity, and a sensitivity analysis that removed trials with authors employed by the sponsoring industry (leaving 33 trials, 1,482 participants) produced a smaller effect size, which they read as reporting bias. An independent 2020 meta-analysis likewise could not demonstrate any benefit in normotensive or elevated-blood-pressure participants, a randomized trial of 50 g/day dark chocolate in prehypertension was null, and COSMOS - the largest and longest trial ever run on cocoa flavanols - missed its primary cardiovascular endpoint.

Sources (6)
  1. Effect of cocoa on blood pressure.
    Ried K, Fakler P, Stocks NP, 2017 (Cochrane) · PMID 28439881 · mixed

    Across 35 trials (40 comparisons, 1,804 participants, 2-18 weeks), flavanol-rich cocoa lowered systolic BP 1.76 mmHg (95% CI -3.09 to -0.43) and diastolic 1.76 mmHg (95% CI -2.57 to -0.94); systolic fell about 4 mmHg in 9 hypertensive comparisons (401 participants) but showed no significant difference in 23 normotensive comparisons (1,063 participants, subgroup test P=0.08); evidence was downgraded from high to moderate quality for unexplained heterogeneity, and a sensitivity analysis that removed trials with authors employed by the sponsoring industry - leaving 33 trials and 1,482 participants - produced a smaller effect size, indicating some reporting bias.

  2. Does chocolate reduce blood pressure? A meta-analysis.
    Ried K, Sullivan T, Fakler P, Frank OR, Stocks NP, 2010 · PMID 20584271 · mixed

    Pooling 15 trial arms from 13 studies, cocoa lowered systolic BP 3.2 mmHg and diastolic 2.0 mmHg overall, but subgroup analysis was significant only in hypertensive or prehypertensive participants (systolic -5.0 mmHg, P=0.0009) and not in normotensives (systolic -1.6 mmHg, P=0.17; diastolic -1.3 mmHg, P=0.12).

  3. Cocoa Consumption and Blood Pressure in Middle-Aged and Elderly Subjects: a Meta-Analysis.
    Jafarnejad S, Salek M, Clark CCT, 2020 · PMID 31907636 · mixed

    Across 13 studies (758 participants), cocoa lowered systolic BP by 2.77 mmHg (95% CI -5.28 to -0.27, P=0.03, I2=89%) and diastolic by 1.47 mmHg (95% CI -2.40 to -0.55, P=0.001); effects appeared in longer-duration and hypertensive subgroups, and the authors stated the analysis could not conclude any beneficial effect in normotensive or elevated-blood-pressure subjects.

  4. Dark chocolate or tomato extract for prehypertension: a randomised controlled trial.
    Ried K, Frank OR, Stocks NP, 2009 · PMID 19583878 · null

    In 36 prehypertensive adults given 50 g/day of 70% dark chocolate (750 mg polyphenols), tomato extract or placebo over 6 months, blood pressure changes within and between groups were not significant and independent of treatment; half the chocolate group found the daily dose hard to consume and 20% considered it an unacceptable long-term option.

  5. Effect of cocoa flavanol supplementation for the prevention of cardiovascular disease events: the COcoa Supplement and Multivitamin Outcomes Study (COSMOS) randomized clinical trial.
    Sesso HD, Manson JE, Aragaki AK, et al., 2022 · PMID 35294962 · null

    Among 21,442 older adults given 500 mg/day cocoa flavanols for a median 3.6 years, total cardiovascular events did not differ significantly from placebo (410 vs 456 events; HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78-1.02, P=0.11); cardiovascular death was lower as a secondary endpoint (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54-0.98) and all-cause mortality was not (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.77-1.03), while a per-protocol analysis censoring at non-adherence did support a lower risk of total cardiovascular events (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.72-0.99). There were no safety concerns.

  6. Impact of flavan-3-ols on blood pressure and endothelial function in diverse populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Lagou V, Greyling A, Ferruzzi MG, et al., 2025 · PMID 40126033 · positive

    Across 145 RCTs (5,205 participants) of flavan-3-ol-rich foods and supplements delivering a mean 586 mg total flavan-3-ols - a broader class than cocoa, also covering tea, grape extract and apples - repeated consumption lowered office BP 2.8/2.0 mmHg and 24-hour ambulatory BP 3.7/2.6 mmHg, with larger office effects at elevated or hypertensive baseline BP (5.9/2.7 mmHg); heterogeneity exceeded I2 50% and was unexplained, limiting strength of evidence to 'moderate', and two authors were employed by Unilever and Lipton Teas.
