Conventional medicine

Duloxetine

Moderate evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Duloxetine is an antidepressant that also turns down pain signalling, and it is the one medicine of its kind that researchers are genuinely confident helps with long-term pain, especially knee arthritis. The relief is real but moderate rather than dramatic, it takes a few weeks to show up, and for back pain specifically the trials disagree — one found a clear benefit and another the same company ran found none at the end of the study. Side effects like nausea, dry mouth and fatigue lead a meaningful minority of people to stop, and it should never be started or stopped without a doctor because of how it interacts with other medicines.

Safety

Tolerability is the main practical limit, and it worsens with dose. In a 401-participant chronic low back pain trial, 15.2% on 60 mg discontinued for adverse events versus 5.4% on placebo; in a dose-ranging trial the 120 mg arm reached 24.1% versus 8.5% on placebo. Across older-adult trials, 7 of 15 studies reported increased withdrawals in the antidepressant arm, and gastrointestinal adverse events ran three to four times placebo rates in knee osteoarthritis trials. The most common effects are nausea, dry mouth, fatigue, dizziness, constipation, diarrhoea, sweating and reduced appetite; nausea often eases after the first week or two. Duloxetine can raise blood pressure and heart rate, and can cause hyponatremia, particularly in older adults. Do not stop abruptly — a discontinuation syndrome with dizziness, sensory disturbance, irritability and flu-like symptoms is common, so taper with a prescriber. Cochrane rated the safety evidence for all antidepressants in chronic pain as very low certainty, so the true harm profile in this population is not well characterised. Benefits build over several weeks; it is not a rescue medicine.

Who should be careful: Should not be used with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, or within 14 days of stopping one (and at least 5 days after stopping duloxetine before starting an MAOI). Avoid in uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma, substantial alcohol use or chronic liver disease because of hepatotoxicity risk, and in severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min). Antidepressants including duloxetine carry a boxed warning for increased suicidal thoughts and behaviours in children, adolescents and young adults under 25. Use caution in uncontrolled hypertension and in people with a history of mania or bipolar disorder.

Interactions: Serotonin syndrome risk rises when combined with other serotonergic agents — SSRIs, other SNRIs, tramadol, tapentadol, triptans, linezolid, lithium, methylene blue or St John's wort. Combining duloxetine with NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants or antiplatelets increases bleeding risk, which matters directly here because NSAIDs are a common co-treatment for the same pain. Strong CYP1A2 inhibitors such as fluvoxamine and ciprofloxacin substantially raise duloxetine levels; smoking lowers them. Duloxetine inhibits CYP2D6 and can raise levels of drugs such as metoprolol, flecainide and certain tricyclics. Alcohol should be limited because of the combined liver burden.

What the evidence says

Evidence for duloxetine in chronic musculoskeletal pain is the most consistent of any centrally acting agent reviewed here, though the effect is modest and uneven by pain site. A Cochrane network meta-analysis of 176 studies (28,664 participants) found duloxetine consistently the highest-ranked antidepressant across efficacy outcomes: at the standard 60 mg dose it gave substantial (50%) pain relief with an odds ratio of 1.91 (95% CI 1.69 to 2.17) across 16 studies and 4490 participants, and reduced continuous pain intensity by SMD -0.31 (95% CI -0.39 to -0.24) across 18 studies and 4959 participants, both moderate certainty. Effects differ by pain site: a BMJ meta-analysis of 33 trials (5318 participants) found SNRIs reduced back pain by 5.30 points (95% CI 3.30 to 7.31) on a 0-100 scale at 3-13 weeks — moderate certainty but judged small and not clinically important — versus 9.72 points (95% CI 6.69 to 12.75) in osteoarthritis at low certainty, where a clinically important effect could not be excluded. In knee osteoarthritis specifically, a meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (1713 participants) found statistically significant moderate benefits on pain, function and quality of life to 13 weeks, with gastrointestinal adverse events three to four times more frequent than placebo. The two manufacturer-sponsored chronic low back pain trials disagree: a 401-participant trial found duloxetine 60 mg significantly better than placebo on average pain (P at or below .001) with 15.2% discontinuing for adverse events versus 5.4%, while an earlier 404-participant dose-ranging trial missed its primary endpoint entirely, with no dose separating from placebo at endpoint despite separation from weeks 3 to 11. Cochrane rated safety evidence for all antidepressants in chronic pain as very low certainty and found no reliable evidence of long-term efficacy for any of them.

Doses studied: 60 mg once daily is the standard studied dose and was as efficacious as higher doses for most outcomes in the Cochrane network meta-analysis. Some chronic low back pain trials escalated non-responders to 120 mg/day after 7 weeks, or randomised 20, 60 and 120 mg arms directly; the 120 mg arm carried markedly worse tolerability. Most trials ran 12-14 weeks; average RCT length across the antidepressant literature was 10 weeks.

Why this grade: A Cochrane network meta-analysis of 176 studies and 28,664 participants concluded duloxetine is the only antidepressant it is confident about for chronic pain, ranking it highest across efficacy outcomes with moderate-certainty evidence, and multiple placebo-controlled RCTs in knee osteoarthritis are positive. But the effect is modest, not large: pain intensity SMD -0.31, and a BMJ meta-analysis found the SNRI effect on back pain 'small and not clinically important' (-5.30 points on a 0-100 scale), with a clinically important effect only possibly present in osteoarthritis. The back-pain trial record is also inconsistent rather than uniformly positive — of two manufacturer-sponsored 12- to 13-week trials, one met its primary endpoint and one did not, with the failed trial showing separation from weeks 3 to 11 that was lost by endpoint. Guideline support is conditional, not strong. Safety evidence across all antidepressants for pain was rated very low certainty, and discontinuation for adverse events runs roughly three times placebo at 60 mg and higher still at 120 mg. Real RCT and meta-analysis evidence, overall positive but modest and mixed by pain site — that is the definition of moderate, and it is graded down from strong because certainty about long-term efficacy and safety is absent.

Sources (7)
  1. Antidepressants for pain management in adults with chronic pain: a network meta-analysis.
    Birkinshaw H, Friedrich CM, Cole P, et al., 2023 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 37160297 · positive

    176 studies, 28,664 participants, across fibromyalgia (59 studies), neuropathic pain (49) and musculoskeletal pain (40); average trial length 10 weeks. Duloxetine 60 mg gave substantial pain relief with OR 1.91 (95% CI 1.69 to 2.17; 16 studies, 4490 participants; moderate certainty) and reduced pain intensity by SMD -0.31 (95% CI -0.39 to -0.24; 18 studies, 4959 participants; moderate certainty); standard dose was as efficacious as high dose. Duloxetine was the only antidepressant the authors were confident about, with milnacipran promising but less certain. Safety outcomes were very low certainty for all antidepressants, most studies measured short-term outcomes only and excluded people with low mood, and there was no reliable evidence for long-term efficacy of any of them.

  2. Efficacy and safety of antidepressants for the treatment of back pain and osteoarthritis: systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Ferreira GE, McLachlan AJ, Lin CC, Zadro JR, Abdel-Shaheed C, O'Keeffe M, Maher CG, 2021 (BMJ) · PMID 33472813 · mixed

    33 trials, 5318 participants. Moderate-certainty evidence that SNRIs reduced back pain by 5.30 points (95% CI 3.30 to 7.31) on a 0-100 scale at 3-13 weeks, and back-pain disability by 3.55 points (95% CI 1.88 to 5.22) — effects the authors judged small and not clinically important. For osteoarthritis, low-certainty evidence of a 9.72-point pain reduction (95% CI 6.69 to 12.75) at 3-13 weeks, where a clinically important effect could not be excluded. Tricyclics and other antidepressants did not reduce back pain or disability.

  3. A double-blind, randomized trial of duloxetine versus placebo in the management of chronic low back pain.
    Skljarevski V, Ossanna M, Liu-Seifert H, Zhang Q, Chappell A, Iyengar S, Detke M, Backonja M, 2009 (Eur J Neurol); conducted by Lilly Research Laboratories · PMID 19469829 · null

    404 adults with non-radicular chronic low back pain randomised to duloxetine 20, 60 or 120 mg once daily or placebo for 13 weeks; 267 completed. The trial missed its primary endpoint: no significant differences existed between any dose of duloxetine and placebo in reduction of weekly mean 24-hour average pain at endpoint. Duloxetine 60 mg was superior to placebo from weeks 3 to 11 but superiority was not maintained at weeks 12-13. Secondary measures (patient global impression, Roland-Morris disability, BPI average pain and interference) favoured 60 mg. Significantly more patients on 120 mg discontinued because of adverse events (24.1%) than on placebo (8.5%).

  4. Duloxetine versus placebo in patients with chronic low back pain: a 12-week, fixed-dose, randomized, double-blind trial.
    Skljarevski V, Zhang S, Desaiah D, Alaka KJ, Palacios S, Miazgowski T, Patrick K, 2010 (J Pain); conducted by Lilly Research Laboratories · PMID 20472510 · positive

    401 adults with non-neuropathic chronic low back pain and baseline average pain of 4 or more, randomised to duloxetine 60 mg once daily or placebo for 12 weeks. Duloxetine produced a significantly greater reduction in Brief Pain Inventory average pain (P at or below .001) and greater improvement on patient global impression, BPI severity and interference, and 50% response rate; the Roland-Morris disability score and 30% response rate showed only numerical improvement. Significantly more duloxetine patients discontinued because of adverse events (15.2% vs 5.4%; P=.002), with nausea and dry mouth most common.

  5. Efficacy and safety of duloxetine in osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Osani MC, Bannuru RR, 2019 (Korean J Intern Med) · PMID 30871298 · positive

    7 RCTs (2102 participants) met inclusion criteria and 5 (1713 participants) were pooled. Duloxetine showed statistically significant, moderate benefits on pain, function and quality of life in knee osteoarthritis for up to 13 weeks. Reported incidence of gastrointestinal adverse events was three to four times higher with duloxetine than placebo, and the authors concluded patient preference and clinical judgement must be weighed before initiation.

  6. Efficacy and safety of antidepressants for pain in older adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Narayan SW, Naganathan V, Vizza L, et al., 2024 (Br J Clin Pharmacol) · PMID 39265130 · mixed

    15 studies, 1369 participants aged 65 and over; duloxetine and amitriptyline were each the most studied drug (6/15 studies) and knee osteoarthritis the most studied condition (6/15). For knee osteoarthritis, antidepressants gave no statistically significant effect in the immediate term (0-2 weeks: -5.6, 95% CI -11.5 to 0.3); duloxetine gave a statistically significant but, in the authors' words, very small intermediate-term effect (-9.1, 95% CI -11.8 to -6.4). Almost half (7/15) of studies reported increased withdrawal due to adverse events in the antidepressant arm. Most trials had fewer than 100 participants, disclosed industry ties, and unclear or high risk of bias.

  7. 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Osteoarthritis of the Hand, Hip, and Knee.
    Kolasinski SL, Neogi T, Hochberg MC, et al., 2020 (Arthritis Care Res) · PMID 31908149 · positive

    Duloxetine received a conditional — not strong — recommendation for osteoarthritis of the hand, hip and knee, grouped with acetaminophen and tramadol, while exercise, weight loss, oral NSAIDs and topical NSAIDs for knee OA received strong recommendations. The panel emphasised shared decision-making accounting for patient values, preferences and comorbidities.
