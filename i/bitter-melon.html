Bitter melon (Momordica charantia)

Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Bitter melon is a vegetable eaten across Asia, Africa and the Caribbean and is one of the most traditional remedies for diabetes, but the clinical trials are small and the results are underwhelming: the best-run studies found little or no change in the three-month average blood sugar, and when compared directly it lowered blood sugar less than a modest dose of metformin. In prediabetes, a few trials found fasting glucose came down by a small amount over 8 to 12 weeks, while a shorter 4-week trial found nothing. It is safe to eat as food, but concentrated extracts should be avoided in pregnancy because animal studies show harm to the fetus, and it is not a replacement for any medication you have been prescribed.

Safety

Bitter melon is not a replacement for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and nothing here is a reason to stop or reduce a prescription. Human trials up to 16 weeks report only mild gastrointestinal complaints and no serious adverse events (Cochrane 2012, PMID 22895968; Peter et al. 2019, PMID 30385422; Mes et al. 2025, PMID 40199408), with no change in creatinine or liver transaminases in the placebo-controlled Philippine trial (Dans et al. 2007, PMID 17493509). Cucurbitacins, the bitter compounds in this plant family, are recognised as a major class of toxic compounds in related Cucurbitaceae species, implicated in poisoning and death (Omokhua-Uyi and Van Staden 2020, PMID 32422356). Preparations vary widely (juice, powder, extract, seed, leaf) and, as Cochrane noted, no two trials tested the same product, which is a major reason the evidence is hard to interpret.

Who should be careful: Pregnancy and women trying to conceive: a water extract of unripe fruit given to pregnant Sprague Dawley rats produced malformations in 8.65% of litters versus 1.62% in controls, with 31.2% of malformed pups having multiple congenital malformations and nine resorption sites versus none in controls, and the authors conclude it should be used with caution in humans (Uche-Nwachi and McEwen 2009, PMID 21304609); a dry leaf extract given to pregnant Wistar rats reduced feed and water intake and body-mass gain, indicating maternal toxicity, although it did not markedly affect reproductive outcomes or skeletal malformations and the authors describe it as low maternal toxicity with cytotoxicity in vitro (Trautenmuller et al. 2022, PMID 36529899). Concentrated extracts should therefore be avoided in pregnancy even though the cooked vegetable is a normal food. Evidence on male fertility is not alarming: bitter melon seed extract did not alter reproductive activity or offspring production in male golden hamsters (Choi et al. 2017, PMID 28785742). Type 1 diabetes: not studied, and not a substitute for insulin.

Interactions: Assume additive glucose lowering with insulin, a sulfonylurea or metformin rather than treating it as theoretical: pooled trials estimate fasting glucose falling 0.72 to 0.85 mmol/L and post-meal glucose 1.43 to 2.28 mmol/L in type 2 diabetes (PMID 30385422, PMID 38784554), although those pooled estimates rest on low to very low quality evidence and the best-designed individual trials were null. If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, check your glucose more often for the first few weeks after starting or stopping bitter melon, and ask your prescriber to review your doses rather than adjusting them yourself. This matters more than for most items on this page because bitter melon is also an everyday vegetable in many households: a change in how much you eat, not just whether you take a capsule, can move your readings. No formal pharmacokinetic interaction studies in humans were retrieved.

What the evidence says

Everything measured is a surrogate: fasting glucose, post-meal glucose, fructosamine, HbA1c, HOMA-IR. No bitter melon trial has assessed progression from prediabetes to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney disease, amputation or death, and the Cochrane review notes that no trial investigated death from any cause, morbidity, quality of life or costs. That 2012 Cochrane review (4 RCTs up to 3 months, 479 participants, generally high risk of bias, no meta-analysis possible because no preparation was tested twice) found no statistically significant difference in glycaemic control versus placebo and no significant change compared with metformin or glibenclamide. The most rigorous placebo trial (40 patients with HbA1c 7-9%, 3 months) found an HbA1c difference of 0.22% favouring bitter melon (95% CI -0.40 to 0.84, P=0.48) with only 11% achieved power against the targeted 1% decline; a 12-week trial in 90 patients found HbA1c unchanged in both arms although average fasting glucose fell (P=0.014); and in a 4-week four-arm trial only 2,000 mg/day reduced fructosamine (-10.2 umol/L), less than metformin 1,000 mg/day (-16.8 umol/L). Meta-analyses that pool lower-quality trials are more favourable: Peter et al. (10 studies, n=1,045, moderate-to-high risk of bias) estimated fasting glucose -0.72 mmol/L, postprandial glucose -1.43 mmol/L and HbA1c -0.26% on low to very low quality evidence; Zhang et al. (8 trials, n=423) estimated fasting glucose -0.85 mmol/L and HbA1c -0.38%; and a 2025 GRADE-adherent meta-analysis of 25 trials with 34 sub-studies estimated standardised mean differences of -0.46 for fasting glucose, -0.57 for HbA1c, -0.48 for insulin and -0.52 for HOMA-IR, with no effect on beta-cell function. In prediabetes, 2.5 g/day powder for 8 weeks lowered fasting glucose by 0.31 mmol/L (5.6 mg/dL) versus a cucumber-juice control in 52 Tanzanian adults, with larger effects at higher baseline glucose and not all participants responding; and in a pair of Dutch trials, a 4-week crossover (n=30, 2.4 g/day) found no significant differences at all, while a 12-week parallel trial (n=38, 3.6 g/day) found fasting glucose, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR all improved, with fasting glucose falling about 0.05 mmol/L per week.

Doses studied: 2-4 g/day dried fruit powder or 500-2,000 mg/day extract capsules for 4-16 weeks in type 2 diabetes; 2.5 g/day powder, 2.4 g/day freeze-dried fruit juice or 3.6 g/day freeze-dried whole fruit for 4-12 weeks in prediabetes.

Why this grade: The Cochrane review found no statistically significant difference from placebo across 4 RCTs and judged the evidence insufficient; the best-designed placebo-controlled trials are null on HbA1c (a 0.22% difference with only 11% achieved power, and unchanged HbA1c in a 90-person 12-week trial); the head-to-head trial found bitter melon weaker than a low dose of metformin; and the most rigorous prediabetes trial to date was null over 4 weeks and only positive over 12. Later meta-analyses pooling low- to very-low-quality trials do estimate benefit, including a 2025 GRADE-adherent synthesis of 25 trials, but they rest on preparations no two of which were the same, on moderate-to-high risk of bias, and entirely on surrogate markers: no trial has assessed progression to diabetes, vascular events, kidney disease or death. Held at early rather than moderate because the highest-quality individual trials remain null and the evidence base is heterogeneous and preliminary rather than settled; it is not not_supported, because the evidence is not yet adequate to rule benefit out.

Sources (9)
  1. Momordica charantia for type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Ooi CP, Yassin Z, Hamid TA (Cochrane), 2012 · PMID 22895968 · null

    4 RCTs of up to 3 months, 479 participants, risk of bias generally high, with no meta-analysis possible because no preparation was tested twice: no statistically significant difference in glycaemic control between Momordica charantia preparations and placebo, and no significant change in reliable glycaemic parameters versus metformin or glibenclamide; no serious adverse effects; no trial investigated death, morbidity, quality of life or costs; the authors concluded the evidence is insufficient.

  2. The effect of Momordica charantia capsule preparation on glycemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus needs further studies.
    Dans AM, Villarruz MV, Jimeno CA, et al., 2007 · PMID 17493509 · null

    Randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 40 patients with type 2 diabetes and HbA1c 7-9%, two capsules three times daily for 3 months on top of standard therapy: the difference in mean HbA1c change was 0.22% in favour of bitter melon (95% CI -0.40 to 0.84, P=0.4825); no significant effect on fasting blood sugar, total cholesterol, weight, creatinine, ALT or AST; against a targeted 1% decline the achieved power was only 11%, so no definite conclusion about effectiveness could be drawn.

  3. Hypoglycemic efficacy and safety of Momordica charantia (bitter melon) in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.
    Kim SK, Jung J, Jung JH, et al., 2020 · PMID 32951763 · mixed

    Randomized placebo-controlled 12-week trial, 90 patients with type 2 diabetes in the final analysis: HbA1c remained unchanged in both the bitter melon extract and placebo groups, while average fasting glucose decreased in the bitter melon group (P=0.014); no serious adverse events were reported.

  4. Hypoglycemic effect of bitter melon compared with metformin in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients.
    Fuangchan A, Sonthisombat P, Seubnukarn T, et al., 2011 · PMID 21211558 · mixed

    4-week multicentre randomized double-blind active-control trial: bitter melon 2,000 mg/day reduced fructosamine by 10.2 umol/L (95% CI -19.1 to -1.3) versus 16.8 umol/L (95% CI -31.2 to -2.4) with metformin 1,000 mg/day, while 500 and 1,000 mg/day did not significantly reduce fructosamine; the authors describe the effect as modest and less than metformin.

  5. Bitter gourd (Momordica charantia L.) supplementation for twelve weeks improves biomarkers of glucose homeostasis in a prediabetic population.
    Mes JJ, van den Belt M, van der Haar S, et al., 2025 · PMID 40199408 · mixed

    Two randomized controlled trials in prediabetes. Study 1 (4-week crossover, n=30, freeze-dried bitter gourd juice 2.4 g/day vs cucumber control) found NO significant differences between bitter gourd and placebo. Study 2 (12-week parallel, n=38, freeze-dried whole fruit 3.6 g/day) found significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose (P=0.014), fasting insulin (P=0.007) and HOMA-IR (P=0.003), with between-treatment effects on fasting glucose (P=0.026) and HOMA-IR (P=0.045) and no effect on other glucose-metabolism biomarkers; fasting glucose fell about 0.05 mmol/L per week; no indications of health risks or side effects.

  6. Momordica charantia L. lowers elevated glycaemia in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients: Systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Peter EL, Kasali FM, Deyno S, et al., 2019 · PMID 30385422 · positive

    10 studies of type 2 diabetes (n=1,045, 4-16 weeks follow-up, overall moderate to high risk of bias): versus placebo, fasting glucose -0.72 mmol/L (95% CI -1.33 to -0.12), postprandial glucose -1.43 mmol/L (95% CI -2.18 to -0.67) and HbA1c -0.26% (95% CI -0.49 to -0.03); fasting glucose -0.31 mmol/L in prediabetes (n=52, downgraded to low quality); the authors state the conclusion rests on low to very low quality evidence with sparse safety data.

  7. Efficacy of Momordica charantia in glycaemic control and insulin resistance among patients with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. A GRADE-adherent meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Mkhize SAL, Phoswa WN, Ngubane PS, Mokgalaboni K, 2025 · PMID 41280283 · positive

    25 randomised trials with 34 sub-studies in prediabetes or type 2 diabetes: fasting blood glucose SMD -0.46 (95% CI -0.73 to -0.18, P=0.0012), HbA1c SMD -0.57 (95% CI -0.83 to -0.31, P<0.0001), insulin SMD -0.48 (P=0.0082) and HOMA-IR SMD -0.52 (P=0.0195), but NO effect on beta-cell function (HOMA-beta, P=0.586); the authors note that prior quantitative syntheses report contradictory findings.

  8. Effects of Momordica charantia L. supplementation on glycemic control and lipid profile in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Zhang X, Zhao Y, Song Y, Miao M, 2024 · PMID 38784554 · positive

    8 trials, 423 patients with type 2 diabetes: fasting blood glucose -0.85 mmol/L (95% CI -1.44 to -0.26, I2 73.4%), postprandial glucose -2.28 mmol/L (95% CI -3.35 to -1.21, I2 66.9%), HbA1c -0.38% (95% CI -0.53 to -0.23, I2 37.6%) and total cholesterol -0.38 mmol/L, with no significant differences in triglycerides, HDL or LDL; the authors call for further high-quality studies to validate the results.

  9. Bitter gourd reduces elevated fasting plasma glucose levels in an intervention study among prediabetics in Tanzania.
    Krawinkel MB, Ludwig C, Swai ME, Yang RY, Chun KP, Habicht SD, 2018 · PMID 29339109 · positive

    Randomized placebo-controlled single-blind crossover trial in 52 adults with prediabetes, 2.5 g/day bitter gourd powder versus cucumber juice for 8 weeks each: fasting plasma glucose change differed by 0.31 mmol/L (5.6 mg/dL, P=0.031, Cohen's d 0.62) across 44 complete datasets, with larger effects at higher baseline glucose and not all participants responding; no serious adverse effects.
