Cognitive behavioural therapy for fatigue

Moderate evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

This is talking therapy aimed at the habits, thoughts and activity patterns that can keep exhaustion going, not at telling you the tiredness is imaginary. Several good trials show it helps people report less fatigue, especially after cancer treatment and after COVID-19. The honest caveats are that people know which treatment they got, the improvements are mostly in what people say rather than what they can do, and in two long trials the advantage had faded by a year because the comparison groups caught up. In ME/CFS its role is now limited to helping people cope rather than aiming at recovery.

Safety

CBT for fatigue carries low physical risk and adverse events in trials have been uncommon: PACE recorded serious adverse reactions in 3 of 161 CBT participants (2%), similar to the other arms (1-2%), and the long COVID trial recorded 8 adverse events during CBT versus 20 during care as usual, none serious. The more substantial risk is not medical but framing: when therapy is presented as though changed beliefs and behaviour will resolve the illness, people whose illness does not resolve can be left feeling blamed, and patient organisations have consistently raised this. NICE (2021) now states CBT should be offered in ME/CFS only as a supportive intervention, not as a treatment aimed at recovery.

Who should be careful: CBT is not a substitute for finding a medical cause of fatigue; it should follow, not replace, appropriate workup for anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, diabetes, medication effects and other treatable causes. Anyone with active suicidal thinking, untreated severe depression, psychosis or a substance use disorder needs those addressed first by appropriate mental health care rather than a fatigue-focused protocol.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Where CBT is delivered alongside a graded activity component, the cautions that apply to graded exercise apply here too, particularly for anyone with post-exertional malaise.

What the evidence says

A Cochrane review of 15 trials (1,043 participants with chronic fatigue syndrome) found CBT reduced fatigue at post-treatment versus usual care (SMD -0.39, 95% CI -0.60 to -0.19; 6 studies, 373 participants), with clinical response in 40% of CBT participants versus 26% on usual care, though findings at follow-up were inconsistent (Price 2008). In the PACE trial (n=641), CBT lowered fatigue scores by 3.4 points (95% CI 1.8-5.0) versus specialist medical care alone at 52 weeks (White 2011), but that advantage did not hold: at a median of 31 months there was little evidence of any difference between the randomised groups, the pacing and specialist-care groups having improved to a similar level, with 44% of responders having taken additional treatment after the trial (Sharpe 2015). An independent reanalysis using the originally protocol-specified primary outcome found CBT and graded exercise did not significantly outperform the control group after correcting for the trial's own pre-specified comparisons, that recovery rates were low and not significantly different across groups, and that significant effects were almost entirely confined to self-report measures and did not endure beyond two years (Wilshire 2018). The same rise-then-converge pattern appeared independently in multiple sclerosis: CBT reduced fatigue by 6.7 CIS20r points at 16 weeks (95% CI -10.7 to -2.7) but the difference had disappeared by 52 weeks (0.5, 95% CI -3.6 to 4.4), with no clinically relevant effect on participation (van den Akker 2017). Evidence is more encouraging elsewhere: in severe fatigue 3-12 months after COVID-19, CBT reduced fatigue severity by 8.8 points versus care as usual (95% CI -11.9 to -5.8, Cohen's d 0.69, n=114), sustained at 6 months (Kuut 2023); and in severely fatigued disease-free cancer survivors, CBT produced a 13.3-point fatigue advantage over waiting list (95% CI 8.6-18.1), with clinically significant improvement in 54% versus 4% (Gielissen 2006). Waiting-list and usual-care comparators inflate apparent effects in unblinded trials, and this should temper how all of these numbers are read.

Doses studied: The MS trial delivered 12 individual sessions with a psychologist over 16 weeks against three MS-nurse consultations (van den Akker 2017). The long COVID trial provided CBT targeting perpetuating factors of fatigue over 17 weeks (Kuut 2023). The cancer-survivor trial delivered individual therapy with outcomes assessed at 6 months (Gielissen 2006). Trials in this literature typically run three to six months of individual sessions.

Why this grade: Multiple randomised trials and a Cochrane review show CBT reduces self-reported fatigue compared with usual care or waiting list across several populations, which rules out the lower grades. It falls short of strong because the trials are necessarily unblinded with self-reported primary outcomes; because the advantage disappears by long-term follow-up in two independent trials in different diseases (PACE in ME/CFS, TREFAMS-CBT in multiple sclerosis) as comparison groups catch up; because an independent reanalysis using the originally specified protocol found the effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons; and because guidance in ME/CFS has narrowed CBT to a supportive role rather than a treatment aimed at recovery.

Sources (7)
  1. Cognitive behaviour therapy for chronic fatigue syndrome in adults.
    Price JR et al. (Cochrane), 2008 · PMID 18646067 · positive

    Across 15 studies with 1,043 participants, CBT versus usual care (6 studies, 373 participants) reduced fatigue at post-treatment with SMD -0.39 (95% CI -0.60 to -0.19), and 40% of CBT participants (4 studies, 371 participants) showed clinical response versus 26% on usual care; the review reports this as OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.29-0.76), a figure whose direction is the reverse of the response percentages and is easily misread. Findings at follow-up were inconsistent.

  2. Comparison of adaptive pacing therapy, cognitive behaviour therapy, graded exercise therapy, and specialist medical care for chronic fatigue syndrome (PACE): a randomised trial.
    White PD et al., 2011 · PMID 21334061 · positive

    In 641 participants, mean fatigue scores at 52 weeks were 3.4 points lower for CBT than specialist medical care alone (95% CI 1.8-5.0, p=0.0001) and physical function scores 7.1 points higher (95% CI 2.0-12.1, p=0.0068); serious adverse reactions occurred in 3 of 161 CBT participants (2%).

  3. Rehabilitative treatments for chronic fatigue syndrome: long-term follow-up from the PACE trial.
    Sharpe M et al., 2015 · PMID 26521770 · null

    At a median 31 months after randomisation (481 of 641 participants responding), there was little evidence of differences in outcomes between the originally randomised treatment groups; participants originally assigned to adaptive pacing or specialist medical care alone had improved to a similar level as CBT and GET, though 210 (44%) had received additional treatment after the trial.

  4. Cognitive behavioral therapy positively affects fatigue in patients with multiple sclerosis: Results of a randomized controlled trial.
    van den Akker LE et al., 2017 · PMID 28528567 · mixed

    In 91 patients with severe MS-related fatigue randomised to 12 individual CBT sessions over 16 weeks or three MS-nurse consultations, CBT reduced CIS20r fatigue by 6.7 points at 16 weeks (95% CI -10.7 to -2.7), but the between-group difference had disappeared by 52 weeks (0.5, 95% CI -3.6 to 4.4) and no clinically relevant effect on societal participation was found.

  5. Rethinking the treatment of chronic fatigue syndrome-a reanalysis and evaluation of findings from a recent major trial of graded exercise and CBT.
    Wilshire CE et al., 2018 · PMID 29562932 · negative

    Using the originally protocol-specified primary outcome of overall improvement rates, CBT and graded exercise did not significantly outperform the control group after correcting for the number of comparisons specified in the trial protocol; recovery rates were consistently low and not significantly different across groups, and significant effects were almost entirely confined to self-report measures and did not endure beyond two years.

  6. Efficacy of Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy Targeting Severe Fatigue Following Coronavirus Disease 2019: Results of a Randomized Controlled Trial.
    Kuut TA et al., 2023 · PMID 37155736 · positive

    In 114 mainly non-hospitalised, self-referred patients severely fatigued 3-12 months after COVID-19, 17 weeks of CBT reduced fatigue severity by 8.8 points versus care as usual across follow-up assessments (95% CI -11.9 to -5.8; Cohen's d 0.69), with the difference present at end of treatment (-9.3, 95% CI -13.3 to -5.3) and sustained at 6-month follow-up (-8.4, 95% CI -13.1 to -3.7); 8 adverse events were recorded during CBT versus 20 during care as usual, none serious.

  7. Effects of cognitive behavior therapy in severely fatigued disease-free cancer patients compared with patients waiting for cognitive behavior therapy: a randomized controlled trial.
    Gielissen MFM et al., 2006 · PMID 17050873 · positive

    Among 98 analysed cancer survivors with somatically unexplained fatigue (112 randomised), CBT produced a 13.3-point greater reduction in fatigue severity than waiting list (95% CI 8.6-18.1) and clinically significant improvement in 54% versus 4% of participants at 6 months.
