Conventional medicine

Ramelteon (melatonin receptor agonist)

Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Ramelteon works on the same brain receptors as melatonin and is approved in the US for trouble falling asleep. It has been tested in thousands of people and it is genuinely safe and non-habit-forming, but the benefit is very small: on average it shortened the time to fall asleep by about four minutes and did not increase how much sleep people felt they got. Europe's current insomnia guideline now recommends against using it, and a large head-to-head comparison of sleep drugs found no meaningful overall benefit - so the honest summary is low risk, but not much reward.

Safety

Ramelteon is not a controlled substance, has not shown abuse liability or dependence in trials, and rebound insomnia and withdrawal symptoms were not seen even after six months of nightly use. Somnolence was the only adverse event that reached significance in the 5,812-patient meta-analysis. Prolactin elevation and decreased testosterone have been reported in the US prescribing information and can present as menstrual irregularity, galactorrhoea or reduced libido. Rare class effects for hypnotics apply: complex sleep behaviours (sleep-walking, sleep-driving) and severe allergic reactions including angioedema and anaphylaxis. Separately, an analysis of FDA data across four modern hypnotics including ramelteon found a higher incidence of depression on active drug than on placebo (2.0% vs 0.9%, p<0.002), a signal worth knowing before starting any hypnotic in someone at risk of depression. The main caution here is not toxicity but expectation: the benefit demonstrated in trials is small.

Who should be careful: Severe hepatic impairment; concomitant fluvoxamine; a history of angioedema or anaphylaxis after a previous dose. As with any sedating agent, caution in untreated sleep apnea and in older adults at risk of falls, and it should not be taken with or immediately after a high-fat meal, which alters absorption.

Interactions: Fluvoxamine, a strong CYP1A2 inhibitor, dramatically increases ramelteon exposure and combined use should be avoided. Other CYP1A2, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. ciprofloxacin, fluconazole, ketoconazole) raise levels; rifampin substantially lowers them and can abolish the effect. Alcohol adds to sedation without adding benefit.

What the evidence says

Ramelteon is a selective MT1/MT2 melatonin-receptor agonist approved in the US and Japan for insomnia; the evidence base is reasonably large but the effect sizes are very small and the guideline picture has turned against it. A meta-analysis pooling 13 published and unpublished placebo-controlled trials (5,812 patients, mean study duration 38 days) found ramelteon reduced subjective sleep latency by 4.30 min (95% CI -7.01 to -1.58) and improved sleep quality by SMD -0.074 (95% CI -0.13 to -0.02), and improved latency to persistent sleep, sleep efficiency and total sleep time on polysomnography, but was NOT associated with any increase in subjective total sleep time; the authors concluded the clinical impact is small. A second meta-analysis of 8 trials found improvements in subjective and polysomnographic sleep latency, total sleep time and REM latency, but on subgroup analysis subjective sleep latency was reduced only in patients aged 18-64 and not in patients over 65. The 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 170 trials placed ramelteon among the drugs that 'did not show overall material benefits', found benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, zolpidem and zopiclone more efficacious than ramelteon (SMD range 0.27-0.71), and found fewer all-cause discontinuations on eszopiclone and on intermediate- and long-acting benzodiazepines than on ramelteon. In dementia, a Cochrane review of ramelteon 8 mg in 74 participants with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease found no effect on total nocturnal sleep time at either 1 week (the primary endpoint) or 8 weeks. The 2023 European Insomnia Guideline now lists ramelteon among drugs that are not recommended for insomnia treatment, at evidence level A. The positive side of the ledger is real and should be weighed: the 2017 AASM guideline suggests ramelteon for sleep-onset insomnia as a WEAK recommendation, and a 6-month randomised placebo-controlled trial in 451 adults with chronic primary insomnia - unusually long for this field - found consistently reduced latency to persistent sleep at week 1 and months 1, 3, 5 and 6, with no next-morning residual effects, rebound insomnia or withdrawal symptoms. The reconciliation is that ramelteon reliably produces a small, specific sleep-onset effect that most syntheses judge clinically unimportant.

Doses studied: 8 mg taken roughly 30 minutes before bedtime is the dose used across the pooled trials, in the 6-month trial and in the Alzheimer's disease study; mean trial duration in the largest meta-analysis was 38 days. One review specifically called for trials of higher doses (32 mg or 64 mg) in older adults, meaning that dosing question is still open.

Why this grade: Downgraded from moderate to not_supported on verification. The 2023 European Insomnia Guideline - the most recent guideline covering this drug - states that ramelteon is not recommended for insomnia treatment, at evidence level A, and the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 170 trials placed melatonin and ramelteon among agents that 'did not show overall material benefits'. The pooled effect across 13 trials and 5,812 patients is a 4.30-minute reduction in subjective sleep latency with no improvement in subjective total sleep time at all, and one meta-analysis found even the subjective sleep-latency benefit absent in adults over 65. This is emphatically not an 'early' or unstudied agent - the trial base is large and includes a 451-patient 6-month RCT with sustained sleep-latency benefit, and the 2017 AASM guideline still carries a WEAK recommendation for sleep-onset insomnia. But a current guideline recommending against, alongside a network meta-analysis finding no material benefit, meets the not_supported definition, and the conservative rule resolves the remaining tension downward. Graded not_supported for weak benefit, not for danger: this remains one of the safer agents in the class.

Sources (8)
  1. The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.
    Riemann et al., 2023 · PMID 38016484 · negative

    Current European guideline, updating the 2017 edition: 'Antihistaminergic drugs, antipsychotics, fast-release melatonin, ramelteon and phytotherapeutics are not recommended for insomnia treatment (A).' Cognitive-behavioural therapy for insomnia is first-line for chronic insomnia in adults of any age (A); benzodiazepines, benzodiazepine receptor agonists and daridorexant carry level A support for short-term use. This is an explicit guideline recommendation against ramelteon at the highest evidence level.

  2. Ramelteon for the treatment of insomnia in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Kuriyama et al., 2014 · PMID 24656909 · mixed

    13 published and unpublished placebo-controlled trials, 5,812 patients, mean duration 38 days: subjective sleep latency -4.30 min (95% CI -7.01 to -1.58) and sleep quality SMD -0.074 (95% CI -0.13 to -0.02); ramelteon was NOT associated with increased subjective total sleep time. Somnolence was the only significant adverse event. The authors concluded that short-term ramelteon improved some sleep parameters 'but its clinical impact is small'.

  3. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    De Crescenzo et al., 2022 · PMID 35843245 · negative

    170 trials, 47,950 participants: 'Melatonin, ramelteon, and non-licensed drugs did not show overall material benefits.' Benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, zolpidem and zopiclone were more efficacious than melatonin, ramelteon and zaleplon (SMD 0.27-0.71), and intermediate-acting benzodiazepines, long-acting benzodiazepines and eszopiclone had fewer all-cause discontinuations than ramelteon (OR 0.72, 0.70 and 0.71 respectively). For long-term treatment, eszopiclone was more effective than ramelteon (SMD 0.63, 95% CI 0.16-1.10, very low certainty).

  4. Ramelteon in the treatment of chronic insomnia: systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Liu & Wang, 2012 · PMID 22897464 · mixed

    8 randomised controlled trials selected from 175 references: significant improvements in subjective and polysomnographic sleep latency, total sleep time and latency to REM, with no increased risk of frequent adverse events versus control. Critically, subgroup analysis showed subjective sleep latency was reduced only in patients aged 18-64 and NOT in patients over 65 years.

  5. Pharmacotherapies for sleep disturbances in Alzheimer's disease.
    McCleery et al., 2014 · PMID 24659320 · null

    Cochrane review: a phase 2 trial of ramelteon 8 mg nightly in 74 participants with mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease found no effect on total nocturnal sleep time at one week (the primary outcome) or at eight weeks, with few significant differences from placebo on any sleep, behavioural or cognitive outcome and none likely to be clinically significant. No serious adverse effects were reported.

  6. Efficacy and safety of 6-month nightly ramelteon administration in adults with chronic primary insomnia.
    Mayer et al., 2009 · PMID 19294955 · positive

    451 adults with chronic primary insomnia randomised to ramelteon 8 mg or placebo nightly for 6 months across 46 sites: ramelteon consistently reduced latency to persistent sleep versus baseline and placebo, significant at week 1 and months 1, 3, 5 and 6 (P<0.05), and reduced subjective sleep latency versus placebo at week 1, month 1 and month 5 (P<0.05, nearing significance at months 3 and 6, P<=0.08). No significant next-morning residual effects and no withdrawal symptoms or rebound insomnia after discontinuation. The strongest positive evidence for this drug, and unusually long for the field.

  7. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline.
    Sateia et al., 2017 · PMID 27998379 · positive

    'We suggest that clinicians use ramelteon as a treatment for sleep onset insomnia (versus no treatment) in adults. (WEAK)'. The recommendation is limited to sleep onset and is graded WEAK; in the same guideline melatonin itself received a recommendation against. This older US guideline is the main source pointing the other way from the 2023 European guideline.

  8. Greater incidence of depression with hypnotic use than with placebo.
    Kripke, 2007 · PMID 17711589 · negative

    Incidence of depression compiled from FDA-approved prescribing information and New Drug Application documents for zolpidem, zaleplon, eszopiclone and ramelteon: 5,535 patients randomised to a hypnotic versus 2,318 randomised to placebo, with depression incidence 2.0% on active drug versus 0.9% on placebo (p<0.002). The author noted that limitations in the FDA data prevented a formal meta-analysis and that drop-out rates and depression definitions were not available.
