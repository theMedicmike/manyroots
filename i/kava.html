Herbs

Kava (Piper methysticum)

Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Not supported by evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Kava is a South Pacific root with a genuinely calming effect, and older, smaller studies found people taking it reported less anxiety. But the biggest and most careful modern study found it worked no better than a dummy pill for diagnosed anxiety disorder, two independent reviews reached the same conclusion, and kava carries a rare but real risk of serious liver injury that led several countries to ban it for a time. If you consider it at all, it should be short-term, without alcohol, and with your doctor aware.

Safety

Kava's defining safety issue is rare, idiosyncratic, but potentially severe liver injury, reported worldwide with both traditional aqueous and Western solvent extracts; this drove European regulatory bans in the early 2000s (Germany's was later overturned in court). Short-term trial monitoring is reassuring — a 6-week RCT found no liver-test differences versus placebo — but the 16-week trial found liver-function-test abnormalities significantly more often on kava, plus significantly more tremor/shakiness and poorer memory. Heavy chronic use is also associated with a reversible scaly skin condition (kava dermopathy). Anyone using kava should limit duration, avoid alcohol, and stop immediately if jaundice, dark urine, or unusual fatigue develops; periodic liver-enzyme checks are prudent.

Who should be careful: Do not use with pre-existing liver disease, heavy alcohol use, or alongside hepatotoxic medications; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding; avoid in Parkinson's disease due to possible dopamine-antagonist activity. Kava's legal status varies by country — it has been restricted or banned in several jurisdictions.

Interactions: Additive sedation with benzodiazepines, alcohol, and other CNS depressants; potential interactions via liver enzyme (CYP450) inhibition with many prescription drugs; avoid combining with other potentially hepatotoxic supplements. Do not drive until individual response is known, given the memory and tremor signals in the 16-week trial.

What the evidence says

The trial record for kava in anxiety is extensive but conflicting. A Cochrane review of 11 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (645 participants), searched only through August 2002, found a significant HAM-A reduction versus placebo (weighted mean difference 5.0 points, 95% CI 1.1-8.8, p=0.01, from six trials totalling 345 patients) over 1-24 weeks, and an 8-week reference-controlled trial in 129 GAD outpatients found kava extract LI 150 indistinguishable from buspirone and opipramol (about 75% responders and 60% remission in each arm) — but that trial had no placebo arm, so it establishes similarity, not efficacy. The modern evidence in diagnosed GAD is null: a pooled analysis of three US placebo-controlled trials (n=64) found no effect for kava and a significant effect favouring placebo among participants with higher baseline anxiety, and the largest and longest trial — a 16-week phase III RCT in 171 non-medicated adults with GAD using an aqueous extract standardized to 240 mg kavalactones/day — found no difference from placebo (relative reduction of 1.37 HAM-A points favouring placebo, p=0.25; remission 17.4% vs 23.8%), with liver-function-test abnormalities, poorer memory and tremor significantly more frequent on kava, though no participant met criteria for herb-induced liver injury. Two independent syntheses agree: a 2018 systematic review's meta-analysis of three placebo-controlled GAD trials (n=130) found effect sizes of 0.59-0.99 that did not reach statistical significance and concluded the evidence is insufficient to confirm an effect beyond placebo, and a 2022 network meta-analysis found kava effective overall (MD -2.46, 95% CrI -4.47 to -0.32) but possibly ineffective specifically in GAD (MD -0.17, 95% CrI -2.55 to -1.97). On short-term safety, a 6-week RCT (n=75) found no liver-function differences versus placebo and no evidence of withdrawal or addiction. Rare severe liver injury has nonetheless been reported worldwide with both traditional aqueous and Western acetonic/ethanolic extracts, prompting European regulatory bans in the early 2000s; the mechanism remains unresolved, with mould hepatotoxins among proposed but unproven culprits.

Doses studied: Standardized extracts delivering roughly 120-280 mg kavalactones/day (LI 150 400 mg extract/day; aqueous extract 240 mg kavalactones/day in the largest trial), for 1-24 weeks; the longest controlled trial ran 16 weeks.

Why this grade: Downgraded from moderate. The positive evidence is old and small: the Cochrane meta-analysis searched only to August 2002 and its significant result rests on six older trials (n=345), and the 8-week comparison against buspirone and opipramol had no placebo arm. Every rigorous modern dataset in diagnosed GAD is null — a pooled analysis of three US placebo-controlled trials, the largest and longest trial (n=171, 16 weeks, numerically favoring placebo), a 2018 review whose pooled effect sizes did not reach significance, and a 2022 network meta-analysis finding kava possibly ineffective specifically in GAD. A signal may persist for short-term situational anxiety, which is the only reason this is not graded not_supported. The documented hepatotoxicity story limits the grade further and dominates the risk-benefit conversation regardless of efficacy.

Sources (8)
  1. Kava extract for treating anxiety.
    Pittler MH & Ernst E, 2003 · PMID 12535473 · positive

    Cochrane review of 11 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (645 participants), searches updated to August 2002: meta-analysis of six trials using total HAM-A score suggests a significant reduction with kava versus placebo (weighted mean difference 5.0 points, 95% CI 1.1-8.8, p=0.01, n=345). Adverse events reported in the reviewed trials were mild, transient and infrequent, and the authors judged kava relatively safe for short-term use (1-24 weeks) while calling for further investigation of long-term safety. Predates all rigorous modern GAD trials.

  2. Kava in generalized anxiety disorder: three placebo-controlled trials.
    Connor KM et al., 2006 · PMID 16877894 · null

    Pooled analysis of three randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled US trials in DSM-IV GAD (n=64; kava 28, placebo 30, venlafaxine 6): no significant differences between treatment groups in any individual trial, no effect found for kava in the pooled analyses, and a significant effect favouring PLACEBO among participants with higher baseline anxiety. No evidence of hepatotoxicity. The authors conclude the findings do not support the use of kava in DSM-IV GAD.

  3. Kava for generalised anxiety disorder: A 16-week double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled study.
    Sarris J et al., 2020 · PMID 31813230 · null

    Largest kava GAD trial: 171 non-medicated adults, aqueous root extract standardized to 120 mg kavalactones twice daily, 16 weeks. Anxiety reduction did not differ from placebo (relative reduction of 1.37 points favouring placebo, p=0.25); remission was 17.4% on kava versus 23.8% on placebo (p=0.46). Poorer memory (36 vs 23, p=0.044), tremor/shakiness (36 vs 23, p=0.024), and liver-function-test abnormalities were significantly more frequent on kava, though no participant met criteria for herb-induced hepatic injury. The authors conclude this extract was not effective for diagnosed GAD.

  4. Kava for Generalized Anxiety Disorder: A Review of Current Evidence.
    Ooi SL et al., 2018 · PMID 29641222 · null

    Systematic review of 12 articles: supportive evidence came from two placebo-controlled trials and one reference-controlled trial, while one negative trial found kava no more effective than placebo. Meta-analysis of three placebo-controlled trials (n=130) favoured kava with standardized mean differences between 0.59 and 0.99 but WITHOUT reaching statistical significance. The authors conclude current evidence, although promising, is insufficient to confirm the effect of kava for GAD beyond placebo.

  5. Medicinal herbs for the treatment of anxiety: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Zhang W et al., 2022 · PMID 35378276 · mixed

    Independent Bayesian network meta-analysis of 29 RCTs comparing 12 medicinal herbs: kava was an effective anxiolytic overall (HAM-A MD -2.46, 95% CrI -4.47 to -0.32) but possibly ineffective in patients with generalized anxiety disorder specifically (MD -0.17, 95% CrI -2.55 to -1.97). The authors describe all findings as preliminary given unconvincing sample sizes and potential placebo effectiveness.

  6. Kava-Kava extract LI 150 is as effective as Opipramol and Buspirone in Generalised Anxiety Disorder--an 8-week randomized, double-blind multi-centre clinical trial in 129 out-patients.
    Boerner RJ et al., 2003 · PMID 12807341 · mixed

    In 129 GAD outpatients (127 ITT) randomized to kava LI 150 400 mg, buspirone 10 mg, or opipramol 100 mg daily for 8 weeks, NO significant differences were observed on any efficacy or safety measure; about 75% of each group responded (>=50% HAM-A reduction) and about 60% achieved full remission. There was no placebo arm, so equivalence to two active drugs in an 8-week trial cannot establish efficacy over placebo.

  7. Kava for the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder RCT: analysis of adverse reactions, liver function, addiction, and sexual effects.
    Sarris J et al., 2013 · PMID 23348842 · positive

    In a 6-week double-blind RCT (n=75, 120 mg kavalactones/day titrated to 240 mg in non-responders), there were no significant differences versus placebo in liver function tests, no significant adverse reactions attributable to kava, and no differences in withdrawal or addiction between groups. Kava significantly increased female sexual drive on an ASEX sub-domain (p=0.040) with no negative effects in males.

  8. Herbal hepatotoxicity by kava: update on pipermethystine, flavokavain B, and mould hepatotoxins as primarily assumed culprits.
    Teschke R et al., 2011 · PMID 21377431 · negative

    Review of the kava hepatotoxicity story: rare liver injury occurred worldwide with traditional aqueous extracts in the South Pacific and with acetonic and ethanolic extracts in Western countries, suggesting the solvent used plays no major causative role. Pipermethystine was not detectable in commercial Western extracts and flavokavain B was present at levels far too low to cause experimental liver injury; contaminant mould hepatotoxins such as aflatoxins are proposed but unproven, and the mechanism remains unresolved.

For insomnia

Kava has a real reputation for calming anxiety, but even that is shakier than it looks: the biggest and longest trial ever run found it worked no better than a dummy pill. For sleep itself the picture is clearer — a 391-person trial found no benefit for insomnia, and a review pooling 14 trials came to the same conclusion. It also carries a serious liver-safety history: kava was banned across the European Union and Canada after cases of liver failure that led to transplants and deaths, and the largest modern trial found abnormal liver blood tests significantly more often in the people taking it. We would not recommend it for insomnia.

Safety

Kava carries the most serious safety signal in this group and it is the reason to treat it differently from an ordinary herbal sleep aid. By January 2003 kava extracts had been banned in the entire European Union and Canada and were subject to US FDA cautions, after 11 cases of hepatic failure leading to liver transplants including four deaths; 78 hepatotoxicity cases in total were available for review, of which 4 were probably attributable to kavalactones alone and 23 potentially attributable but complicated by co-ingestion of other hepatotoxic compounds. A 2021 systematic review of 936 herb-induced liver injury cases names kava among the most frequently implicated supplements. The injury pattern appears idiosyncratic, meaning it is not reliably dose-predictable and cannot be avoided simply by taking less. Crucially, the reassurance offered by short trials does not survive longer ones: the 4-week WS 1490 trial reported no drug-related adverse events or laboratory changes, and the insomnia meta-analysis found similar or fewer adverse events with kava than placebo, but the 16-week phase III GAD trial found LIVER FUNCTION TEST ABNORMALITIES SIGNIFICANTLY MORE FREQUENT IN THE KAVA GROUP, along with significantly more reports of poorer memory and tremor/shakiness — although no participant in that trial met criteria for herb-induced hepatic injury. In fairness to the toxicology literature, the 2004 review concluded that direct toxicity of kava extracts is small and that its risk-benefit ratio compares acceptably with prescription anxiolytics; the concern is idiosyncratic injury and interaction-driven potentiation rather than predictable dose toxicity. Legal status varies by country; check local regulation before purchase.

Who should be careful: Do not use with existing liver disease, hepatitis, elevated liver enzymes, regular alcohol use, or concurrent hepatotoxic medication. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Kava is banned or restricted in a number of jurisdictions, so availability alone is not a safety signal. Given that the best sleep-specific evidence is null, that the anxiety evidence has itself been contradicted by the largest trial in the field, and that the liver risk is real, kava is not a reasonable choice for insomnia; anyone using it despite that should do so under clinical supervision with liver-function monitoring, and stop immediately at any sign of jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain or unexplained fatigue.

Interactions: Substantial and mechanistically grounded. Kavalactones inhibit cytochrome P450, so kava can alter levels of the many prescription drugs metabolized by that system — a broad interaction risk, not a narrow one. Kava also reduces liver glutathione content, which lowers the liver's defence against other hepatotoxic agents; the reviewed case series specifically noted that many hepatotoxicity cases involved concomitant ingestion of other potentially hepatotoxic compounds, and the toxicology review judged the potential for drug interactions and potentiation of other compounds' toxicity to be large even while judging direct toxicity small. Do not combine kava with alcohol, acetaminophen, statins, methotrexate or any other hepatotoxic drug. Additive sedation with benzodiazepines, other sedative-hypnotics and alcohol is plausible. The phase III trial's finding of increased tremor and memory complaints suggests caution with driving, machinery and other centrally acting drugs. Anyone on prescription medication should consult a clinician or pharmacist before using kava.

What the evidence says

The sleep-specific evidence for kava is null. Jacobs et al. (2005) randomized 391 adults with both anxiety and insomnia to kava (n=121), valerian (n=135) or double placebo (n=135) for 28 days with the Insomnia Severity Index as a co-primary outcome; the placebo group improved by 14.4 points on the STAI-State and 8.3 points on the ISI, and neither kava nor valerian relieved anxiety or insomnia more than placebo, with kava 2.7 points worse than placebo on the anxiety scale (95% CI -0.8 to +6.2, a confidence interval crossing zero) and results unchanged among the 83% fully adherent. Leach and Page (2015), a systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 RCTs in 1602 participants with insomnia covering valerian, chamomile, kava and wuling, found no statistically significant difference between any herbal medicine and placebo or active control on any of thirteen measures of clinical efficacy. The one positive trial is Lehrl (2004): 61 patients with sleep disturbance associated with non-psychotic anxiety, tension and restlessness received kava extract WS 1490 200 mg/day or placebo for 4 weeks, with significant gains in SF-B 'Quality of sleep' (p=0.007) and 'Recuperative effect after sleep' (p=0.018) — a small trial in an anxiety population rather than primary insomnia. The anxiety indication is also less secure than commonly stated: a 2003 Cochrane review of 11 trials in 645 participants found a weighted mean difference of 5.0 points on the Hamilton Anxiety scale favouring kava (95% CI 1.1 to 8.8, p=0.01, n=345), but a subsequent 16-week phase III trial in 171 people with diagnosed GAD found a non-significant difference favouring placebo by 1.37 points (p=0.25) with remission in 17.4% versus 23.8%. Safety is decisive: by January 2003 kava extracts had been banned across the European Union and Canada and carried US FDA advisories following 11 cases of hepatic failure leading to liver transplants including four deaths out of 78 reviewed hepatotoxicity cases, and the phase III trial found liver function test abnormalities significantly more frequent in the kava group.

Doses studied: For sleep, kava extract WS 1490 200 mg/day (kavalactone-standardized) for 4 weeks was the dose in the single positive trial. The null insomnia trial used a kava capsule preparation over 28 days. The null phase III anxiety trial used an aqueous dried-root extract standardized to 120 mg kavalactones twice daily for 16 weeks. Cochrane anxiety trials ran 1-24 weeks. No dose has been shown to be both effective for insomnia and safe over the long term, and no dose eliminates the idiosyncratic liver risk.

Why this grade: For SLEEP specifically, adequate evidence exists and it is null. A 391-participant randomized placebo-controlled trial with the Insomnia Severity Index as a co-primary outcome found kava did not relieve insomnia more than placebo, and a systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 RCTs in 1602 participants with insomnia found no statistically significant difference between any herbal medicine, kava included, and placebo on any of thirteen measures of clinical efficacy. The single positive sleep trial (n=61) studied sleep disturbance secondary to anxiety disorders, not primary insomnia. The fallback argument that kava's anxiety benefit might carry over is itself weak: although a 2003 Cochrane review found an effect, the largest and longest trial since — a 16-week phase III study in 171 people with diagnosed GAD — found kava no better than placebo, and a 2018 network meta-regression found only a non-significant effect. Combined with documented hepatotoxicity that produced regulatory bans, liver transplants and deaths, and with significantly more liver-function abnormalities in the kava arm of that phase III trial, 'not_supported' is the honest grade for insomnia.

  4. Clinical efficacy of kava extract WS 1490 in sleep disturbances associated with anxiety disorders. Results of a multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial.
    Lehrl S, 2004 · PMID 14706720 · positive

    The one positive sleep trial, and a small one. 61 patients with sleep disturbance associated with non-psychotic anxiety, tension and restlessness received WS 1490 200 mg/day or placebo for 4 weeks. Both primary variables favoured kava: SF-B 'Quality of sleep' (p=0.007) and 'Recuperative effect after sleep' (p=0.018), plus HAMA psychic anxiety (p=0.002).   5. Kava kava: examining new reports of toxicity.
    Clouatre DL, 2004 · PMID 15068826 · negative

    HEPATOTOXICITY. By January 2003 kava extracts were banned in the entire European Union and Canada and subject to US FDA cautions and advisories, following 11 cases of hepatic failure leading to liver transplants, including four deaths. 78 hepatotoxicity cases were available for review; 4 probably linked to kavalactones taken alone, 23 potentially linked but involving concomitant ingestion of other potentially hepatotoxic compounds. Three proposed mechanisms: cytochrome P450 inhibition, reduced liver glutathione and cyclooxygenase inhibition. Toxicity appears idiosyncratic. In fairness to the source, the author also concludes that direct toxicity of kava extracts 'is quite small under any analysis' and that the risk-to-benefit ratio 'remains good in comparison with that of other drugs used to treat anxiety' — while stressing that the potential for drug interactions and potentiation of other compounds' toxicity is large.

  6. Herb-induced liver injury: Systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Ballotin VR, Bigarella LG, Brandao ABM, Balbinot RA, Balbinot SS, Soldera J, 2021 · PMID 34307603 · negative

    SAFETY, NEGATIVE. Systematic review of 446 references and 936 cases of herb-induced liver injury across 79 herbs or herbal compounds. Kava kava is named among the most commonly reported supplements causing herb-induced liver injury. Across ALL herbs in the review, 82.8% of cases had complete clinical recovery, but 6.6% required liver transplantation, 10.4% died and 1.5% developed chronic liver disease — figures for the whole case series, not kava specifically, and drawn from published case reports, which over-represent severe outcomes.

  7. Kava extract for treating anxiety.
    Pittler MH, Ernst E, 2003 · PMID 12535473 · positive

    POSITIVE, BUT FOR ANXIETY NOT SLEEP, AND SUPERSEDED IN PRACTICE. Cochrane review of 11 randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trials, 645 participants. Meta-analysis of six studies using Hamilton Anxiety scale total score found a weighted mean difference of 5.0 favouring kava (95% CI 1.1 to 8.8, p=0.01, n=345). Adverse events were mild, transient and infrequent; authors judged kava relatively safe for short-term treatment (1-24 weeks) but explicitly called for further investigation of the long-term safety profile. No withdrawal notice for this review was found in PubMed, but it predates both the hepatotoxicity reports and the 2019 null phase III GAD trial, and should not be read as the current state of the anxiety evidence.

  8. Complementary and Alternative Medicine Treatments for Generalized Anxiety Disorder: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Baric H, Dordevic V, Cerovecki I, Trkulja V, 2018 · PMID 29508154 · null

    NOT SIGNIFICANT FOR KAVA. Systematic review of 32 RCTs of complementary and alternative treatments in GAD. Direct comparisons of Piper methysticum extracts to placebo (4 quality trials, n=233) were highly heterogeneous; network meta-regression reduced heterogeneity and suggested only a modest and NON-SIGNIFICANT kava effect (baseline- and duration-adjusted Hamilton Anxiety difference -3.24, 95% CI -6.65 to 0.17, p=0.059; kava 4 arms n=139, placebo 5 arms n=359). By contrast lavender extract and a hawthorn/California poppy/magnesium combination were superior to placebo. Authors note safety reporting across this literature was poor.
