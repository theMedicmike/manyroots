Modafinil and stimulants

Not supported by evidence · for fatigue & low energy

For fatigue & low energy

ImportantIf one of these was prescribed for you, don't stop or change it because of this grade, which is about fatigue, not the conditions they are prescribed for, such as narcolepsy. Talk to your prescriber.

Wakefulness drugs like modafinil, and stimulants like methylphenidate, are sometimes prescribed off-label to help people push through fatigue. When they have been tested properly against a dummy pill in people with real, severe fatigue, most of the large trials found no difference from placebo, and more people on the drug reported side effects. They can help with genuine sleepiness in specific diagnosed sleep disorders, but that is a different problem from the bone-deep tiredness this page is about.

Safety

These are prescription medicines with measurable harms, and the trials that failed to show benefit still showed side effects. In the multiple sclerosis crossover, adverse events were reported by 40% on modafinil, 40% on methylphenidate and 39% on amantadine versus 31% on placebo, and three serious adverse events occurred (pulmonary embolism and myocarditis on amantadine, and a multiple sclerosis exacerbation requiring hospital admission on modafinil). In advanced cancer, grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 40 of 93 patients on methylphenidate versus 29 of 97 on placebo (p=0.06). In an English primary-care cohort of 1,096 patients prescribed modafinil, causality assessment confirmed that modafinil can induce cardiac, psychiatric and skin events in individual patients, with some events including psychosis occurring only at the higher dose; the UK licence has since been restricted to narcolepsy alone after a safety review. Real-world use is heavily off-label: 34% of that same cohort were being treated for multiple sclerosis, outside the licence.

Who should be careful: Not for self-medication, and not something to source without a prescriber. Anyone with cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, anxiety or psychotic illness, or a history of stimulant misuse should not start these without specialist assessment. Safety in pregnancy is not established, and skin events have been causally linked to modafinil in individual patients, so seek medical advice promptly if a rash develops. Using a stimulant to override fatigue before looking for a cause risks masking a treatable or serious condition, and none of these drugs address why the fatigue is there.

Interactions: Modafinil induces CYP3A4/5 activity in humans: in a controlled study of 41 women on a combined oral contraceptive, four weeks of modafinil markedly reduced exposure to triazolam and modestly reduced exposure to ethinyl estradiol. This matters in practice, not just on paper - in a real-world cohort fewer than half of women of childbearing potential prescribed modafinil were on a recommended contraceptive programme and three became pregnant while taking it with the oral contraceptive pill, so confirm contraceptive cover with a prescriber before, during and after treatment. The same induction can lower levels of other CYP3A4/5 substrates, so a full medication review is warranted. Stimulants add to caffeine and other sympathomimetics on heart rate, blood pressure and sleep, and the resulting sleep disruption can worsen the fatigue they are being taken for.

What the evidence says

In a phase 3 trial of 867 randomised patients (631 evaluable) with fatigue during chemotherapy, modafinil 200 mg daily showed a significant interaction with baseline fatigue (p=0.017) such that benefit appeared only in the severe-fatigue subgroup (n=458), with no benefit in mild or moderate fatigue and no effect on depression. In 208 randomised patients with advanced non-small-cell lung cancer, FACIT-Fatigue improved on both arms but the between-group difference was 0.20 (95% CI -3.56 to 3.97), and the authors concluded modafinil had no effect on cancer-related fatigue and should not be prescribed outside a trial. A four-period crossover trial in 141 people with multiple sclerosis found mean Modified Fatigue Impact Scale scores of 40.6 on placebo, 41.3 on amantadine, 39.0 on modafinil and 38.6 on methylphenidate (p=0.20), with adverse events in 39-40% on each drug versus 31% on placebo; a placebo-controlled trial of methylphenidate in 141 patients with advanced cancer likewise found no separation from placebo (median FACIT-Fatigue improvement 5.5 versus 6.0, p=0.69), and the only modafinil trial in chronic fatigue syndrome, a 14-patient crossover at 200 and 400 mg/day, found no effect on self-rated fatigue, quality of life or mood and worse performance on mental flexibility and motor speed at the higher dose. The positive signals are narrow: a 4-week crossover in 60 patients meeting 1994 CDC criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome found methylphenidate 10 mg twice daily better than placebo on fatigue (Checklist Individual Strength mean difference -9.7, p<0.0001) but only 17% met the prespecified clinically significant response threshold, and a Cochrane review of 45 trials, 18 drugs and 4,696 participants found a superior pooled effect for methylphenidate in cancer-related fatigue (SMD 0.49, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.83) while concluding that no specific drug can be recommended. A separate meta-analysis of 8 randomised trials found modafinil and armodafinil do improve residual excessive sleepiness, attention and clinical global impression in adequately CPAP-treated sleep apnea, without confirmed improvement in quality of life or other cognitive domains and with significantly more dropouts than placebo, which is a sleepiness indication rather than evidence for general fatigue.

Doses studied: Modafinil 200 mg daily (chemotherapy trial); modafinil 100 mg daily for days 1-14 then 200 mg daily for days 15-28 (lung cancer trial); modafinil 200 mg/day and 400 mg/day for 20-day periods (chronic fatigue syndrome crossover); modafinil up to 100 mg twice daily, methylphenidate up to 10 mg twice daily and amantadine up to 100 mg twice daily, each for up to 6 weeks (multiple sclerosis crossover); methylphenidate 5 mg every 2 hours as needed up to 20 mg daily (advanced cancer trial); methylphenidate 10 mg twice daily for 4 weeks (chronic fatigue syndrome crossover).

Why this grade: Adequate, well-powered placebo-controlled trials exist in the populations where these drugs are most often prescribed for fatigue (cancer, multiple sclerosis and chronic fatigue syndrome) and the largest are null, with more adverse events on drug than placebo. No trial has tested them in undifferentiated persistent fatigue in otherwise healthy adults. The positive signals are narrow: a 60-person chronic fatigue syndrome crossover in which only 17% met the clinically significant response threshold, and a modest pooled estimate for methylphenidate in cancer-related fatigue that the Cochrane reviewers themselves judged insufficient to recommend any specific drug. Modafinil does have supportive evidence for residual excessive sleepiness in CPAP-treated sleep apnea, but sleepiness is a different target from fatigue and that indication is not what this entry grades.

Sources (10)
  1. A phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, clinical trial of the effect of modafinil on cancer-related fatigue among 631 patients receiving chemotherapy: a University of Rochester Cancer Center Community Clinical Oncology Program Research base study.
    Randomised trialJean-Pierre et al., 2010 · PMID 20564068 · mixed

    Among 867 randomised and 631 evaluable patients, modafinil 200 mg daily showed no overall benefit on fatigue; a significant treatment-by-baseline-fatigue interaction (p=0.017) meant only patients with severe baseline fatigue (n=458) benefited, while those with mild or moderate fatigue did not, and there was no effect on depression (p>0.05).

  2. Modafinil for the treatment of fatigue in lung cancer: results of a placebo-controlled, double-blind, randomized trial.
    Randomised trialSpathis et al., 2014 · PMID 24778393 · null

    In 208 randomised adults with advanced non-small-cell lung cancer, FACIT-Fatigue improved similarly in both arms over 28 days (modafinil 5.29, placebo 5.09) with a between-group difference of 0.20 (95% CI -3.56 to 3.97) and no difference in secondary outcomes including depression, sleepiness and quality of life; the authors concluded modafinil had no effect on cancer-related fatigue and should not be prescribed outside a clinical trial setting, its use being associated with a clinically significant placebo effect.

  3. Methylphenidate and/or a nursing telephone intervention for fatigue in patients with advanced cancer: a randomized, placebo-controlled, phase II trial.
    Randomised trialBruera et al., 2013 · PMID 23690414 · null

    Among 141 evaluable patients with advanced cancer and fatigue of at least 4/10, median FACIT-Fatigue improvement did not differ between methylphenidate and placebo (5.5 versus 6.0, p=0.69) or across all four groups (p=0.16); grade 3 or higher adverse events occurred in 40 of 93 on methylphenidate versus 29 of 97 on placebo (p=0.06).

  4. Safety and efficacy of amantadine, modafinil, and methylphenidate for fatigue in multiple sclerosis: a randomised, placebo-controlled, crossover, double-blind trial.
    Journal articleNourbakhsh et al., 2021 · PMID 33242419 · null

    In 141 randomised patients (136 analysed) with multiple sclerosis fatigue, estimated mean Modified Fatigue Impact Scale scores at maximal tolerated dose were 40.6 on placebo, 41.3 on amantadine, 39.0 on modafinil and 38.6 on methylphenidate (p=0.20), with adverse events in 39-40% on each drug versus 31% on placebo and three serious adverse events; the authors concluded the results do not support indiscriminate use.

  5. Chronic treatment with modafinil may not be beneficial in patients with chronic fatigue syndrome.
    Randomised trialRandall et al., 2005 · PMID 16272188 · null

    In 14 patients with chronic fatigue syndrome without major depression or significant sleepiness, a double-blind placebo-controlled crossover of modafinil 200 and 400 mg/day over 20-day treatment periods produced no effect on self-ratings of fatigue, quality of life or mood, only mixed effects across two cognitive tasks, and worse performance on tests of mental flexibility and motor speed at 400 mg compared with placebo.

  6. Does methylphenidate reduce the symptoms of chronic fatigue syndrome?
    Randomised trialBlockmans et al., 2006 · PMID 16443425 · mixed

    In a 4-week double-blind crossover trial in 60 patients meeting 1994 CDC criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome, methylphenidate 10 mg twice daily beat placebo on fatigue (Checklist Individual Strength mean difference -9.7, p<0.0001; visual analogue scale -1.0, p=0.001) and concentration, but only 17% of patients achieved the prespecified clinically significant improvement in fatigue and 22% in concentration.

  7. Pharmacological treatments for fatigue associated with palliative care.
    Meta-analysisMucke et al., 2015 · PMID 26026155 · mixed

    Across 45 included studies, 18 drugs and 4,696 participants, meta-analysis showed a superior estimated effect for methylphenidate in cancer-related fatigue (SMD 0.49, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.83) and only weak, inconclusive evidence for amantadine, pemoline and modafinil in multiple sclerosis; the reviewers concluded that, based on limited evidence, no specific drug can be recommended for fatigue in palliative care.

  8. Pharmacotherapy for residual excessive sleepiness and cognition in CPAP-treated patients with obstructive sleep apnea syndrome: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisAvellar et al., 2016 · PMID 27865102 · mixed

    Across 8 randomised trials in adults with obstructive sleep apnea and residual excessive sleepiness despite adequate CPAP use, modafinil and armodafinil improved excessive daytime sleepiness, attention/alertness and clinical condition on the CGI-C, but no improvement could be confirmed in quality of life or other cognitive domains including memory, executive function and language; no severe adverse effects occurred but the drugs were associated with significant dropout rates compared with placebo.

  9. Safety profile of modafinil across a range of prescribing indications, including off-label use, in a primary care setting in England: results of a modified prescription-event monitoring study.
    Journal articleDavies et al., 2013 · PMID 23483377 · negative

    In a cohort of 1,096 English primary-care patients prescribed modafinil, 34% had an indication of multiple sclerosis (outside the licence); fewer than half of women of childbearing potential were established on a recommended contraceptive programme and three became pregnant while taking modafinil with the oral contraceptive pill; causality assessment confirmed the potential of modafinil to induce cardiac, psychiatric and skin events in individual patients, additional events including psychosis occurred at the higher dose only, and the UK licence has since been restricted to treating narcolepsy alone following a safety review.

  10. Effect of modafinil on the pharmacokinetics of ethinyl estradiol and triazolam in healthy volunteers.
    Randomised trialRobertson et al., 2002 · PMID 11823757 · negative

    In 41 women on long-term combined oral contraception (ethinyl estradiol 0.035 mg with norgestimate), modafinil 200 mg for 7 days followed by 400 mg for 21 days produced a marked decrease in maximum plasma concentration and area under the curve for triazolam and a much smaller decrease for ethinyl estradiol, demonstrating in vivo induction of CYP3A4/5 (more gastrointestinal than hepatic) and potential for metabolic drug-drug interactions.
