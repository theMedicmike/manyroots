Herbs

St John's Wort (Hypericum perforatum)

Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood

For depression & low mood

St John's wort has more human research behind it than any other herb for low mood, and for mild to moderate depression it has generally done about as well as prescription antidepressants with fewer side effects. But the two biggest and most rigorous American studies found it worked no better than a sugar pill, so the picture is genuinely mixed. The bigger issue is that it speeds up how your liver clears many medications, which can quietly make birth control, transplant drugs, HIV medication, blood thinners and others stop working - so this is one to discuss with a doctor or pharmacist before starting, not to try on your own.

Safety

On its own St John's wort is usually well tolerated - trials consistently show fewer dropouts for side effects than with SSRIs (OR 0.53) or older antidepressants (OR 0.24). In the 11-centre US trial, headache was the only adverse event that occurred more often than on placebo (41% vs 25%). Other reported effects include gastrointestinal upset, restlessness, dry mouth, dizziness and photosensitivity (increased sunburn risk). The real hazard is not the herb's own side effects but what it does to other medicines. Anyone taking a prescription medication should treat St John's wort as requiring a pharmacist or physician review before starting AND before stopping, since stopping it can allow the levels of a co-prescribed drug to rise again.

Who should be careful: Do not use alongside immunosuppressants after organ transplant (ciclosporin, tacrolimus) - a documented interaction with ciclosporin caused acute rejection in two heart transplant patients. Do not use with HIV protease inhibitors such as indinavir. Do not combine with anticancer medicines without oncology advice, since St John's wort induces the enzymes and transporters that clear many of them. Not appropriate as a self-directed treatment for bipolar depression: as with any antidepressant-acting agent it may precipitate hypomania, mania or mood cycling, and case reports document this; anyone with a personal or family history of mania should be assessed before use. Not established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Not established for severe depression - the trial literature does not cover it. Not appropriate for depression with suicidal thinking, or as a substitute for urgent care.

Interactions: Potent inducer of CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein via the pregnane-X-receptor, with the degree of induction reported to track hyperforin content (note that this specific finding comes from a review authored largely by staff of a St John's wort manufacturer). Documented alterations in the pharmacokinetics of ciclosporin, digoxin, tacrolimus, indinavir, warfarin, alprazolam, simvastatin and oral contraceptives - meaning breakthrough bleeding and contraceptive failure, loss of anticoagulant control, and transplant-drug or antiretroviral failure are all real risks. By the same mechanism, assume any medicine cleared by CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein may be affected, and check rather than guess. Combining with SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, triptans or other serotonergic drugs raises the risk of serotonin toxicity and should not be self-directed. Because low-hyperforin preparations are reported to induce enzymes much less, product choice may materially change interaction risk - but hyperforin content is rarely stated on the label, so this is not something a shopper can act on reliably.

What the evidence says

The 2008 Cochrane review (29 trials, 5,489 patients) found a response rate ratio versus placebo of 1.28 (95% CI 1.10-1.49) across nine larger trials and 1.87 (95% CI 1.22-2.87) across nine smaller trials, with equivalence to SSRIs (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.90-1.11; 12 trials) and to tri/tetracyclics (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90-1.15; 5 trials). Dropouts due to adverse effects were substantially lower than with older antidepressants (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.13-0.46) and with SSRIs (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.34-0.83). A 2016 systematic review of 35 studies (6,993 patients) reported RR 1.53 (95% CI 1.19-1.97) versus placebo with I-squared of 79%, graded moderate quality. Against this, the NIH-funded Hypericum Depression Trial (n=340) found full response in 23.9% on hypericum versus 31.9% on placebo (p=0.21), with sertraline also failing to beat placebo on the primary outcomes, and an 11-centre trial (n=200) found no treatment effect on the HAM-D (p=0.16 for treatment, p=0.58 for time-by-treatment). Cochrane explicitly notes that country of origin and trial precision both correlate with effect size, which complicates interpretation. Efficacy in severe depression has not been established, and both the Cochrane and 2016 reviews name that as an unfilled gap.

Doses studied: Most trials used a standardised hydroalcoholic extract at 300 mg three times daily (900 mg/day), typically standardised to ~0.3% hypericin. The two large US trials both started at 900 mg/day with dose escalation on inadequate response: up to 1200 mg/day in the 11-centre trial and up to 1500 mg/day in the 12-site NIH trial, each over 8 weeks. Hyperforin content, which drives the drug interactions, varies widely between commercial products and is often not disclosed on the label.

Why this grade: Multiple meta-analyses find St John's wort superior to placebo and comparable to SSRIs in mild-to-moderate depression, which would support a higher grade. It is held at moderate rather than strong for four documented reasons: (1) two large, well-conducted US multicentre placebo-controlled trials found no effect at all, one of which also failed to separate its sertraline comparator arm from placebo; (2) the Cochrane review reports marked heterogeneity and an explicit association between country of origin and effect size, with trials from German-speaking countries reporting more favourable results; (3) there is essentially no evidence in severe depression, which both Cochrane and the 2016 review flag as a gap; and (4) product-to-product variability means the meta-analytic results apply only to the specific extracts tested. Under the rule of taking the lower grade when torn, the two null large trials are decisive against 'strong'. The drug-interaction profile is a separate and serious practical limitation, not a grading factor, but it means this herb cannot be treated as a benign self-care option.

Sources (6)
  1. St John's wort for major depression.
    Linde K, Berner MM, Kriston L, 2008 (Cochrane Database Syst Rev) · PMID 18843608 · positive

    29 trials, 5,489 patients. Response rate ratio vs placebo 1.28 (95% CI 1.10-1.49) in 9 larger trials, 1.87 (95% CI 1.22-2.87) in 9 smaller trials; equivalent to SSRIs (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.90-1.11, 12 trials) and to tri/tetracyclics (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90-1.15, 5 trials); fewer dropouts for adverse effects than older antidepressants (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.13-0.46) and than SSRIs (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.34-0.83). Authors state that placebo-controlled results showed marked heterogeneity, that trials from German-speaking countries reported findings more favourable to hypericum, and that the association of country of origin and precision with effect size complicates interpretation.

  2. Effect of Hypericum perforatum (St John's wort) in major depressive disorder: a randomized controlled trial.
    Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group (Davidson JRT et al.), 2002 (JAMA) · PMID 11939866 · null

    340 outpatients, 12 US academic and community sites, 8 weeks, LI-160 extract 900-1500 mg/day, baseline HAM-D at least 20. Neither hypericum nor sertraline was significantly different from placebo on either primary outcome. Mean HAM-D change: placebo -9.20 (SE 0.67) vs hypericum -8.68 (SE 0.68), p=0.59; sertraline -10.53 (SE 0.72), p=0.18. Full response occurred in 31.9% on placebo vs 23.9% on hypericum (p=0.21) and 24.8% on sertraline (p=0.26). Authors state the complete absence of any trend suggestive of efficacy for hypericum is noteworthy.

  3. Effectiveness of St John's wort in major depression: a randomized controlled trial.
    Shelton RC, Keller MB, Gelenberg A, et al., 2001 (JAMA) · PMID 11308434 · null

    200 adults with major depression (baseline HAM-D at least 20) across 11 US academic medical centres; 900 mg/day for 4 weeks increased to 1200 mg/day without adequate response, 8 weeks total. Random coefficient analyses showed significant effects for time but not for treatment (HAM-D p=0.16) or time-by-treatment interaction (p=0.58). Response rates did not differ between groups. Remission was higher on hypericum (14/98, 14.3%) than placebo (5/102, 4.9%, p=0.02), but rates were very low in both arms. Headache was the only adverse event more frequent on hypericum than placebo (39/95, 41% vs 25/100, 25%). Conclusion: not effective for major depression.

  4. A systematic review of St. John's wort for major depressive disorder.
    Apaydin EA, Maher AR, Shanman R, et al., 2016 (Syst Rev) · PMID 27589952 · positive

    35 studies, 6,993 patients. More treatment responders than placebo: RR 1.53 (95% CI 1.19-1.97), I-squared 79%, 18 RCTs, n=2,922, moderate quality of evidence. No difference in effectiveness versus antidepressants (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90-1.14, 17 RCTs) with fewer adverse events (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.56-0.81, 11 RCTs). Authors flag heterogeneity, absence of research in severe depression, and poor adverse-event reporting as reasons the findings should be interpreted with caution.

  5. Clinical relevance of St. John's wort drug interactions revisited.
    Nicolussi S, Drewe J, Butterweck V, Meyer zu Schwabedissen HE, 2020 (Br J Pharmacol) · PMID 31742659 · negative

    Reviews 20 years of documented pharmacokinetic interactions. St John's wort activates the pregnane-X-receptor, inducing CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, and has altered the pharmacokinetics of ciclosporin, digoxin, tacrolimus, indinavir, warfarin, alprazolam, simvastatin and oral contraceptives. A pharmacokinetic interaction with ciclosporin caused acute rejection in two heart transplant patients in 2000. The degree of CYP3A4 induction correlates significantly with the hyperforin content of the preparation. CONFLICT OF INTEREST: three of the four authors are affiliated with Medical Research, Max Zeller Soehne AG, a commercial manufacturer of St John's wort preparations; the hyperforin-content conclusion commercially favours low-hyperforin products, so weigh it accordingly.

  6. Mania associated with St. John's wort.
    Nierenberg AA, Burt T, Matthews J, Weiss AP, 1999 (Biol Psychiatry) · PMID 10624554 · negative

    Two cases of mania temporally associated with St John's wort use. Authors conclude that, as with other antidepressant agents, it may precipitate hypomania, mania or increased cycling of mood states, particularly in patients with occult bipolar disorder, and that because the majority of users take it without any formal psychiatric evaluation the risk of hypericum-induced mania may be significant. They recommend physicians screen for a history of hypomania or mania before recommending it.
