Conventional medicine

CGRP monoclonal antibodies and gepants

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

These are the newest migraine drugs, and they work by blocking a nerve signalling molecule called CGRP. Some are monthly injections or an infusion taken to prevent attacks, some are daily or alternate-day tablets for prevention, and a couple are tablets you take during an attack. In large trials they genuinely beat a dummy treatment, but the average gain is modest - often one to two fewer migraine days a month than placebo - and a UK health technology review found no proof they work better than old, cheap topiramate. They are usually well tolerated, but in 2026 US regulators added a blood pressure warning to the whole class, so it is worth having your blood pressure checked if you start one.

Safety

Blood pressure is the live safety question. An FDA pharmacovigilance review through October 2025 identified 106 hypertension cases probably or possibly caused by CGRP blockers - two-thirds starting within 30 days, 40 needing new or increased antihypertensive treatment - and the US Prescribing Information for the entire CGRP ligand and receptor blocker class now describes hypertension risk under Warnings and Precautions. Against that, a registry study of 125 people on erenumab for 12-24 months found systolic pressure steady at 118-121 mmHg and diastolic at 78-80 mmHg with no hypertensive effect. The two findings are not reconciled, so have your blood pressure checked before starting and monitored afterwards, especially if you already have hypertension. Otherwise the class is generally well tolerated over 12-plus months, with only about 3% of people stopping for adverse events - though every study carrying that estimate was judged at severe risk of bias. Injection-site reactions are the most common problem with the subcutaneous antibodies and were reported by most users of some agents; constipation is a recognised labelled effect of erenumab in particular and can occasionally be severe. Nausea, somnolence, dry mouth and fatigue are reported with the gepants. Preventive benefit takes weeks to establish and the antibodies have long half-lives, so effects - and any adverse effects - persist after stopping.

Who should be careful: Hypersensitivity to the specific agent. People with significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease were largely excluded from the pivotal trials, so efficacy and safety in that group are not established. Uncontrolled or poorly controlled hypertension warrants caution given the new class labelling. Not established in pregnancy or breastfeeding; the monoclonal antibodies have long half-lives and would persist for months after the last dose, which matters if pregnancy is planned. Some gepants require dose adjustment or avoidance in severe hepatic or renal impairment.

Interactions: Monoclonal antibodies are not metabolised by cytochrome P450 and have few drug interactions. Gepants are CYP3A4 substrates: strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. ketoconazole, clarithromycin) raise exposure and strong inducers (e.g. rifampicin, carbamazepine, St John's wort) reduce it, so dose adjustment or avoidance may be required. Atogepant is also affected by OATP inhibitors. If a CGRP blocker raises blood pressure, an interaction with existing antihypertensive therapy is the practical consequence to watch.

What the evidence says

This class covers PREVENTIVE monoclonal antibodies (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) and gepants used either preventively (atogepant, rimegepant) or ACUTELY (ubrogepant, rimegepant); the two literatures must not be conflated. In the pivotal STRIVE trial (955 patients, baseline 8.3 migraine days/month), erenumab reduced monthly migraine days by 3.2 (70 mg) and 3.7 (140 mg) versus 1.8 on placebo, with 50%-or-greater response in 43.3% and 50.0% versus 26.6%. In ADVANCE (873 participants), atogepant 10-60 mg reduced monthly migraine days by 4.2-4.4 in weeks 9-12 versus 3.0 on placebo, with separation from placebo by the first post-dose day. For acute use the absolute effect is smaller: in ACHIEVE I (1,672 participants), 2-hour pain freedom was 19.2% with ubrogepant 50 mg and 21.2% with 100 mg versus 11.8% with placebo, and the 2024 acute network meta-analysis ranked eletriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan and zolmitriptan above both rimegepant and ubrogepant. A chronic-migraine network meta-analysis of 12 RCTs found all six included drugs beat placebo but concluded the absolute differences in headache/migraine days are, at best, modest (best estimate eptinezumab 300 mg, -2.46 monthly headache days, 95% CrI -3.23 to -1.69). The most important contrary finding is comparative: the 2024 NIHR health technology assessment of 11 RCTs and 7,352 adults with chronic migraine concluded it did not find evidence that CGRP monoclonal antibodies are more clinically effective and cost-effective than topiramate or onabotulinumtoxinA, and the 2025 ACP guideline rates all of its preventive recommendations conditional and low-certainty. Long-term safety evidence is limited: a 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies with 12 months or more of follow-up pooled 23% discontinuation for any reason and 3% for adverse events with no emergent signals, but judged every study at severe risk of bias. Separately, an FDA pharmacovigilance review to October 2025 identified 106 hypertension cases probably or possibly caused by CGRP blockers, and the US labelling for the entire class now describes hypertension risk in Warnings and Precautions; a 125-patient erenumab registry over 12-24 months found blood pressure stable and within normal range, so the two lines of evidence are not yet reconciled.

Doses studied: PREVENTIVE: erenumab 70 or 140 mg subcutaneously monthly; galcanezumab 120/240 mg; fremanezumab monthly or quarterly; eptinezumab 30, 100 or 300 mg intravenously; atogepant 10, 30 or 60 mg orally once daily; rimegepant 75 mg orally disintegrating tablet every other day. ACUTE: ubrogepant 50 or 100 mg per attack; rimegepant per attack.

Why this grade: Multiple large phase 3 RCTs across four monoclonal antibodies and several gepants show consistent, replicated separation from placebo for prevention, confirmed in network meta-analyses - but graded moderate rather than strong, deliberately. The absolute preventive benefit is roughly 1-2.5 headache days per month over placebo; the 2025 ACP guideline rates every one of its preventive recommendations conditional with LOW-certainty evidence; a 2024 NIHR health technology assessment found no evidence that these antibodies are more clinically effective or cost-effective than topiramate or onabotulinumtoxinA; the acute gepants are less efficacious than the best triptans; the 12-month-plus safety studies are all at severe risk of bias; and in 2026 the US labelling for the whole class added a hypertension warning.

Sources (10)
  1. A Controlled Trial of Erenumab for Episodic Migraine.
    Goadsby et al., 2017 (N Engl J Med, STRIVE) · PMID 29171821 · positive

    955 patients randomised, baseline 8.3 migraine days/month. Over months 4-6 migraine days fell by 3.2 (erenumab 70 mg) and 3.7 (140 mg) versus 1.8 with placebo (P<0.001 each). 50%-or-greater reduction: 43.3% and 50.0% versus 26.6% placebo. Acute migraine-specific medication days fell 1.1 and 1.6 versus 0.2. Adverse event rates were similar to placebo; authors note long-term safety and durability required further study.

  2. Time course of efficacy of atogepant for the preventive treatment of migraine: Results from the randomized, double-blind ADVANCE trial.
    Schwedt et al., 2022 (Cephalalgia) · PMID 34521260 · positive

    873 participants with 4-14 migraine days/month randomised to atogepant 10, 30 or 60 mg or placebo daily for 12 weeks. Weeks 1-4: -3.1 to -3.9 monthly migraine days across doses versus -1.6 placebo. Weeks 9-12: -4.2 to -4.4 versus -3.0 placebo (P<0.0002). Migraine on post-dose day 1 in 10.8-14.1% on atogepant versus 25.2% on placebo.

  3. Ubrogepant for the Treatment of Migraine.
    Dodick et al., 2019 (N Engl J Med, ACHIEVE I) · PMID 31800988 · mixed

    1,672 participants, single acute attack. 2-hour pain freedom: 11.8% placebo, 19.2% ubrogepant 50 mg (P=0.002), 21.2% 100 mg (P<0.001) - an absolute gain of 7-9 percentage points. Freedom from the most bothersome symptom at 2 hours: 27.8%, 38.6%, 37.7%. Adverse events within 48 h: 12.8% placebo, 9.4% 50 mg, 16.3% 100 mg (nausea, somnolence, dry mouth).

  4. Clinical effectiveness of pharmacological interventions for managing chronic migraine in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Naghdi et al., 2023 (J Headache Pain) · PMID 38057728 · mixed

    12 RCTs of six drugs in chronic migraine. All beat placebo on monthly headache and migraine days; eptinezumab 300 mg best for headache days (MD -2.46, 95% CrI -3.23 to -1.69), fremanezumab monthly best for migraine days (MD -2.77, -3.36 to -2.17). Authors state explicitly: 'The absolute differences in the number of headache/migraine days are, at best, modest.' No eligible trials existed for amitriptyline, candesartan or propranolol to compare against.

  5. Preventive drug treatments for adults with chronic migraine: a systematic review with economic modelling.
    Mistry et al., 2024 (Health Technol Assess) · PMID 39365169 · negative

    11 RCTs, 7,352 adults with chronic migraine. CGRP monoclonal antibodies reduced headache/migraine days by 2.0-2.5 per month, onabotulinumtoxinA by just under 2, topiramate by less than 1.5. Despite the antibodies ranking highest, the authors' stated conclusion is that they 'did not find evidence that the calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies are more clinically and cost-effective when compared to topiramate or Botox'. Topiramate was the least costly option and the most cost-effective up to 50,000 GBP per QALY. Most people on some CGRP antibodies reported injection site issues.

  6. Comparative effects of drug interventions for the acute management of migraine episodes in adults: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Karlsson et al., 2024 (BMJ) · PMID 39293828 · negative

    In the acute-treatment network of 137 RCTs and 89,445 participants, eletriptan, rizatriptan, sumatriptan and zolmitriptan were all more efficacious than the recently marketed rimegepant and ubrogepant for 2-hour pain freedom. The authors recommend the most effective triptans as preferred acute treatment, positioning acute gepants as second-line rather than equivalent.

  7. Arterial hypertension: A safety risk of calcitonin gene-related peptide ligand and receptor blocker class.
    Croteau et al., 2026 (Headache; US FDA Division of Pharmacovigilance) · PMID 41923443 · negative

    Retrospective pharmacovigilance case series of FAERS and the literature through 15 October 2025 covering the eight non-erenumab CGRP ligand/receptor blockers marketed in the US. 106 hypertension cases with a probable or possible causal association were identified: 78 (74%) with CGRP-ligand monoclonal antibodies and 28 (26%) with the small molecules. Onset was within 30 days of starting in two-thirds of cases; new or increased antihypertensive treatment was needed in 40 of 106; the CGRP blocker was discontinued in 76% (47/62). The authors state the US Prescribing Information of the entire class now includes hypertension risk in the Warnings and Precautions section.

  8. Migraine Patients Treated With Erenumab and the Risk of Hypertension: A Registry-Based Real-World Study.
    Lampl et al., 2026 (Eur J Neurol) · PMID 41952601 · null

    Registry-based retrospective study of 125 people with migraine treated with erenumab (starting 70 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks) for at least 12 months, with blood pressure measured every 3 months. Systolic pressure ranged 118-121 mmHg and diastolic 78-80 mmHg across visits, remaining stable and within normal physiological range; 54 patients followed to 24 months also showed stable pressure. Authors conclude the study did not find sufficient evidence that erenumab leads to hypertension - a counterweight to, not a refutation of, the FDA case series.

  9. Decoding the long-term safety of anti-CGRP (receptor) mAbs: a meta-analysis and systematic review.
    Hoehne et al., 2026 (J Headache Pain) · PMID 41484941 · mixed

    14 records corresponding to 11 individual studies with 12 months or more of anti-CGRP mAb exposure. Pooled discontinuation for any reason 23%; discontinuation specifically due to adverse events only 3%, staying below 5% beyond year one. Overall adverse event incidence was high at baseline (>70%) but did not increase with longer follow-up. Critically, ALL studies were judged to have severe risk of bias by ROBINS-I, and the authors call the long-term evidence base limited.

  10. Prevention of Episodic Migraine Headache Using Pharmacologic Treatments in Outpatient Settings: A Clinical Guideline From the American College of Physicians.
    Qaseem et al., 2025 (Ann Intern Med) · PMID 39899861 · mixed

    Guideline for adults with 1-14 headache days per month. CGRP monoclonal antibodies (eptinezumab, erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) and gepants (atogepant, rimegepant) were among the eight drug classes ACP evaluated for initiating preventive monotherapy. All three ACP recommendations have conditional strength and low-certainty evidence - a direct statement that no preventive drug in this class, or any other, has high-certainty support.
