Herbs

Cinnamon (Cassia and Ceylon)

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Cinnamon has been tested in dozens of small studies in people with type 2 diabetes, and the honest answer is mixed: it tends to nudge fasting blood sugar down a little, but whether it improves the three-month average (HbA1c) is genuinely unsettled, and the Cochrane review concluded there was not enough evidence to recommend it. In prediabetes, two small trials found it kept fasting glucose from creeping up and smoothed daily glucose swings. If you use it, choose Ceylon (true) cinnamon, because the cheaper Cassia type sold in most supermarkets contains coumarin, which can harm the liver at high daily intakes. Cinnamon is a possible addition alongside your usual care, never a replacement for medication you have been prescribed.

Safety

Cinnamon is not a replacement for prescribed glucose-lowering medication, and nothing here is a reason to stop or reduce a prescription. Adverse reactions in trials were infrequent and generally mild (Cochrane 2012, PMID 22972104); a 4-week 4 g/day prediabetes trial found no difference in digestive symptoms versus placebo (Zelicha et al. 2024, PMID 38290699); 103 prediabetic participants taking a water-soluble extract showed no ECG changes (Pender et al. 2018, PMID 30477858); and a meta-analysis of 7 trials (256 people) found no overall adverse effect on ALT, AST or ALP (Mousavi et al. 2021, PMID 34212447). These trials were short, so they cannot exclude cumulative coumarin liver injury from years of high cassia intake. Coumarin content in cassia products varies enormously between brands and even within one tree, and the price premium on Ceylon cinnamon makes it vulnerable to fraudulent adulteration with cheaper cassia (Pages-Rebull et al. 2024, PMID 38266436).

Who should be careful: High-dose Cassia cinnamon (the usual supermarket and supplement species) should be avoided by people with liver disease, those taking other liver-toxic medicines, and children or small adults, because the tolerable amount of coumarin is set by body weight. That tolerable daily intake is 0.1 mg of coumarin per kilogram of body weight per day - about 6 mg for a 60 kg adult and about 2 mg for a 20 kg child - derived from human as well as animal liver-injury data, and heavy consumers of cassia can reach it from food alone (PMID 20024932). Because cassia has been measured at up to roughly 10,000 mg of coumarin per kilogram (PMID 20853872), a gram-level cassia supplement can use up or exceed an adult's entire daily allowance before any cinnamon in food is counted, and that same review of the human data identified a subgroup of people more susceptible to coumarin liver injury than the animals tested (PMID 20024932). Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum zeylanicum/verum) has been measured at under 0.01 mg/g dry weight coumarin versus 2.23 mg/g for cassia (De Silva et al. 2021, PMID 34813609) and is the safer choice for daily gram-level use. Cinnamon is not a substitute for glucose-lowering medication, and the Cochrane review found no benefit in type 1 diabetes either.

Interactions: Additive glucose lowering with insulin or sulfonylureas is theoretically possible and warrants monitoring; in 10 of 11 trials reviewed by Costello et al. participants continued their usual hypoglycaemic medications alongside cinnamon (PMID 27618575), and those authors advise patients keep following authoritative recommendations for their prescribed drugs. No specific prescription-drug pharmacokinetic interaction was documented in the trials retrieved, though high-coumarin cassia is a theoretical concern alongside other hepatotoxic drugs.

What the evidence says

Everything measured in these trials is a surrogate: fasting glucose, post-meal glucose, HbA1c, insulin resistance. The Cochrane review states explicitly that no trial reported health-related quality of life, morbidity, mortality or costs, so there is no evidence that cinnamon prevents any complication of diabetes. In type 2 diabetes, that 2012 Cochrane review (10 RCTs, 577 participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, mean 2 g/day predominantly cassia for 4-16 weeks) found the fasting glucose effect inconclusive and no significant difference in HbA1c, insulin or postprandial glucose, with risk of bias high or unclear in all but two trials. Later meta-analyses agree on fasting glucose but not HbA1c: Allen et al. (10 RCTs, n=543) found fasting glucose -24.59 mg/dL but HbA1c -0.16% (95% CI -0.39 to 0.02, not significant); Deyno et al. (16 RCTs) found fasting glucose -0.545 mmol/L and HOMA-IR -0.714 with no significant HbA1c or lipid change; de Moura et al. (28 RCTs, 3,054 patients) found fasting glucose -15.26 mg/dL and HbA1c -0.56 (I2 94%), with the HbA1c benefit confined to doses of 2 g/day or less; and an umbrella meta-analysis of 11 meta-analyses put the pooled HbA1c effect at -0.10% (95% CI -0.17 to -0.03), statistically significant but clinically negligible. An earlier meta-analysis of single herbs found Cinnamomum cassia did not significantly improve glycaemic control while three other herbs did. A trial that measured insulin sensitivity directly was null: 1.5 g/day cassia for 6 weeks in 25 postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes changed neither whole-body insulin sensitivity nor oral glucose tolerance. In prediabetes, 500 mg three times daily for 12 weeks held fasting glucose steady while placebo rose (between-group difference 5 mg/dL, n=27 per group), and 4 g/day for 4 weeks lowered continuously monitored 24-hour glucose in 18 adults with obesity; no trial has measured progression to diabetes. Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum zeylanicum) is less studied and its results are mixed: a 12-week Sri Lankan RCT of 1,000 mg/day extract in 150 adults missed its primary LDL-cholesterol endpoint (adjusted mean difference 6.05 mg/dL, 95% CI -2.43 to 14.52, P=0.161) while showing a significant fasting-glucose reduction; an acute crossover in 18 healthy volunteers found the 0-120 min glucose AUC reduced 14.8% but not significantly (P=0.15); and a four-month Sri Lankan RCT of 210 people with type 2 diabetes reported fasting glucose and HbA1c declining from baseline in both 250 mg and 500 mg extract arms. Coumarin content separates the species: analysis of 47 German retail powders confirmed high coumarin in cassia, with stick samples ranging from below the limit of detection to about 10,000 mg/kg even within a single tree, while Ceylon cinnamon has been measured at under 0.01 mg/g dry weight versus 2.23 mg/g for cassia.

Doses studied: 1-6 g/day powdered bark (predominantly Cassia) or 120-500 mg/day extracts for 4-18 weeks in type 2 diabetes; 500 mg three times daily for 12 weeks or 4 g/day for 4 weeks in prediabetes; 250-1,000 mg/day Ceylon extract for 12 weeks to 4 months.

Why this grade: Low end of moderate. Twenty-eight RCTs and roughly a dozen meta-analyses exist, and most show a modest fall in fasting glucose (about 0.5 mmol/L, 10-25 mg/dL) in type 2 diabetes, so the trial base is neither small nor few and 'early' does not fit. But the clinically relevant endpoint is unproven: the Cochrane review (10 RCTs), Allen 2013, Suksomboon 2011 (for cassia specifically), Deyno 2019 and Vanschoonbeek 2006 found no significant HbA1c or insulin-sensitivity effect, while the largest 2025 meta-analysis found -0.56% with 94% heterogeneity and an umbrella review of 11 meta-analyses put the pooled HbA1c effect at just -0.10%. Individual trials are small and mostly at high or unclear risk of bias; species and dose vary; every measured endpoint is a surrogate, with the Cochrane review noting that no trial reported morbidity, mortality or quality of life; and no clinical guideline endorses it. Those last two points alone rule out 'strong'.

Sources (11)
  1. Cinnamon for diabetes mellitus.
    Leach MJ, Kumar S (Cochrane), 2012 · PMID 22972104 · null

    10 RCTs, 577 participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, mean dose 2 g/day (predominantly cassia) for 4-16 weeks: effect on fasting blood glucose inconclusive; no statistically significant difference in HbA1c, serum insulin or postprandial glucose versus control; risk of bias high or unclear in all but two trials; no trial reported health-related quality of life, morbidity, mortality or costs; authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to support cinnamon for type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

  2. Cinnamon use in type 2 diabetes: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Allen RW, Schwartzman E, Baker WL, Coleman CI, Phung OJ, 2013 · PMID 24019277 · mixed

    10 RCTs, 543 patients, 120 mg/day to 6 g/day for 4-18 weeks: fasting plasma glucose -24.59 mg/dL (95% CI -40.52 to -8.67), LDL-C -9.42 mg/dL and triglycerides -29.59 mg/dL, but no significant effect on HbA1c (-0.16%, 95% CI -0.39 to 0.02); heterogeneity I2 66.5-94.7%, which the authors say may limit applicability to patient care.

  3. Efficacy and safety of cinnamon in type 2 diabetes mellitus and pre-diabetes patients: A meta-analysis and meta-regression.
    Deyno S, Eneyew K, Seyfe S, et al., 2019 · PMID 31425768 · mixed

    16 randomized controlled studies: cinnamon reduced fasting blood glucose by 0.545 mmol/L (95% CI -0.910 to -0.18, I2 83.6%) and HOMA-IR by 0.714 (95% CI -1.388 to -0.04) versus placebo, but there was no significant change in HbA1c or lipid profiles, and meta-regression found no factor significantly affecting treatment response.

  4. Meta-analysis of the effect of herbal supplement on glycemic control in type 2 diabetes.
    Suksomboon N, Poolsup N, Boonkaew S, Suthisisang CC, 2011 · PMID 21843614 · null

    9 randomized placebo-controlled trials of single herbs in type 2 diabetes (n=487, minimum 8 weeks, all reporting HbA1c): Ipomoea batatas, Silybum marianum and Trigonella foenum-graecum significantly improved glycaemic control, whereas Cinnamomum cassia did not.

  5. Effects of cinnamon supplementation on metabolic biomarkers in individuals with type 2 diabetes: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    de Moura SL, Gomes BGR, Guilarducci MJ, et al., 2025 · PMID 38917435 · positive

    28 RCTs of 30-120 days, 3,054 patients with type 2 diabetes: fasting glucose -15.26 mg/dL (95% CI -22.23 to -8.30, I2 88%), postprandial glucose -39.22 mg/dL (I2 100%), HbA1c -0.56 (95% CI -0.99 to -0.13, I2 94%) and HOMA-IR -0.76; only doses of 2 g/day or less reduced HbA1c (-0.68).

  6. Cinnamon supplementation does not improve glycemic control in postmenopausal type 2 diabetes patients.
    Vanschoonbeek K, Thomassen BJ, Senden JM, Wodzig WK, van Loon LJ, 2006 · PMID 16549460 · null

    25 postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes assigned to Cinnamomum cassia 1.5 g/day or placebo for 6 weeks in a double-blind controlled trial: no time-by-treatment interaction for whole-body insulin sensitivity or oral glucose tolerance, and no change in the fasting blood lipid profile; the authors conclude more research is warranted before health claims are made.

  7. Influence of Cinnamon on Glycemic Control in Individuals With Prediabetes: A Randomized Controlled Trial.
    Romeo GR, Lee J, Mulla CM, et al., 2020 · PMID 33123653 · positive

    Double-blind trial in prediabetes, cinnamon 500 mg three times daily versus placebo for 12 weeks (n=27 per group): fasting plasma glucose rose with placebo but remained stable with cinnamon, a between-group difference of 5 mg/dL (P<0.05); cinnamon but not placebo reduced glucose AUC and 2-hour OGTT glucose from baseline; no serious adverse events; the authors note longer, larger studies are needed to address progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

  8. Effects of Cinnamomum zeylanicum (Ceylon cinnamon) extract on lipid profile, glucose levels and its safety in adults: A randomized, double-blind, controlled trial.
    Muthukuda D, de Silva CK, Ajanthan S, et al., 2025 · PMID 39854533 · mixed

    150 adults with LDL-C 100-190 mg/dL randomized to Ceylon cinnamon extract 1,000 mg/day or placebo for 12 weeks (127 assessed): the primary endpoint LDL-C was NOT significantly reduced (baseline-adjusted mean difference 6.05 mg/dL, 95% CI -2.43 to 14.52, P=0.161); fasting blood sugar was significantly lower (adjusted mean difference 8.59 mg/dL, 95% CI 0.59 to 16.59, P=0.036), with the effect concentrated in participants with type 2 diabetes; safety profile favourable.

  9. Do Cinnamon Supplements Have a Role in Glycemic Control in Type 2 Diabetes? A Narrative Review.
    Costello RB, Dwyer JT, Saldanha L, et al., 2016 · PMID 27618575 · mixed

    11 RCTs enrolling 694 adults with type 2 diabetes, 4-16 weeks, doses 120-6,000 mg/day; participants continued their hypoglycaemic medications in 10 of the 11 studies. All 11 reported some fasting-glucose reduction and HbA1c decreases were very modest, but only four studies reached American Diabetes Association treatment goals; the authors conclude effects are modest and that clinicians should recommend patients continue following authoritative recommendations for diet, lifestyle changes and hypoglycaemic drugs.

  10. Quantification of flavoring constituents in cinnamon: high variation of coumarin in cassia bark from the German retail market and in authentic samples from indonesia.
    Woehrlin F, Fry H, Abraham K, Preiss-Weigert A, 2010 · PMID 20853872 · negative

    Analysis of 47 retail cinnamon powders confirmed high coumarin levels in cassia cinnamon, with stick samples from two packages ranging from below the limit of detection to about 10,000 mg/kg, and high variation even within a single Indonesian tree; the authors (German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment) note coumarin causes hepatotoxicity in experimental animals and in a proportion of the human population, and that the tolerable daily intake may be exceeded in consumers with high cassia intake.

  11. Toxicology and risk assessment of coumarin: focus on human data.
    Abraham K, Wohrlin F, Lindtner O, Heinemeyer G, Lampen A, 2010 · PMID 20024932 · negative

    Review by the German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment: coumarin is hepatotoxic, and after the European Food Safety Authority discounted a genotoxic mechanism in 2004 a tolerable daily intake of 0.1 mg/kg body weight was derived from animal hepatotoxicity data. Clinical data from patients treated with coumarin as a medicinal drug revealed a subgroup of the human population more susceptible to the hepatotoxic effect than the animal species investigated, cause unknown; using that human data a TDI of 0.1 mg/kg body weight was derived, confirming the EFSA value. Nutritional exposure is mainly due to cassia cinnamon, and a telephone survey of more than 1,000 randomly selected people during the German Christmas season indicated that heavy consumers of cassia cinnamon may reach a daily coumarin intake corresponding to the TDI.
