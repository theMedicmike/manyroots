SSRIs/SNRIs (first-line antidepressants for anxiety)

Strong evidence · for anxiety & stress Strong evidence · for depression & low mood

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

SSRIs and SNRIs are the prescription antidepressants doctors usually try first for ongoing anxiety, and the largest reviews of the trials show they help more people than a placebo does — roughly one extra person in seven gets a meaningful improvement. They also cause more side effects than placebo: about one extra person in 17 stops taking them because of side effects, and stopping suddenly can bring on unpleasant withdrawal-type symptoms, so any change should be planned with a prescriber. The benefit is real but modest, builds over weeks rather than days, and the published research has painted a somewhat rosier picture than the full regulatory record does.

Safety

Side effects are the main reason people stop: in the PubMed-retrieved 2025 Cochrane review of 37 GAD trials, dropout due to adverse effects was more than double that on placebo (RR 2.18, 95% CI 1.81-2.61; roughly one additional person per 17 treated). Commonly reported early effects include nausea, headache, jitteriness or a temporary increase in anxiety, and sleep changes; sexual dysfunction is commonly reported and can persist while treatment continues. These medicines are not addictive in the dependence sense, but per the PubMed-retrieved 2024 meta-analysis roughly one in six to seven people experience discontinuation symptoms attributable to the drug when stopping (severe in about 3%), so taper gradually under medical supervision rather than stopping abruptly. Improvement accrues over the acute treatment phase rather than immediately — the retrieved dose-response meta-analysis modelled weekly outcomes across 12 weeks, with SNRI improvement front-loaded early and SSRI improvement accruing steadily through the acute phase. Trial populations were adults with moderate-to-severe GAD without serious medical comorbidity, so findings may not transfer directly to medically complex patients.

Who should be careful: Do not combine with monoamine oxidase inhibitors (risk of serotonin syndrome). Use caution with citalopram/escitalopram in people with cardiac conduction problems (dose-related QT effects), in bipolar disorder (can trigger mania), in people under 25 (regulatory warnings about treatment-emergent suicidal thoughts), and in pregnancy — prescriber discussion required. Not appropriate to start or stop without medical supervision.

Interactions: Increased bleeding risk when combined with NSAIDs, aspirin or anticoagulants; serotonin syndrome risk with tramadol, triptans, lithium, St John's wort and other serotonergic agents; SNRIs can raise blood pressure. Some SSRIs inhibit liver enzymes that metabolise other drugs, so a full medication and supplement list should go to the prescriber.

What the evidence says

According to PubMed-retrieved evidence, a 2025 Cochrane review of 37 randomised trials (12,226 adults with GAD, 4-28 weeks of double-blind treatment) found antidepressants superior to placebo for treatment response (RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.29-1.55; 20 studies, 7,267 participants; high-certainty; NNTB 7, 95% CI 5-9), with overall dropout no different from placebo (RR 1.03, 0.93-1.14) but dropout due to adverse effects more than doubled (RR 2.18, 1.81-2.61; NNTH 17, 13-112). A network meta-analysis of 89 trials (25,441 patients) found duloxetine (mean difference -3.13 HAM-A points, 95% CrI -4.13 to -2.13), venlafaxine (-2.69) and escitalopram (-2.45) more efficacious than placebo with relatively good acceptability, while paroxetine was efficacious but poorly tolerated versus placebo. A second network meta-analysis (91 articles, 14,812 participants) estimated SMD -0.67 (95% CrI -0.90 to -0.43) for SSRIs and -0.54 (-0.79 to -0.30) for SNRIs, and a dose-response meta-analysis of 57 placebo-controlled trials (N=16,056) found no significant efficacy differences between agents or between classes, with higher SSRI (but not SNRI) doses giving greater improvement and higher doses of both classes increasing side-effect dropout. Against this, an analysis of FDA reviews versus publications found 41 of 57 trials (72%) positive per FDA but 43 of 45 published conclusions (96%) positive, with the published pooled effect (Hedges g 0.38) 15% higher than the FDA-based estimate (g 0.33) — an inflation that was not statistically significant, but clear evidence that the literature overstates how often these drugs succeed. Harms of stopping are well quantified: across 79 studies (21,002 patients) at least one discontinuation symptom occurred in 31% after stopping an antidepressant versus 17% after stopping placebo (attributable incidence about 15%; severe symptoms 2.8% vs 0.6%), while a relapse-prevention meta-analysis (28 trials, n=5,233) found discontinuation tripled relapse odds (OR 3.11, 95% CI 2.48-3.89; relapse 36.4% vs 16.4% over up to one year).

Doses studied: Standard licensed therapeutic ranges of each agent; double-blind treatment in the 2025 Cochrane review ran 4 to 28 weeks, and most acute efficacy trials ran 8-12 weeks. The dose-response meta-analysis found higher SSRI doses within the therapeutic range produced greater symptom improvement and greater likelihood of response, while higher SNRI doses did not; higher doses of both classes increased dropout due to side effects.

Why this grade: Meets the definition on two independent routes. (1) Consistent benefit across multiple large syntheses retrieved from PubMed: a 2025 Cochrane review of 37 RCTs (12,226 participants) rated the response benefit high-certainty (RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.29-1.55; NNTB 7), alongside an 89-trial network meta-analysis (25,441 patients), a 91-article network meta-analysis (14,812 participants), a 57-trial dose-response meta-analysis (N=16,056), and a 28-trial relapse-prevention meta-analysis. (2) Explicit clinical-guideline first-line support: the WFSBP Version 3 guideline states SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line medications for anxiety disorders. 'Strong' here means consistency plus guideline position, NOT a large effect: absolute gains are roughly 2-3 HAM-A points versus placebo (SMD about -0.5 to -0.7), dropout due to adverse effects is more than doubled versus placebo (RR 2.18; NNTH 17), discontinuation symptoms are a documented harm, and an FDA-versus-publication analysis found the published literature reports far more positive trials (96% of publications) than the regulatory record does (72% of trials).

Sources (9)
  1. Antidepressants versus placebo for generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).
    Kopcalic et al., 2025 (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews) · PMID 39880377 · mixed

    Across 37 RCTs (12,226 adults with GAD, 4-28 weeks), antidepressants beat placebo on treatment response (>=50% HAM-A reduction; RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.29-1.55; 20 studies, 7,267 participants; high-certainty evidence; NNTB 7, 95% CI 5-9). Acceptability was no different from placebo (all-cause dropout RR 1.03, 0.93-1.14; 33 studies, 11,294 participants), and fewer dropped out for lack of efficacy (RR 0.41, 0.33-0.50), but MORE dropped out because of adverse effects (RR 2.18, 95% CI 1.81-2.61; 32 studies, 11,793 participants; NNTH 17, 95% CI 13-112) — all high-certainty. Trials excluded people with serious medical comorbidity and regular benzodiazepine use.

  2. Reporting Bias in Clinical Trials Investigating the Efficacy of Second-Generation Antidepressants in the Treatment of Anxiety Disorders: A Report of 2 Meta-analyses.
    Roest et al., 2015 (JAMA Psychiatry) · PMID 25806940 · mixed

    Comparing FDA reviews of 57 premarketing trials of 9 second-generation antidepressants for anxiety disorders against the published literature: 41 of 57 trials (72%) were positive per FDA, but 43 of 45 published article conclusions (96%) were positive (P<.001). Trials the FDA judged positive were 5 times more likely to be published in agreement with that judgement (RR 5.20, 95% CI 1.87-14.45). Study publication bias (P<.001), outcome reporting bias (P=.02) and spin (P=.02) were all detected. The published pooled effect size (Hedges g 0.38, 95% CI 0.33-0.42) was 15% higher than the FDA-based estimate (g 0.33, 0.29-0.38), though this difference was not statistically significant (beta 0.04, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.10; P=.18).

  3. Pharmacological treatments for generalised anxiety disorder: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Slee et al., 2019 (Lancet) · PMID 30712879 · mixed

    Across 89 trials (25,441 patients), duloxetine (MD -3.13 HAM-A, 95% CrI -4.13 to -2.13), venlafaxine (-2.69, -3.50 to -1.89) and escitalopram (-2.45, -3.27 to -1.63) were more efficacious than placebo with relatively good acceptability; sertraline and fluoxetine were also efficacious and well tolerated but limited by small sample sizes. The same analysis found paroxetine (an SSRI) effective but poorly tolerated compared with placebo.

  4. Pharmacological and psychological interventions for generalized anxiety disorder in adults: A network meta-analysis.
    Chen et al., 2019 (Journal of Psychiatric Research) · PMID 31494377 · positive

    In 91 articles (14,812 participants), SSRIs (SMD -0.67, 95% CrI -0.90 to -0.43) and SNRIs (SMD -0.54, -0.79 to -0.30) outperformed placebo for GAD — statistically robust but modest effect sizes.

  5. Systematic review and meta-analysis: Dose-response curve of SSRIs and SNRIs in anxiety disorders.
    Jakubovski et al., 2018/2019 (Depression and Anxiety) · PMID 30479005 · positive

    Across 57 placebo-controlled trials (N=16,056) in adult anxiety disorders, both SSRIs and SNRIs were effective, with no significant efficacy differences between agents within a class or between the classes; the greatest treatment benefits were seen in social anxiety disorder. Higher SSRI doses within the therapeutic range (but not higher SNRI doses) gave significantly greater symptom improvement and likelihood of response, and for both classes higher doses increased dropout due to side effects.

  6. Risk of relapse after antidepressant discontinuation in anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder: systematic review and meta-analysis of relapse prevention trials.
    Batelaan et al., 2017 (BMJ) · PMID 28903922 · positive

    In 28 double-blind relapse-prevention trials (n=5,233), stopping antidepressants tripled relapse odds versus continuing (OR 3.11, 95% CI 2.48-3.89) and shortened time to relapse (HR 3.63, 2.58-5.10; 11 studies). Summary relapse prevalence was 36.4% on placebo versus 16.4% on continued antidepressant over up to one year; the authors caution that the absence of evidence beyond one year is not advice to stop at one year.

  7. Incidence of antidepressant discontinuation symptoms: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Henssler et al., 2024 (Lancet Psychiatry) · PMID 38851198 · negative

    Across 79 studies (21,002 patients), at least one discontinuation symptom occurred in 31% (95% CI 27-35%) after stopping an antidepressant versus 17% (14-21%) after stopping placebo — an attributable incidence of about 15%, roughly one in six to seven patients. Severe discontinuation symptoms occurred in 2.8% versus 0.6%. Desvenlafaxine, venlafaxine, imipramine and escitalopram were associated with higher frequencies; imipramine, paroxetine and desvenlafaxine/venlafaxine with greater severity. Heterogeneity was substantial.

  8. World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) guidelines for treatment of anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and posttraumatic stress disorders - Version 3. Part I: Anxiety disorders.
    Bandelow et al., 2022 (World Journal of Biological Psychiatry) · PMID 35900161 · positive

    Consensus guideline from 33 international experts across 22 countries, based on evaluation of 1,007 RCTs covering panic disorder/agoraphobia, GAD, social anxiety disorder, specific phobias and childhood anxiety disorders. States explicitly that SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line medications and CBT is the first-line psychotherapy for anxiety disorders, with separate recommendations for non-responders and recommendations against interventions with insufficient evidence.

  9. Evidence-based pharmacological treatment of anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder: a revision of the 2005 guidelines from the British Association for Psychopharmacology.
    Baldwin et al., 2014 (Journal of Psychopharmacology) · PMID 24713617 · positive

    International consensus revision of the British Association for Psychopharmacology guidelines, giving evidence-graded recommendations across recognition, acute treatment, longer-term treatment, combination treatment, and further approaches for patients who have not responded to first-line interventions in anxiety disorders, PTSD and OCD.

For depression & low mood

Antidepressants like sertraline, fluoxetine, escitalopram, venlafaxine and duloxetine are the medicines doctors usually try first for depression, and they have been tested in more people than almost any other treatment. On average they help more than a dummy pill, but the average benefit is modest: when researchers looked at individual people inside 232 trials, only about 15 in 100 appeared to get a large effect that the drug itself was responsible for, and the benefit looks biggest in people who are severely depressed and smallest in people whose depression is mild. They are real medicines with real side effects, they take a few weeks to do anything, and stopping them suddenly can make you feel unwell, so they are worth a proper conversation with a prescriber rather than a guess.

Safety

Prescription-only; requires a clinician to start, adjust and stop. Common effects include nausea, sleep and appetite changes, agitation early in treatment, and sexual dysfunction, which is frequent and often persists while the drug is taken. Less common but serious: hyponatraemia (particularly in older adults), increased bleeding risk, and serotonin syndrome when combined with other serotonergic drugs. In pooled placebo-controlled SSRI trials, serious adverse events occurred in about 31 per 1000 participants versus 22 per 1000 on placebo. US labelling carries a boxed warning about increased suicidal thoughts and behaviour in people under 25 during early treatment; in an FDA meta-analysis of 372 trials and 99,231 adults the odds ratio for suicidal behaviour was 2.30 (95% CI 1.04-5.09) under age 25, roughly neutral at 25-64, and lower than placebo at 65 and over, so close monitoring in the first weeks matters most for younger adults. Do not stop abruptly: about one in six to seven people experience discontinuation symptoms above the placebo rate, and severe symptoms occur in roughly 3%; taper with a prescriber. Benefit takes weeks, not days, and a dose-and-age reanalysis found the balance of efficacy against adverse events unfavourable for every studied antidepressant above about age 70, which is a reason for careful prescribing in older people rather than a prohibition.

Who should be careful: Concurrent or recent MAOI use (risk of serotonin syndrome) and known hypersensitivity. Different in bipolar disorder, which is outside this node: antidepressant monotherapy is generally not recommended in bipolar depression because of the risk of switching into mania or hypomania and of cycle acceleration, and guidelines call for mood-stabiliser cover and specialist input. Use caution in people with a history of mania, uncontrolled epilepsy, significant bleeding disorders, or hyponatraemia. Discuss pregnancy and breastfeeding with a prescriber rather than stopping unilaterally.

Interactions: MAOIs, linezolid and methylene blue (serotonin syndrome, contraindicated). Additive serotonergic risk with triptans, tramadol, lithium, St John's wort and other antidepressants. Increased bleeding risk with NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants and antiplatelets. Fluoxetine and paroxetine are strong CYP2D6 inhibitors and can reduce activation of tamoxifen and raise levels of some beta-blockers, antipsychotics and tricyclics. Citalopram and escitalopram prolong the QT interval and interact with other QT-prolonging drugs. Venlafaxine and duloxetine can raise blood pressure; duloxetine requires caution in hepatic impairment and heavy alcohol use.

What the evidence says

The largest network meta-analysis (522 double-blind RCTs, 116,477 participants) found all 21 studied antidepressants superior to placebo on response, with odds ratios from 2.13 (95% CrI 1.89-2.41, amitriptyline) down to 1.37 (1.16-1.63, reboxetine); only agomelatine (0.84) and fluoxetine (0.88) had fewer dropouts than placebo, 46 of 522 trials (9%) were at high risk of bias, and certainty of evidence was moderate to very low. Magnitude, not direction, is where the evidence is contested. An FDA individual-participant analysis of 232 placebo-controlled trials and 73,388 participants found a random-effects drug-placebo difference of 1.75 HAMD17 points (95% CI 1.63-1.86) that increased with baseline severity, best fitted by three response distributions, with drug-treated participants more likely to show a Large response (24.5% vs 9.6%); the authors concluded roughly 15% of participants have a substantial antidepressant effect beyond placebo. Severity-dependence also appears in patient-level data: across 6 trials (718 patients) the medication-placebo Cohen d was below 0.20 at baseline HDRS under 23 and only crossed the NICE clinical-significance threshold at baseline HDRS 25. A systematic review of 131 placebo-controlled SSRI trials (27,422 participants) found a mean HDRS reduction of 1.94 points (95% CI -2.50 to -1.37, from the 49 trials contributing that outcome), below its pre-specified 3-point threshold, alongside increased serious adverse events (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08-1.75, 44 trials; 31 per 1000 vs 22 per 1000), with all trials judged at high risk of bias. Maintenance evidence is more consistent: in the 478-patient ANTLER trial, relapse by 52 weeks occurred in 39% who continued versus 56% who tapered to placebo (HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.56-2.70). Discontinuation is a genuine harm: pooling 79 studies and 21,002 patients, 31% (95% CI 0.27-0.35) reported at least one discontinuation symptom after stopping an antidepressant versus 17% (0.14-0.21) after stopping placebo, an incidence difference of 0.08 (0.04-0.12) in RCTs.

Doses studied: Prescription-only; dose is set and titrated by a prescriber within licensed ranges, and trials pooled in the major meta-analyses used standard licensed therapeutic doses across 21 agents. A Bayesian network meta-analytic reanalysis of that same 21-drug dataset expressed doses in fluoxetine equivalents and modelled dose jointly with age, reporting agomelatine and escitalopram as the best-balanced options for ages 30-65 (escalation to 40 and 60 mg/day fluoxetine equivalents respectively) and finding that no antidepressant showed balanced benefit in patients older than 70 because adverse events exceeded efficacy. Onset is typically over weeks, not days.

Why this grade: Guideline first-line status is unambiguous and the evidence base is enormous: a 522-trial, 116,477-participant network meta-analysis found every one of 21 antidepressants more effective than placebo for response. The grade reflects that the direction of effect is consistently positive and replicated. It is not a claim that the effect is large: the honest controversy is over magnitude, not existence. An FDA individual-participant analysis of 232 trials and 73,388 participants put the average drug-placebo difference at 1.75 HAMD17 points and concluded that only about 15% of participants show a substantial antidepressant effect beyond placebo. A 131-trial systematic review found the average SSRI-placebo difference (1.94 HDRS points, from the 49 trials contributing that outcome) fell below its own pre-specified 3-point clinical-significance threshold and flagged increased serious adverse events, and a patient-level meta-analysis found the drug-placebo gap may be minimal in mild-to-moderate depression. Certainty of evidence in the largest network meta-analysis was rated moderate to very low.

Sources (8)
  1. Comparative efficacy and acceptability of 21 antidepressant drugs for the acute treatment of adults with major depressive disorder: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Cipriani, Furukawa, Salanti, et al., 2018 (Lancet) · PMID 29477251 · positive

    Across 522 double-blind RCTs and 116,477 participants, all 21 antidepressants were more efficacious than placebo, with response ORs ranging from 2.13 (95% CrI 1.89-2.41) for amitriptyline to 1.37 (1.16-1.63) for reboxetine. Only agomelatine (OR 0.84) and fluoxetine (0.88) had fewer dropouts than placebo, while clomipramine had more (1.30). 46 of 522 trials (9%) were rated high risk of bias and certainty of evidence was moderate to very low. The analysis excluded trials with 20% or more participants with bipolar, psychotic or treatment-resistant depression.

  2. Response to acute monotherapy for major depressive disorder in randomized, placebo controlled trials submitted to the US Food and Drug Administration: individual participant data analysis.
    Stone, Yaseen, Miller, et al., 2022 (BMJ) · PMID 35918097 · mixed

    Individual participant data from 232 double-blind placebo-controlled monotherapy trials submitted to the FDA between 1979 and 2016 (73,388 participants) gave a random-effects drug-placebo difference of 1.75 HAMD17 points (95% CI 1.63 to 1.86), increasing significantly with baseline severity. Responses were best fitted by three distributions with mean improvements of 16.0, 8.9 and 1.7 points; drug-treated participants were more likely to show a Large response (24.5% vs 9.6%) and less likely a Minimal response (12.2% vs 21.5%). The authors concluded that about 15% of participants have a substantial antidepressant effect beyond a placebo effect.

  3. Antidepressant drug effects and depression severity: a patient-level meta-analysis.
    Fournier, DeRubeis, Hollon, et al., 2010 (JAMA) · PMID 20051569 · mixed

    Using individual patient data from 6 trials (718 patients), the medication-placebo effect size was estimated at Cohen d below 0.20 for patients with baseline HDRS scores under 23, and only crossed the National Institute for Clinical Excellence threshold for a clinically significant difference at a baseline HDRS of 25. The authors concluded benefit may be minimal or nonexistent, on average, in patients with mild or moderate symptoms, and substantial in very severe depression.

  4. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors versus placebo in patients with major depressive disorder. A systematic review with meta-analysis and Trial Sequential Analysis.
    Jakobsen, Katakam, Schou, et al., 2017 (BMC Psychiatry) · PMID 28178949 · negative

    The review included 131 placebo-controlled trials enrolling 27,422 participants, all judged at high risk of bias. In the 49 trials contributing the outcome, SSRIs reduced HDRS scores by a mean difference of 1.94 points (95% CI -2.50 to -1.37), below the authors' pre-defined 3-point threshold for clinical significance. Across 44 trials, SSRIs increased serious adverse events (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08-1.75), corresponding to 31 per 1000 versus 22 per 1000. The authors concluded the potential small beneficial effects seemed outweighed by harmful effects.

  5. Maintenance or Discontinuation of Antidepressants in Primary Care.
    Lewis, Marston, Duffy, et al., 2021 (NEJM; ANTLER trial) · PMID 34587384 · positive

    In 478 UK primary-care patients well enough to consider stopping, relapse by 52 weeks occurred in 92 of 238 (39%) who maintained their antidepressant versus 135 of 240 (56%) randomly assigned to taper onto placebo (HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.56-2.70, P<0.001). The discontinuation group also reported more symptoms of depression, anxiety and withdrawal. Adherence was 70% in the maintenance group and 52% in the discontinuation group.

  6. Incidence of antidepressant discontinuation symptoms: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Henssler, Schmidt, Schmidt, et al., 2024 (Lancet Psychiatry) · PMID 38851198 · mixed

    Across 79 studies and 21,002 patients, at least one discontinuation symptom was reported by 31% (95% CI 0.27-0.35) after stopping an antidepressant versus 17% (0.14-0.21) after stopping placebo; among RCTs the summary incidence difference was 0.08 (0.04-0.12). Severe discontinuation symptoms occurred in 2.8% (1.4-5.7%) versus 0.6% (0.2-1.3%) on placebo. Desvenlafaxine, venlafaxine, imipramine and escitalopram were associated with higher frequency of symptoms; imipramine, paroxetine and either desvenlafaxine or venlafaxine with greater severity. Allowing for non-specific effects, the authors put attributable incidence at approximately 15%, affecting one in six to seven people who stop.

  7. Risk of suicidality in clinical trials of antidepressants in adults: analysis of proprietary data submitted to US Food and Drug Administration.
    Stone, Laughren, Jones, et al., 2009 (BMJ) · PMID 19671933 · mixed

    Meta-analysis of 372 double-blind randomised placebo-controlled trials in 99,231 adults found risk of suicidality strongly age-dependent. For suicidal behaviour the odds ratios were 2.30 (95% CI 1.04-5.09) under age 25, 0.87 (0.58-1.29) at 25-64, and 0.06 (0.01-0.58) at 65 and over; for suicidal behaviour or ideation combined, 1.62 (0.97-2.71), 0.79 (0.64-0.98) and 0.37 (0.18-0.76) respectively. The increased risk in adults under 25 approaches that seen in children and adolescents.

  8. Optimal doses of antidepressants in dependence on age: Combined covariate actions in Bayesian network meta-analysis.
    Holper, 2020 (EClinicalMedicine) · PMID 31993575 · mixed

    A random-effects Bayesian network meta-analytic reanalysis of the 21-drug Cipriani dataset, modelling dose (in fluoxetine equivalents) jointly with age, identified agomelatine and escitalopram as best balanced for efficacy and tolerability in ages 30-65, escalable to 40 and 60 mg/day fluoxetine equivalents respectively. Seven agents including amitriptyline, sertraline and trazodone showed no relevant balanced benefit. No antidepressant showed balanced benefit in patients older than 70 because adverse events exceeded efficacy.
