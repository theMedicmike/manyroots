Low-FODMAP diet

Strong evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

This is the eating plan with the most research behind it for IBS, and gut specialists often suggest it first. For four to six weeks you cut back on a family of carbohydrates that ferment in the gut and draw in water, then you add them back one at a time to find your own personal triggers. Many people feel meaningfully better, but be realistic: in a head-to-head study it worked no better than simpler, gentler advice about how and when to eat, in one 2025 review it showed no clear benefit at all, and the overall quality of the research is rated low. It is meant to be a short, guided experiment with a dietitian, not a permanent way of eating.

Safety

Short-term tolerability is good: in the largest comparative trial (CARIBS, 294 analysed) no serious adverse events or treatment-related deaths occurred, and only two participants in each dietary arm stopped for adverse events. But this is a restrictive elimination diet with real trade-offs, not a benign 'clean eating' plan. The AGA advises it be delivered with a registered dietitian where possible, that restriction last no more than 4-6 weeks, and that it be abandoned for another treatment if there is no clinical response in a predetermined period. Measured microbial effects are real but not uniformly adverse: faecal Bifidobacterium fell significantly in a sham-controlled RCT (8.8 vs 9.2 rRNA genes/g, P=.008) with no change in diversity, while a 21-day controlled crossover found higher faecal pH (7.37 vs 7.16, p=0.001) and reduced total bacterial abundance (9.63 vs 9.83 log10 copies/g, p<0.001) but SIMILAR short-chain fatty acid concentrations and GREATER microbial diversity. A 2024 umbrella review notes that some studies suggest the diet may increase faecal pH and dysbiosis and reduce short-chain fatty acids. The long-term consequences of sustained restriction for the microbiome and for nutritional adequacy remain unresolved by the trial literature, which is precisely why the restriction phase is time-limited. Restriction also carries psychological risk: the AGA states that routine screening for disordered eating or eating disorders by careful dietary history is critical because these are common and often overlooked in gastrointestinal conditions.

Who should be careful: Not appropriate as a self-directed indefinite diet. Do not start before a positive IBS diagnosis has been made and coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease have been excluded - the ACG guideline advises coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin in people with IBS and diarrhoea. The AGA names poor candidates for restrictive diet interventions: people consuming few culprit foods, those at risk of malnutrition, those who are food insecure, and those with an eating disorder or uncontrolled psychiatric disorder. Avoid unsupervised use in people who are underweight or losing weight, and in children, pregnancy or breastfeeding, where the trial evidence does not extend. Unexplained weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, fever or new symptoms after age 50 are reasons to see a clinician rather than change diet.

Interactions: No drug interactions are described in the trial literature. Practical overlaps matter more. The diet restricts lactose, dairy and many high-fibre foods, so calcium and fibre intake need active attention. Fibre supplements, prebiotics such as inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides and galacto-oligosaccharides, and prebiotic-containing probiotics directly conflict with the restriction phase and should be reviewed before starting. Running a low-FODMAP diet at the same time as another elimination diet compounds restriction and makes it impossible to identify the real trigger. In a sham-controlled trial a co-administered multistrain probiotic restored Bifidobacterium abundance (9.1 vs 8.8 rRNA genes/g, P=.019) without changing symptom outcomes (IBS-SSS 207 +/- 98 vs 192 +/- 93, P=.721).

What the evidence says

A 2021 network meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (944 patients) ranked a low-FODMAP diet first for global IBS symptoms versus habitual diet (RR of symptoms not improving 0.67, 95% CI 0.48-0.91) and superior to BDA/NICE dietary advice for bloating or distension (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.55-0.94). A 2021 meta-analysis of 12 papers found a moderate-to-large reduction in IBS severity (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -0.88 to -0.44, I-squared 54%), equating to a 45-point IBS-SSS reduction (95% CI -77 to -14). The same group's 2025 network meta-analysis of 28 RCTs (2338 patients) confirmed benefit versus habitual diet (RR of global symptoms not improving 0.51, 95% CI 0.37-0.70, 24 trials) but ranked the diet fourth, behind a starch-reduced and sucrose-reduced diet (RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.26-0.67); all network comparisons were rated low or very low confidence except the direct low-FODMAP and starch/sucrose-reduced comparisons with habitual diet, rated moderate. Countervailing evidence is substantial. A 2025 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (1118 participants) found no significant benefit for global symptom improvement (RR 1.21, 95% CI 0.98-1.51, I-squared 63%) and reported no significant quality-of-life benefit, though the same abstract gives a mean difference of 4.59 (95% CI 1.50-7.67), an internally inconsistent result; that review pooled RCTs alongside cohort studies. A 2018 GRADE appraisal of 7 RCTs (397 participants) found RR 0.69 (95% CI 0.54-0.88) but rated overall quality 'very low' and noted the three trials with rigorous control diets showed the least magnitude of effect. In a 4-week sham-diet-controlled trial (n=104), adequate symptom relief was 57% versus 38% but did not reach significance on intention-to-treat (P=.051), reaching it only per-protocol (61% vs 39%, P=.042), although IBS-SSS was significantly lower (173 +/- 95 vs 224 +/- 89, P=.001). In a 75-patient multicentre RCT, a low-FODMAP diet was no better than traditional IBS dietary advice (50% vs 46% achieving IBS-SSS reduction of 50 or more, P=.72). In the CARIBS trial (294 analysed), 76% responded to low-FODMAP plus traditional advice, 71% to a low-carbohydrate diet and 58% to optimised medical treatment (p=0.023 across the three groups) - so a restrictive diet containing no FODMAP-specific logic performed nearly as well, which argues against FODMAP specificity as the mechanism. Effects on bowel habit are inconsistent: the original feeding trial found all IBS subtypes reported greater satisfaction with stool consistency but only IBS-D showed altered stool frequency; the 2025 network meta-analysis found no dietary intervention superior to control for bowel habit; and a 2024 umbrella review reported improvements in stool consistency (WMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.902 to -0.07) and frequency (WMD -0.36, 95% CI -0.61 to -0.10).

Doses studied: The AGA Clinical Practice Update describes three phases: restriction lasting no more than 4-6 weeks, then reintroduction of FODMAP foods, then personalisation based on the reintroduction results. Most efficacy RCTs used a 4-week intervention (Böhn 2015, Staudacher 2017, CARIBS 2024). Controlled feeding work targeted less than 0.5 g FODMAPs per meal with almost all food provided over 21 days (Halmos 2014). A 21-day crossover contrasted a measured low-FODMAP intake of 3.05 g/day (95% CI 1.86-4.25) against a typical Australian intake of 23.7 g/day (95% CI 16.9-30.6).

Why this grade: Graded strong on two legs. First, explicit clinical-guideline support: the 2021 ACG guideline states 'We recommend a limited trial of a low fermentable oligosaccharides, disacchardies, monosaccharides, polyols (FODMAP) diet in patients with IBS to improve global symptoms', and the 2022 AGA Clinical Practice Update states that the low-FODMAP diet 'is currently the most evidence-based diet intervention for IBS'. Second, benefit versus a habitual diet is reproduced across multiple meta-analyses (Black 2021 RR of symptoms not improving 0.67, 95% CI 0.48-0.91; van Lanen 2021 SMD -0.66, 95% CI -0.88 to -0.44; Dionne 2018 RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.54-0.88; Cuffe 2025 RR 0.51, 95% CI 0.37-0.70). The caveats are substantial and are stated plainly rather than buried: GRADE-rated certainty of the pooled evidence is 'very low' (Dionne 2018); a 2025 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs was frankly null (RR 1.21, 95% CI 0.98-1.51); the diet was no better than simpler traditional IBS dietary advice in a head-to-head trial; it did not reach significance against a sham diet on intention-to-treat (P=.051); the three trials using rigorous control diets showed the smallest effect; and in the 2025 network meta-analysis it ranked fourth, behind a starch-reduced and sucrose-reduced diet. This is evidence for a supervised 4-6 week restriction phase followed by reintroduction, not for indefinite restriction.

  2. Efficacy of a low FODMAP diet in irritable bowel syndrome: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisBlack CJ, Staudacher HM, Ford AC, 2021 (Gut) · PMID 34376515 · positive

    13 RCTs, 944 patients. Low-FODMAP diet ranked first for global IBS symptoms vs habitual diet (RR of symptoms not improving 0.67, 95% CI 0.48-0.91, P-score 0.99) and beat BDA/NICE advice for bloating/distension (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.55-0.94). The authors note the diet is recommended if general lifestyle and dietary advice fails, that most trials were in secondary or tertiary care, and that none studied reintroduction or personalisation.

  3. Efficacy of a low-FODMAP diet in adult irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisvan Lanen AS, de Bree A, Greyling A, 2021 (Eur J Nutr) · PMID 33585949 · positive

    12 papers (9 parallel, 3 crossover). IBS severity reduced vs control diet (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -0.88 to -0.44, I-squared 54%); among IBS-SSS studies a 45-point mean reduction (95% CI -77 to -14, I-squared 89%); IBS-QoL improved (MD 4.93, 95% CI 1.77-8.08). All three authors were affiliated with Unilever at the time of publication.

  4. The Efficacy of the Low-FODMAP (Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols) Diet in Irritable Bowel Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.
    Narrative reviewKhan Z, Muhammad SA, Amin MS, Gul A, 2025 (Cureus) · PMID 39917138 · null

    Meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (1118 participants) found NO statistically significant benefit for global symptom improvement: RR 1.21 (95% CI 0.98-1.51), I-squared 63%. Five quality-of-life studies were described as showing no statistically significant benefit, although a mean difference of 4.59 (95% CI 1.50-7.67) is also reported. The review pooled 15 RCTs alongside 6 cohort studies in which risk of bias was rated moderate to severe.

  5. A low FODMAP diet plus traditional dietary advice versus a low-carbohydrate diet versus pharmacological treatment in irritable bowel syndrome (CARIBS): a single-centre, single-blind, randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialNybacka S, Törnblom H, Josefsson A, et al., 2024 (Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol) · PMID 38643782 · mixed

    294 participants analysed over 4 weeks. Response (IBS-SSS reduction of 50 or more): 73/96 (76%) low-FODMAP plus traditional advice, 69/97 (71%) low-carbohydrate diet, 59/101 (58%) optimised medical treatment; p=0.023 across the three groups. The low-carbohydrate arm nearly matched the low-FODMAP arm, arguing against FODMAP specificity. Two participants in each intervention group discontinued for adverse events and 5/91 stopped medication for side-effects; no serious adverse events or treatment-related deaths occurred.

  6. Diet low in FODMAPs reduces symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome as well as traditional dietary advice: a randomized controlled trial.

    Multicentre single-blind RCT, 75 randomised (67 completed), 4 weeks. Both arms improved (P<.0001 within each) with NO difference between groups (P=.62): 19 of 38 (50%) in the low-FODMAP arm vs 17 of 37 (46%) in the traditional-dietary-advice arm achieved an IBS-SSS reduction of 50 or more (P=.72).

  8. A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Evaluating the Efficacy of a Gluten-Free Diet and a Low FODMAPs Diet in Treating Symptoms of Irritable Bowel Syndrome.

    7 RCTs, 397 participants: low-FODMAP RR of symptoms remaining 0.69 (95% CI 0.54-0.88, I-squared 25%). The three RCTs using rigorous control diets showed the LEAST magnitude of effect. Overall quality of the data was rated 'very low' by GRADE.

  9. A diet low in FODMAPs reduces symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome.
    Randomised trialHalmos EP, Power VA, Shepherd SJ, Gibson PR, Muir JG, 2014 (Gastroenterology) · PMID 24076059 · positive

    Randomised single-blind crossover feeding trial, 30 IBS patients and 8 controls, 21 days per arm with almost all food provided and a goal of under 0.5 g FODMAPs per meal. Overall gastrointestinal symptom score 22.8 mm (95% CI 16.7-28.8) on the low-FODMAP diet vs 44.9 mm (95% CI 36.6-53.1) on a typical Australian diet (P<.001). All IBS subtypes reported greater satisfaction with stool consistency, but IBS-D was the ONLY subtype with altered faecal frequency and King's Stool Chart scores. Symptoms in healthy controls were minimal and unaltered by either diet.

  10. Diets that differ in their FODMAP content alter the colonic luminal microenvironment.
    Randomised trialHalmos EP, Christophersen CT, Bird AR, Shepherd SJ, Gibson PR, Muir JG, 2015 (Gut) · PMID 25016597 · mixed

    Single-blind randomised crossover, 27 IBS and 6 healthy subjects, 21 days per provided diet (low 3.05 g/day, 95% CI 1.86-4.25, vs Australian 23.7 g/day, 95% CI 16.9-30.6). The low-FODMAP diet raised faecal pH (7.37, 95% CI 7.23-7.51 vs 7.16, 95% CI 7.02-7.30; p=0.001) and reduced total bacterial abundance (9.63 vs 9.83 log10 copies/g; p<0.001), with SIMILAR short-chain fatty acid concentrations and GREATER microbial diversity. Authors conclude the implications of long-term FODMAP reduction require elucidation.

  11. Effects of a low FODMAP diet on the symptom management of patients with irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic umbrella review with the meta-analysis of clinical trials.

    Umbrella review of 11 meta-analyses and 24 RCTs, 1646 participants. Clinical improvement on IBS-SSS (RR 1.42, 95% CI 1.02-1.97, p=0.04), with favourable effects on stool consistency (WMD -0.48, 95% CI -0.902 to -0.07) and frequency (WMD -0.36, 95% CI -0.61 to -0.10); quality of life improved but anxiety and depression did not. Authors note some studies show the diet may increase faecal pH and dysbiosis and reduce short-chain fatty acids, and that the certainty of evidence is low.

  12. ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    GuidelineLacy BE, Pimentel M, Brenner DM, et al., 2021 (Am J Gastroenterol) · PMID 33315591 · positive

  13. AGA Clinical Practice Update on the Role of Diet in Irritable Bowel Syndrome: Expert Review.
