Gut-directed hypnotherapy

Moderate evidence · for gut & digestion

For gut & digestion

Gut-directed hypnotherapy is a guided relaxation and imagery technique, taught over several sessions, that aims to calm the conversation between the brain and the gut. In several good trials, more people who did it reported meaningful relief than people who got usual care or an education class, and in one large trial that relief was still there a year later. But the biggest trial of all, which used a self-guided app, did not beat a simple muscle-relaxation app on its main measure, and the benefit shrank when the therapy was delivered outside specialist centres. It genuinely helps some people and carries very little risk, but it is better thought of as a worthwhile option than a sure thing.

Safety

Gut-directed hypnotherapy has no known physical harms and adverse events in trials have been rare. In the 354-patient IMAGINE trial, eight unexpected serious adverse reactions were reported but the medical ethics committee judged none to be related to the therapy; most were later diagnoses of cancer or inflammatory bowel disease, which underlines that hypnotherapy must never substitute for a proper diagnosis. The 362-patient digital trial reported no serious adverse events and no adverse events leading to discontinuation. Anyone with alarm features (unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, nocturnal symptoms, a family history of bowel cancer or coeliac disease, or new symptoms starting after age 50) needs medical assessment first. Delivery quality appears to matter: benefit in trials came from standardised gut-directed protocols delivered by trained therapists, not generic stage or entertainment hypnosis, and effectiveness was measurably lower when the same therapy was delivered outside specialised centres. Note also that the evidence here is for IBS only; hypnotherapy has been studied separately in inflammatory bowel disease and those results are not interchangeable with IBS results.

Who should be careful: As a matter of standard clinical caution rather than trial evidence, formal hypnosis is generally avoided, or requires specialist psychiatric judgment, in people with active psychosis, schizophrenia, dissociative disorders, or severe untreated PTSD, because absorbed or dissociative states can worsen symptoms or trigger distressing re-experiencing. It is not appropriate as a stand-alone approach for undiagnosed abdominal pain, for suspected inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or bowel cancer, or in place of urgent care for alarm symptoms. People with significant untreated depression or suicidality need proper mental-health assessment rather than a symptom-focused hypnotherapy course alone.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. It has been studied alongside usual medical care and alongside a low-FODMAP diet without harm, though one RCT found no additive benefit from combining hypnotherapy with the diet. If it helps, discuss any change to prescribed IBS medication with the prescriber rather than stopping medication independently.

What the evidence says

In a 2025 network meta-analysis of 67 RCTs (7,441 participants), GDH reduced the risk of global IBS symptoms not improving versus waiting-list control (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66-0.95; 12 trials, 1,507 patients), but had the LOWEST P score (0.39) of the seven therapies with the largest evidence base, behind minimal-contact CBT, telephone disease self-management, dynamic psychotherapy, CBT, disease self-management and internet-based minimal-contact CBT; an earlier network meta-analysis of 41 RCTs (4,072 participants) reported RR 0.67 (95% CI 0.49-0.91, P score 0.57) while noting funnel-plot asymmetry and stating efficacy is likely overestimated. The largest trial to date is not a positive one: a 2023 study randomised 378 patients (362 treated) to a self-administered digital GDH programme or digital muscle relaxation and did NOT meet its primary endpoint (abdominal pain response 30.4% vs 27.1%, P=0.5352), though GDH was superior during the last 4 weeks of treatment (30.9% vs 21.5%, P=0.0232). The largest therapist-delivered trial (IMAGINE, 354 patients across 11 Dutch hospitals) found adequate relief in 40.8% (individual) and 33.2% (group) hypnotherapy versus 16.7% for educational supportive therapy at 3 months (OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.2-7.4) and 40.8%/49.5% versus 22.6% at 12 months (OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.2-6.7), with group delivery non-inferior to individual. Against this, a 2021 meta-analysis of 7 pooled trials found the effect on global symptoms was not statistically significant (SMD 0.24, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.54, I-squared 66%). Setting appears to matter: across two Swedish trials (138 refractory patients) hypnotherapy beat supportive therapy in psychology private practice (P<0.05) but produced only a non-significant trend versus waiting list in a small county hospital (P=0.17), and the authors concluded effectiveness is lower outside highly specialised centres; a UK primary-care RCT likewise found improvement in pain, diarrhoea and overall symptom score at 3 months but no quality-of-life difference and no differences maintained beyond 3 months. A head-to-head RCT (n=74) found 6-week symptom improvement statistically indistinguishable between hypnotherapy, a low-FODMAP diet and their combination (P=0.67) with no additive benefit, although hypnotherapy was superior on trait anxiety (-4, 95% CI -6 to -2, P<0.0001) and trait depression (-3, 95% CI -5 to -0.7, P=0.011). Trials enrolled mixed IBS subtypes and generally did not report subtype-stratified results, so there is no reliable evidence that GDH works better or worse for any one subtype.

Doses studied: Trials have used 5 to 12 sessions of a standardised gut-directed protocol delivered by a trained therapist, individually or in groups: five sessions in a UK primary-care trial, six in the IMAGINE trial, eight over 12 weeks in a nurse-delivered Swedish trial, and twelve at one hour per week in an earlier Swedish trial. Home audio practice between sessions is usual. Subgroup analysis suggests higher-volume courses perform better: at least 8 sessions with at least 6 hours of contact reached significance (SMD 0.51, 95% CI 0.27-0.76), as did at least weekly sessions (SMD 0.45, 95% CI 0.23-0.67), whereas the overall pooled effect did not. Self-administered digital programmes have been tested over 12 weeks.

Why this grade: Multiple network meta-analyses find gut-directed hypnotherapy (GDH) superior to waiting-list control for global IBS symptoms, and a 354-patient therapist-delivered trial found benefit sustained to 12 months. Held at moderate rather than strong for five reasons. The largest trial of any kind (378 randomised, digital GDH vs digital muscle relaxation) MISSED its primary endpoint (30.4% vs 27.1%, P=0.5352). GDH ranked LOWEST of the seven therapies with the largest evidence base in the 2025 network meta-analysis (P score 0.39). A meta-analysis restricted to 7 pooled trials found the effect was not statistically significant (SMD 0.24, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.54). Effectiveness fell when the therapy was delivered outside specialised research centres, with only a non-significant trend versus waiting list in one community setting (P=0.17), and a primary-care trial found no advantage persisting beyond 3 months. Finally, the meta-analysts themselves report that no trial was at low risk of bias, that funnel-plot asymmetry was present, that certainty was low or very low, and that efficacy is therefore likely overestimated. It is not graded 'early' because 12 trials and 1,507 patients pool to a significant effect — that is more than preliminary evidence.

Sources (8)
  1. Efficacy of behavioural therapies for irritable bowel syndrome: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisThakur et al., 2025 · PMID 41077057 · positive

    Network meta-analysis of 67 RCTs (7,441 participants). Gut-directed hypnotherapy reduced the risk of global IBS symptoms not improving versus waiting-list control (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66-0.95; 12 trials, 1,507 patients) but had the lowest P score (0.39) of the seven therapies with the largest evidence base, ranking behind minimal-contact CBT (0.78), telephone disease self-management (0.75), dynamic psychotherapy (0.72), CBT (0.64), disease self-management (0.58) and internet-based minimal-contact CBT (0.43). Authors report evidence of publication bias, that no RCT was at low risk of bias across all domains, and that certainty across the network was low or very low.

  2. A Randomized Parallel-group Study of Digital Gut-directed Hypnotherapy vs Muscle Relaxation for Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    Randomised trialBerry et al., 2023 · PMID 37391055 · null

    The largest GDH randomised trial to date: 378 patients randomised (362 treated) to 12 weeks of self-administered digital gut-directed hypnotherapy or digital muscle relaxation. The PRIMARY ENDPOINT WAS NOT MET - abdominal pain response (>=30% reduction in average daily pain in the 4 weeks after treatment) occurred in a similar proportion of each group, 30.4% (GDH) versus 27.1% (muscle relaxation), with no significant difference (P=0.5352). GDH was superior on secondary timepoints, during the last 4 weeks of treatment (30.9% vs 21.5%, P=0.0232) and across the whole treatment period (29.3% vs 18.8%, P=0.0254). No serious adverse events occurred.

  3. Efficacy of individual and group hypnotherapy in irritable bowel syndrome (IMAGINE): a multicentre randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialFlik et al., 2019 · PMID 30473202 · positive

    Multicentre RCT, 354 patients randomised across 11 Dutch hospitals to six sessions of individual hypnotherapy, group hypnotherapy or group educational supportive therapy. Adequate relief at 3 months: 40.8% individual, 33.2% group, 16.7% control (hypnotherapy vs control OR 2.9, 95% CI 1.2-7.4, p=0.0240); at 12 months 40.8%, 49.5% and 22.6% (OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.2-6.7, p=0.0185). Group delivery was non-inferior to individual delivery.

  4. Systematic review, meta-analysis with subgroup analysis of hypnotherapy for irritable bowel syndrome, effect of intervention characteristics.
    Meta-analysisKrouwel et al., 2021 · PMID 33508441 · null

    Twelve trials reviewed, seven pooled. Hypnotherapy reduced the risk of global gastrointestinal symptoms but the pooled effect was NOT statistically significant (SMD 0.24, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.54, I-squared 66%). Only higher-volume courses reached significance (at least 8 sessions with at least 6 hours contact: SMD 0.51, 95% CI 0.27-0.76), as did at least weekly sessions (SMD 0.45, 95% CI 0.23-0.67) and group delivery (SMD 0.45, 95% CI 0.03-0.88).

  5. Effects of gut-directed hypnotherapy on IBS in different clinical settings-results from two randomized, controlled trials.
    Randomised trialLindfors et al., 2011 · PMID 21971535 · mixed

    Two RCTs, 138 patients with IBS refractory to standard management, conducted deliberately outside specialised research units. In study 1 (n=90, hypnotherapy in psychology private practices vs supportive therapy) the hypnotherapy group improved significantly more than controls (P<0.05). In study 2 (n=48, hypnotherapy in a small county hospital vs waiting list) only a NON-SIGNIFICANT trend in the same direction was seen (P=0.17). The authors concluded that effectiveness is lower when hypnotherapy is delivered outside highly specialised research centres.

  6. Efficacy of psychological therapies for irritable bowel syndrome: systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisBlack et al., 2020 · PMID 32276950 · positive

    Network meta-analysis of 41 RCTs (4,072 participants). Gut-directed hypnotherapy was efficacious after completion of therapy (RR of remaining symptomatic 0.67, 95% CI 0.49-0.91, P score 0.57), similar to face-to-face CBT (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.48-0.80) and self-administered/minimal-contact CBT (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.45-0.83); no therapy was superior to another. Risk of bias was high with funnel-plot asymmetry, and the authors state efficacy is likely to have been overestimated.

  7. Gut-directed hypnotherapy for irritable bowel syndrome: piloting a primary care-based randomised controlled trial.
    Randomised trialRoberts et al., 2006 · PMID 16464325 · mixed

    Primary-care RCT of five hypnotherapy sessions plus usual management versus usual management alone. The intervention group had significantly greater improvement in pain, diarrhoea and overall symptom score at 3 months (P<0.05), but no significant between-group difference in quality of life at any point and no differences maintained beyond 3 months. Intervention patients were significantly less likely to require medication. Authors concluded the lack of difference beyond 3 months prohibits general introduction without further evidence.

  8. Randomised clinical trial: the efficacy of gut-directed hypnotherapy is similar to that of the low FODMAP diet for the treatment of irritable bowel syndrome.
    Randomised trialPeters et al., 2016 · PMID 27397586 · mixed

    RCT of 74 patients allocated to hypnotherapy, low-FODMAP diet or both. Overall symptom improvement at week 6 was -33 mm (hypnotherapy), -30 mm (diet) and -36 mm (combination) with no difference across groups (P=0.67); at least 20 mm improvement in 72%, 71% and 72% respectively. No additive effect was observed from combining the two. Hypnotherapy was superior on trait anxiety (-4, 95% CI -6 to -2, P<0.0001) and trait depression (-3, 95% CI -5 to -0.7, P=0.011).
