Practices

Progressive Muscle Relaxation

Moderate evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Tensing and releasing your muscles group by group is a genuinely recognized insomnia technique - sleep medicine guidelines say clinicians may reasonably use relaxation therapy on its own, and it is cheap, safe and easy to learn. Pooling the trials, it does seem to improve how people rate their sleep, though the benefit was not clear in people over 55 and doing more sessions did not help more. The honest complication is that when researchers pulled apart what actually makes CBT-I work across hundreds of trials, relaxation was the one piece that didn't pull its weight, and might even have gotten slightly in the way. It is a reasonable thing to try, especially if physical tension is your main barrier to sleep - just don't expect it to do the job of the full program.

Safety

Among the lowest-risk interventions available for sleep: nothing is ingested, it costs nothing, and it can be self-taught from a recording. Reported harms in trials are minimal, though adverse-event reporting is thin. Three practical cautions are worth knowing. First, a minority of people experience relaxation-induced anxiety - paradoxical tension, intrusive thoughts, or panic sensations when they deliberately turn attention inward; if that happens, stopping is appropriate and a different approach (stimulus control, sleep restriction) is likely a better fit. Second, the pooled evidence did not show a significant benefit in adults aged 55 and older, so older adults in particular should not persist with it at the expense of a treatment more likely to work for them. Third, and most importantly, the best component evidence suggests relaxation is the weakest ingredient in insomnia treatment: a 241-trial analysis found it added nothing and possibly detracted. Using it as a substitute for full CBT-I, rather than alongside it or as a low-barrier starting point, is the main risk.

Who should be careful: The muscle-tensing phase should be modified or skipped over areas with acute injury, recent surgery, unhealed fracture, significant muscle cramping, or painful conditions where deliberate contraction would hurt; a tension-free 'passive relaxation' or body-scan variant is standard in those cases. People with PTSD or a trauma history may find sustained inward body focus distressing and should ideally learn it with clinician support. Never practice while driving or operating machinery.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. If you are taking a sedative-hypnotic, note that relaxation training has been studied as an alternative to and comparator for such medication - do not taper a prescription on your own on the strength of a relaxation practice; that should be planned with your prescriber.

What the evidence says

Evidence for progressive muscle relaxation in insomnia is guideline-recognized, pooled-positive, and genuinely contested. The 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline, built on a systematic review that screened 1,244 studies and analyzed data from 89, issued a CONDITIONAL recommendation that clinicians may use relaxation therapy as a single-component therapy for chronic insomnia disorder in adults - while reserving its only STRONG recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis dedicated to progressive muscle relaxation and sleep quality pooled 14 RCTs (957 participants) and found improved subjective sleep quality (Hedges g = -1.24, 95% CI -1.71 to -0.77, p<0.001), but with I-squared = 85.5%, benefit in adults under 55 (g -1.40, 95% CI -2.12 to -0.69) and NO significant benefit in adults aged 55 and older (g -1.17, 95% CI -2.67 to 0.32), and no association between session count and outcome. The most important contrary evidence is a 2024 component network meta-analysis in JAMA Psychiatry covering 241 randomized trials and 31,452 participants, which found relaxation procedures were 'potentially counterproductive' within CBT-I packages (remission incremental odds ratio 0.81, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.02) and concluded that beneficial packages should include cognitive restructuring, third-wave components, sleep restriction and stimulus control 'but not relaxation.' Head-to-head data are similarly mixed: in a 91-veteran RCT using progressive muscle relaxation as the active control arm, brief behavioral treatment for insomnia significantly reduced trauma-related nightmares while progressive muscle relaxation did not, and neither reduced non-trauma bad dreams. Positive signals do exist beyond the pooled estimate: a 160-patient trial in maintenance hemodialysis patients found a 7-week CBT-I program including Jacobson progressive muscle relaxation produced significantly greater and more sustained improvements in ISI, PSQI, fatigue, anxiety, depression and SF-36 quality of life than the same program without it, and a 2025 network meta-analysis of 19 postpartum RCTs (1,826 participants) ranked progressive muscle relaxation second of 13 interventions in the first 3 months postpartum (SMD -3.2 versus usual care) - though effect sizes that large in small unblinded trials warrant caution.

Doses studied: Typically taught in 4 to 8 sessions and practiced 10-30 minutes daily, often at bedtime. Cited trials used 7 weeks of Jacobson progressive muscle relaxation embedded in a CBT-I program, and 4-week abbreviated online formats. Notably, the 2026 PMR meta-analysis found no significant association between the number of relaxation sessions and the size of the sleep-quality improvement (meta-regression p=0.2106), so more sessions do not appear to buy more benefit.

Why this grade: Relaxation therapy carries an actual clinical-guideline recommendation from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, which is more than most non-drug options here can claim - but it is a CONDITIONAL suggestion, not the strong first-line recommendation, which the same guideline reserves for multicomponent CBT-I. A 2026 meta-analysis specifically of progressive muscle relaxation (14 RCTs, 957 participants) does find a substantial pooled improvement in subjective sleep quality, but with very high heterogeneity, no significant benefit in adults 55 and older, and no relationship between number of sessions and outcome. Set against that, the largest analysis ever done of CBT-I components (241 trials, 31,452 participants) found relaxation procedures to be the one component that added nothing and was possibly counterproductive, and concluded beneficial CBT-I packages should include cognitive restructuring, sleep restriction and stimulus control but 'not relaxation.' Guideline-recognized and pooled-positive, but with a serious contrary meta-analytic signal and a null in older adults, is squarely moderate; grading it higher would ignore the best available contrary evidence.

Sleep hygiene as a single component received a conditional recommendation AGAINST.

  2. Components and Delivery Formats of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Chronic Insomnia in Adults: A Systematic Review and Component Network Meta-Analysis.
    Network meta-analysisFurukawa Y, Sakata M, Yamamoto R, Nakajima S, Kikuchi S, Inoue M, Ito M, Noma H, Takashina HN, Funada S, Ostinelli EG, Furukawa TA, Efthimiou O, Perlis M, 2024 (JAMA Psychiatry) · PMID 38231522 · negative

    241 randomized trials, 31,452 participants (mean age 45.4, 67% women). Critical CBT-I components were cognitive restructuring (remission iOR 1.68, 95% CI 1.28-2.20), third-wave components (1.49, 1.10-2.03), sleep restriction (1.49, 1.04-2.13) and stimulus control (1.43, 1.00-2.05). Sleep hygiene education was not essential (1.01, 0.77-1.32), and relaxation procedures 'were found to be potentially counterproductive' (iOR 0.81, 95% CI 0.64-1.02). The authors conclude beneficial CBT-I packages 'may include cognitive restructuring, third-wave components, sleep restriction, stimulus control, and in-person delivery but not relaxation.'

  3. The effects of progressive muscle relaxation on sleep quality in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisLi K, Chen S, Liu Y, Yu N, 2026 (Frontiers in Public Health) · PMID 42625730 · mixed

    14 RCTs, 957 participants. Progressive muscle relaxation improved subjective sleep quality overall (Hedges g = -1.24, 95% CI -1.71 to -0.77, p<0.001) but with I-squared = 85.5%. Benefit was significant in adults younger than 55 (g -1.40, 95% CI -2.12 to -0.69) but NOT in adults aged 55 and older (g -1.17, 95% CI -2.67 to 0.32). Effects were seen in both cancer patients (g -1.17, 95% CI -2.24 to -0.09) and non-cancer patients (g -1.29, 95% CI -1.94 to -0.65). Meta-regression found no significant association between the number of relaxation sessions and sleep quality (p=0.2106).

  4. Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an American Academy of Sleep Medicine systematic review, meta-analysis, and GRADE assessment.

    The evidence base underlying the AASM guideline: 1,244 studies screened, 124 met inclusion criteria, 89 provided data suitable for statistical analysis.   5. Brief behavioral treatment for insomnia decreases trauma-related nightmare frequency in veterans.
    Randomised trialRanney RM, Gloria R, Metzler TJ, Huggins J, Neylan TC, Maguen S, 2022 (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine) · PMID 35393934 · mixed

    91 military veterans with insomnia disorder randomized to brief behavioral treatment for insomnia or progressive muscle relaxation therapy as the active control. Brief behavioral treatment significantly reduced trauma-related nightmares from baseline to post-treatment; progressive muscle relaxation therapy did not. Neither treatment reduced non-trauma-related bad dreams. Nightmare reductions were not maintained at 6-month follow-up.

  6. Efficacy of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Combined with Jacobson Progressive Muscle Relaxation in Improving Sleep Quality and Overall Well-Being in Hemodialysis Patients with Insomnia: A Randomized Controlled Trial.
    Randomised trialChen WY, Li J, Xia D, 2025 (Tohoku Journal of Experimental Medicine) · PMID 39814399 · positive

    160 maintenance hemodialysis patients with insomnia randomized to a 7-week CBT-I program with or without Jacobson progressive muscle relaxation. The arm including JPMR showed significantly greater and sustained improvements in Insomnia Severity Index, PSQI, fatigue, anxiety, depression and SF-36 quality of life, with separation visible by week 4 and more pronounced by week 12.

  7. Comparative effectiveness of nonpharmacological interventions on postpartum maternal sleep quality: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.
    Network meta-analysisNguyen CTT, Chiu HY, Lin-Lewry M, Bei B, Tsai SY, Kuo SY, 2025 (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine) · PMID 40745901 · positive

    19 RCTs, 1,826 participants, 13 nonpharmacological interventions. In the first 3 months postpartum, progressive muscle relaxation ranked second versus usual care (SMD -3.2), behind foot reflexology (SMD -3.5) and ahead of massage (SMD -2.0). CBT-I (SMD -1.7) and light-dark therapy (SMD -1.3) were the effective options at 4-12 months postpartum.
