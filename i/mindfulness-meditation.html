A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Mindfulness programs like MBSR teach you to observe thoughts and body sensations without getting swept up in them, usually through an 8-week class with daily home practice at home. In one high-quality trial the full 8-week program worked about as well as a common anxiety medication, with far fewer side effects. But across the wider research it has not beaten other active treatments, it did worse than CBT in people with diagnosed anxiety disorders, and its edge over other therapies largely disappears in studies run by researchers with no stake in mindfulness.

Safety

Mindfulness programs were well tolerated in the best-controlled anxiety trial available: in TAME, study-related adverse events occurred in 15.4% of MBSR participants versus 78.6% on escitalopram, and no MBSR participant dropped out because of one. The honest limitation is that harms reporting in this literature is poor — the meta-analysis of 23 mindfulness RCTs in DSM-5 anxiety disorders concluded adverse events were insufficiently reported — so the real rate of distressing experiences during practice is not well characterised, not demonstrated to be low. Expect that deliberately turning attention toward anxious thoughts and body sensations can feel uncomfortable at first.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications have been established in trials. Because harms data are thin and intensive practice can surface distressing material, people with psychotic disorders, unprocessed trauma or severe depression should undertake intensive meditation — especially silent retreats — only with clinical guidance.

Interactions: No drug interactions. Do not stop an anxiety medication in favor of meditation without talking to the prescriber; in the trial that compared them, the medication arm was not outperformed, only matched.

What the evidence says

Evidence for mindfulness-based programs in adult anxiety and stress is moderate and consistent in direction but weak in relative terms. The AHRQ-commissioned meta-analysis of 47 active-controlled trials (3,515 participants) found moderate evidence of improved anxiety (effect size 0.38, 95% CI 0.12-0.64 at 8 weeks; 0.22, 95% CI 0.02-0.43 at 3-6 months), only low evidence for stress/distress, and no evidence that meditation programs outperformed any active treatment such as drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies. In the TAME randomized noninferiority trial, 276 adults with anxiety disorders were recruited and 208 formed the primary noninferiority sample; 8 weeks of MBSR was noninferior to escitalopram 10-20 mg (CGI-S reduction 1.35 vs 1.43; difference -0.07, 95% CI -0.38 to 0.23), with study-related adverse events in 15.4% of MBSR versus 78.6% of escitalopram participants and no MBSR dropouts due to adverse events. In an earlier GAD-specific RCT (n=93), both MBSR and an active stress-management control significantly reduced Hamilton Anxiety scores without separating on that primary measure, though MBSR was superior on CGI-S, CGI-I, BAI and laboratory stress reactivity. Against this, a meta-analysis of 23 RCTs in DSM-5 anxiety disorders (1,815 adults) found short-term anxiolytic effects added to usual care but MBSR significantly lower in effect than CBT, with no significant differences remaining at 6-12 months and adverse events insufficiently reported. The largest relative-efficacy synthesis (142 samples, 12,005 participants) found mindfulness interventions superior to no treatment (d = 0.55) but only d = 0.23 versus specific active controls and indistinguishable from evidence-based treatments (d = -0.004), with the most consistent disorder-level support for depression, pain, smoking and addictive disorders — not anxiety. A subsequent re-analysis of 60 comparisons (N=5,627) found no evidence of mindfulness superiority over other active psychological treatments once researcher allegiance was absent or balanced.

Doses studied: Standardized MBSR: 8 weekly group classes plus daily home practice (the TAME trial used the weekly 8-week MBSR course); trial benefit is typically measured at 8 weeks with follow-up to 3-6 months, and differences from active comparators are no longer significant by 6-12 months in DSM-5 anxiety disorders.

Why this grade: Meta-analytic evidence shows consistent but modest short-term benefit for anxiety, and one well-conducted randomized trial found 8-week MBSR noninferior to a first-line SSRI. Graded moderate rather than strong because the AHRQ-commissioned synthesis found no evidence meditation beat any active treatment, a 142-sample meta-analysis found mindfulness did not differ from evidence-based treatments (d = -0.004) and did not list anxiety among the disorders with the most consistent support, MBSR underperformed CBT in DSM-5 anxiety disorders, and a re-analysis found the advantage over other active treatments disappeared when researcher allegiance was absent or balanced.

For chronic pain

Mindfulness training teaches you to notice pain and the reactions that pile on top of it - the bracing, the dread, the fight against it - without being swept away. Trials show it helps, modestly, compared with carrying on as usual, and it tends to lift mood and quality of life alongside pain. What it has not shown is any advantage over other active treatments like talk therapy or exercise, and the benefit often fades once the course ends.

Safety

No serious adverse events were reported in the 7-trial MBSR low back pain meta-analysis, but only 7 of the 38 trials in the larger chronic-pain review reported on safety at all - so the harms picture is thin rather than reassuringly clean. The real costs are time and completion: the standard course runs eight weekly two-hour groups with practice between sessions, and in the largest trial nearly half of participants attended fewer than 6 of the 8 sessions. Some people find that turning attention toward pain is uncomfortable in the first weeks.

Who should be careful: People with PTSD, active psychosis, severe depression, dissociative symptoms, or recent trauma should begin only with a teacher trained in trauma-sensitive practice and, ideally, with mental-health support in place, since sustained inward attention can surface distressing memories or intensify dissociation. Intensive silent retreats are a materially different and higher-risk format than a standard 8-week course and are not what these trials studied. Not a substitute for medical evaluation of new or changing pain.

Interactions: No drug interactions. Mindfulness practice is not a reason to stop or taper prescribed pain or psychiatric medication without clinician involvement, and it should not be positioned as evidence that pain is imagined - it is training in relating differently to pain that is real.

What the evidence says

A systematic review of 38 randomised trials found low-quality evidence that mindfulness meditation is associated with a small decrease in pain compared with all types of controls (30 RCTs), with statistically significant effects also for depression symptoms and quality of life; only 7 of the 38 trials reported on safety. A separate meta-analysis of 47 trials with 3515 participants, restricted to trials with active controls, found moderate evidence that mindfulness meditation programmes improved pain (effect size 0.33, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.62), anxiety (0.38 at 8 weeks) and depression (0.30 at 8 weeks), but found no evidence that meditation programmes were better than any active treatment such as drugs, exercise, or other behavioural therapies. In a 342-participant randomised trial in chronic low back pain, 60.5% of the MBSR group achieved clinically meaningful (>=30%) improvement in functional limitations at 26 weeks versus 44.1% with usual care (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.77), and 43.6% versus 26.6% for pain bothersomeness (RR 1.64, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.34), with little change at 52 weeks and no significant difference from CBT. A meta-analysis of 7 trials and 864 low back pain patients found MBSR improved pain intensity versus usual care in the short term (MD -0.96 on a numerical rating scale, 95% CI -1.64 to -0.34) but that this was not sustained in the long term, and found no significant differences versus an active comparator at short- or long-term follow-up. In a 282-participant trial in adults aged 65 and over, an MBSR-modelled programme improved function by only 1.1 points more than health education at 8 weeks (effect size -0.23) and 0.04 points at 6 months (effect size -0.08), and the change in mean pain was not significant at 8 weeks (-0.1, 95% CI -1.1 to 1.0), though by 6 months current pain had improved by an additional -1.8 points (95% CI -3.1 to -0.05).

Doses studied: The dominant format is the standard 8-week MBSR course, which is designed around weekly group sessions plus practice between them. In the largest chronic low back pain trial the intervention was delivered in eight weekly two-hour groups, and in the older-adult trial an 8-week group programme was followed by six monthly sessions. Completion matters: in the 342-person trial only 53.7% attended 6 or more of the 8 sessions.

Why this grade: A 38-trial systematic review found only low-quality evidence of a small pain decrease, and a 47-trial active-control meta-analysis found moderate evidence for pain at an effect size of 0.33 with no evidence that meditation beat any active treatment. A 342-person RCT did show MBSR beating usual care at 26 weeks with findings persisting at 52 weeks, and ACP names MBSR among its moderate-quality-evidence options for chronic low back pain. But a 7-trial meta-analysis found short-term effects were not sustained long-term and vanished against active comparators, and a 282-person trial in older adults found the functional gain was not maintained (effect size -0.08 at 6 months). Consistent small short-term benefit over doing nothing with no established advantage over active alternatives - moderate.

For depression & low mood

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is an eight-week group course that teaches you to notice thoughts and feelings without being pulled under by them. Its best evidence is for staying well after depression rather than for lifting a current episode - it lowers the risk of relapse in people who have had several episodes, though a large direct comparison found it no better than staying on antidepressants. Two things worth knowing: meditation is not risk-free, with roughly one in twelve people in studies reporting an unwanted effect such as increased anxiety, and researchers have shown that mindfulness studies report positive results far more often than their own effect sizes would predict, so the published picture is rosier than reality.

Safety

Not risk-free. Across 83 studies and 6,703 participants, pooled adverse-event prevalence was 8.3% (95% CI 5-12%), rising to 33.2% in observational studies of more intensive practice compared with 3.7% in controlled experimental settings; the most frequently reported were anxiety, depression, and cognitive anomalies, and some occurred in people with no prior mental health history. In the PREVENT trial, serious adverse events were equal between MBCT and maintenance antidepressants (five each, including two deaths in each group), none attributable to the interventions. Structured 8-week clinical programmes with a trained instructor - the format actually tested in the depression trials - appear substantially safer than intensive silent retreat practice, and the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

Who should be careful: Approach with clinical guidance if you have a trauma history, dissociative symptoms, active psychosis, or are in an acute depressive crisis; sustained attention to internal experience can intensify distressing material, and the reviewed evidence base is in remitted or mildly-to-moderately symptomatic outpatients, not acute or high-risk populations. MBCT trials recruited people with recurrent unipolar depression; findings do not transfer to bipolar depression, where meditation practices that disrupt sleep warrant particular caution and should sit alongside mood-stabilising treatment. Not a substitute for emergency care during a suicidal crisis.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. The critical safety point concerns medication tapering rather than the practice itself: in PREVENT, MBCT was delivered with structured support to taper or discontinue antidepressants and was not superior to simply staying on them, so MBCT should never be used as a rationale for stopping antidepressants independently. Any taper needs to be planned and supervised by the prescribing clinician.

What the evidence says

The strongest evidence is for mindfulness-based cognitive therapy as relapse prevention in recurrent depression rather than as treatment for a current episode. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials (1,258 patients; mean age 47.1 years, SD 11.9; 75.0% women) found MBCT reduced the risk of depressive relapse over 60 weeks compared with non-MBCT conditions (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.58-0.82), and, more informatively, compared with active treatments including antidepressants (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.64-0.97), with larger effects in those carrying more pronounced residual symptoms. That advantage did not hold in the largest direct comparison: the 424-patient PREVENT trial, in adults with three or more previous episodes, found no difference in time to relapse or recurrence over 24 months between MBCT with support to taper antidepressants and maintenance antidepressant treatment (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.67-1.18, p=0.43), with both treatments producing enduring positive outcomes and no difference in serious adverse events. For current depressive symptoms the effect is small: a review restricted to trials with active controls (47 trials, 3,515 participants) found moderate evidence of improved depression at effect size 0.30 (95% CI 0.00-0.59) at 8 weeks and 0.23 (95% CI 0.05-0.42) at 3-6 months, and reported no evidence that meditation programmes were better than any active treatment, including drugs, exercise, or other behavioural therapies. A meta-analysis of 15 trials of MBCT, acceptance and commitment therapy, and positive psychotherapy in current major depression (N=946) found a small pooled effect (Hedges' g=0.34, 95% CI 0.14-0.54, p<0.001) with significant heterogeneity (I²=53%), a significant difference between active-control and treatment-as-usual comparisons, and overall evidence quality rated low. The published record is also demonstrably skewed toward positive results: across 124 published mindfulness-therapy trials, 108 (87%) reported at least one positive outcome in the abstract and 109 (88%) concluded the therapy was effective, roughly 1.6 times the number of positive trials expected given an effect of d=0.55; 13 of 21 registered trials (62%) remained unpublished 30 months after completion; and none of 36 systematic reviews or meta-analyses of this literature concluded that its effect estimates were inflated by reporting bias. Harms are under-reported but real: across 83 studies with 6,703 participants, 55 studies (65%) reported at least one type of meditation adverse event, with pooled prevalence 8.3% (95% CI 5-12%) - 3.7% in experimental studies versus 33.2% in observational studies - most commonly anxiety (33% of reports), depression (27%), and cognitive anomalies (25%).

Doses studied: MBCT is a manualised 8-week group programme of weekly sessions with daily home practice, studied as relapse prevention in people with three or more previous depressive episodes who were in full or partial remission. Trials in the broader meditation literature typically ran 8-week mindfulness programmes with outcomes assessed at 8 weeks and 3-6 months.

Why this grade: The strongest evidence is narrow and specific: an individual-patient-data meta-analysis (9 trials, 1,258 patients) supports MBCT for preventing relapse in recurrent depression, including against active treatments. But the definitive head-to-head trial found MBCT no better than maintenance antidepressants, evidence for treating a current episode is small (g=0.30-0.34) and rated low quality, and a rigorous active-controlled review found no evidence that meditation programmes outperform any active treatment. Two further findings hold the grade down rather than let it rise: documented adverse events at a pooled prevalence of 8.3%, and a formal analysis showing this literature produces about 1.6 times as many positive trials as its own effect size predicts, with most registered trials never adequately pre-specifying a primary outcome. Moderate is the honest placement, at the lower end of it.

For fatigue & low energy

Mindfulness means practising deliberate, non-judgemental attention to what is happening right now, usually taught as a group course with daily home practice. Studies in people tired from cancer treatment or multiple sclerosis generally show it helps a moderate amount, and it can make fatigue easier to live alongside even when it does not shrink it. The catch is that when it is compared against another active activity rather than a waiting list, the benefit for fatigue tends to disappear, and in at least one trial the improvement had faded three months later. That suggests part of the effect is attention and structure rather than the meditation itself.

Safety

Mindfulness practice is generally well tolerated and trials report few adverse events, though adverse-event reporting in this literature is widely acknowledged to be poor, so absence of reported harm is weak reassurance. A minority of people experience increased anxiety, agitation, dissociation, or intrusive re-experiencing of traumatic memories during sustained silent practice, and this is more likely with long or intensive formats than with short daily practice. For someone already exhausted, the time demand of a weekly group session plus daily home practice can itself be a burden worth weighing against a modest and possibly temporary benefit.

Who should be careful: Intensive or retreat-format meditation should be approached cautiously and with a trained teacher in people with active psychosis, bipolar disorder in an acute phase, severe dissociative symptoms, or PTSD with untreated trauma, where body-focused attention can trigger flashbacks. Mindfulness is not a substitute for investigating the cause of persistent fatigue and should not delay workup for anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, diabetes or medication effects.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Where mindfulness is used alongside treatment for depression or anxiety, it is an addition rather than a replacement, and psychiatric medication should not be reduced or stopped on the strength of a meditation practice without the prescriber.

What the evidence says

A meta-analysis of 29 randomised trials with 3,476 cancer patients and survivors found those receiving mindfulness-based interventions reported significantly lower fatigue as well as lower anxiety, depression and stress and greater quality of life than control participants, though the abstract reports no fatigue-specific effect size (Xunlin 2019). In multiple sclerosis, a meta-analysis of 23 RCTs found a moderate effect on fatigue both immediately post-intervention and at follow-up, alongside large effects on depression, anxiety, stress and pain immediately post-test — but no significant effect on pain at follow-up, with overall risk of bias rated unclear (Han 2021). The negative evidence is substantial and comparator-driven. A meta-analysis of 20 RCTs of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction in adults with sleep disturbance found that compared with active control, MBSR did not improve sleep-related daytime impairments including fatigue, depression, anxiety, stress or quality of life, and did not improve sleep quality in chronic insomnia or cancer; only the waitlist comparison showed benefit, with substantial heterogeneity (SMD -0.32, 95% CI -0.56 to -0.08, I-squared 71%) (Kim 2022). A meta-analysis restricted to trials with active controls (47 trials, 3,515 participants) found moderate evidence of improvement in anxiety, depression and pain, low evidence of no effect or insufficient evidence for sleep and several other outcomes, and no evidence that meditation programmes were better than any active treatment including drugs, exercise or other behavioural therapies; fatigue was not among the outcomes it assessed (Goyal 2014). In post-stroke fatigue, a Cochrane review found a large pooled effect across mixed interventions including MBSR (SMD -1.07, 95% CI -1.93 to -0.21; 6 trials, 244 participants) that was not present in the trials using adequate allocation concealment (2 trials, 89 participants, SMD -0.38, 95% CI -0.80 to 0.04) (Wu 2015). Durability is also uncertain: a 6-week mindfulness programme in 71 younger breast cancer survivors improved fatigue, sleep disturbance and vasomotor symptoms post-intervention, but those effects were not maintained at 3-month follow-up (Bower 2015). Direct evidence in ME/CFS is limited to a single pilot randomised study of 35 completers, which found lower fatigue after MBCT than waiting list, maintained to 6 months, and which its own authors framed as a feasibility study requiring a larger trial (Rimes 2011).

Doses studied: Most trials used the standard Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction or Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy format of weekly group sessions with daily home practice; the breast-cancer-survivor trial used a shorter 6-week Mindful Awareness Practices programme (Bower 2015). The ME/CFS study used a group MBCT programme adapted for people still experiencing excessive fatigue after CBT, delivered in two separate groups (Rimes 2011). Programme length, session length and home-practice requirements vary across this literature and are not consistently reported.

Why this grade: Dozens of randomised trials and several meta-analyses exist, which puts this above the early grade on volume alone, and pooled results in cancer and multiple sclerosis populations consistently favour mindfulness on fatigue. But the effect is fragile in exactly the ways non-specific effects are. Against active control rather than waiting list, one meta-analysis found no improvement in fatigue or other daytime impairments; a large active-controlled meta-analysis found no evidence meditation outperformed any active treatment; in post-stroke fatigue the pooled benefit vanished in the trials with adequate allocation concealment; and in a randomised trial of younger breast cancer survivors the behavioural improvements including fatigue were not maintained at 3-month follow-up. Moderate is the accurate grade for evidence that exists, is broadly positive, and is modest, comparator-dependent and of uncertain durability.

For insomnia

Mindfulness meditation has been tested for sleep in a good number of real trials, and it does seem to help people fall asleep more easily and rate their sleep as better — compared with doing nothing or with a general relaxation class. The picture gets weaker the harder the comparison: against the standard talk therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) mindfulness generally comes out behind, and one review that looked only at people with diagnosed chronic insomnia could not show a clear benefit at all. It is a reasonable, low-risk thing to try, especially if racing thoughts at bedtime are the problem, but it is not the strongest tool available.

Safety

Generally well tolerated in sleep trials — the MBTI trial in older adults reported that no participants withdrew because of adverse effects (PMID 34193328). Harms are, however, real and under-reported: a systematic review of 83 studies covering 6,703 meditators found an overall adverse-event prevalence of 8.3% (95% CI 5-12%), 3.7% in experimental studies but rising to 33.2% in observational studies, with anxiety (33% of reports), depression (27%) and cognitive anomalies (25%) the most common, occurring in some people with no prior mental health history (Farias 2020, PMID 32820538). For context on dropout — though in a different clinical population, not sleep — a Cochrane review of mindfulness-based interventions for substance use disorders (40 RCTs) found little to no increase in attrition versus no treatment (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.77-1.40) or versus other treatments (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.89-1.26), while noting that few studies reported adverse events at all (PMID 34668188). One practical caution: persistent insomnia can mask undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea, and in one trial in patients with insomnia and mild OSA, surgical treatment of the apnea improved insomnia more than CBT-I alone (PMID 19014072) — meditation is not a substitute for evaluating loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, or unrefreshing sleep despite adequate time in bed.

Who should be careful: No absolute contraindications for a standard secular mindfulness class. Caution and instructor/clinician involvement are warranted for people with active psychosis, severe untreated PTSD or dissociation, or acute suicidality, given documented meditation-related anxiety, depression and cognitive adverse events (PMID 32820538). Not a replacement for assessment of suspected sleep apnea, restless legs, or a mood disorder driving the insomnia.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. If insomnia is being treated with hypnotics or sedating antidepressants, do not stop or taper them on your own while starting a meditation program — dose changes should be made with the prescriber, since abrupt discontinuation of benzodiazepine-receptor agonists can cause rebound insomnia.

What the evidence says

The largest sleep-specific synthesis (Rusch 2018, 18 RCTs, 1,654 participants) found that mindfulness meditation had no effect on sleep quality versus specific active controls — that is, versus evidence-based sleep treatments — with an effect size of 0.03 (95% CI -0.43 to 0.49) post-treatment and -0.14 (95% CI -0.62 to 0.34) at follow-up, both low strength of evidence. Against nonspecific active controls (time- and attention-matched), the same review found a significant but small benefit: ES 0.33 (95% CI 0.17-0.48) post-intervention and 0.54 (95% CI 0.24-0.84) at follow-up, moderate strength. A meta-analysis restricted to chronic insomnia disorder in the general adult population (de Entrambasaguas 2023, nine studies) was more sobering still: versus active controls, mindfulness-based interventions produced a non-significant effect on the Insomnia Severity Index (delta 0.44, p=0.07), a non-significant effect on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (delta 0.52, p=0.18), and a significant but very small effect on a composite sleep variable (delta 0.05, p<0.01); the authors concluded the analysis could not demonstrate that these interventions positively affected insomnia disorder. In diagnosed chronic insomnia (Ong 2014, n=54), MBSR or MBTI reduced total wake time by 43.75 minutes versus 1.09 minutes for self-monitoring, with MBTI reaching 50% remission and 78.6% response at 6 months. A larger trial in adults aged 50-80 (Perini 2021, n=127) found MBTI reduced insomnia severity more than a sleep hygiene/education/exercise program (effect size -1.27 vs -0.69), but no significant interaction effects were observed on objective polysomnography or actigraphy measures. Head-to-head against CBT-I in cancer patients with insomnia (Garland 2014, n=111), MBSR was inferior immediately after the program and only reached noninferiority at 3-month follow-up; sleep onset latency fell 22 minutes with CBT-I versus 14 minutes with MBSR. Goyal 2014, reviewing 47 trials with active controls across all indications, found low evidence of no effect or insufficient evidence of any effect on sleep, and no evidence that meditation programs outperformed any active treatment. The 2021 American Academy of Sleep Medicine systematic review formally evaluated mindfulness alongside CBT-I, brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction, relaxation training, sleep hygiene, biofeedback, paradoxical intention and intensive sleep retraining; the companion guideline recommends multicomponent CBT-I strongly and issues conditional recommendations for brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction and relaxation therapy — mindfulness receives no recommendation.

Doses studied: Standardized 8-week group programs — Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) or Mindfulness-Based Therapy for Insomnia (MBTI) — with one group session per week plus assigned daily home practice. The chronic-insomnia trial (Ong 2014) and the cancer noninferiority trial (Garland 2014) both used 8-week programs; the older-adult trial (Perini 2021) used 2-hour weekly group sessions across 8 weeks.

Why this grade: Many RCTs and several meta-analyses exist specifically for sleep outcomes, and the pooled direction is positive — but essentially only against waitlist or attention-matched controls. In a head-to-head noninferiority trial, MBSR was inferior to CBT-I immediately post-program. The 2021 AASM systematic review formally evaluated mindfulness and the companion clinical practice guideline issued no recommendation for it, while strongly recommending CBT-I. Real and substantial trial evidence, modest and inconsistent magnitude, no guideline endorsement — that is 'moderate', not 'strong'; and the size of the trial base rules out 'early'.

For gut & digestion

Mindfulness training teaches you to notice body sensations, including gut sensations, with curiosity rather than alarm, usually as an eight-week course with daily home practice. Some small trials found it eased IBS symptoms and improved quality of life, but when researchers pooled the studies together the effect on symptom scores just missed being statistically reliable, and one trial's benefit had faded by six months. It is low-risk and may genuinely help with the fear and hypervigilance that come with unpredictable gut symptoms, but the evidence that it reduces the symptoms themselves is still preliminary.

Safety

Mindfulness training is physically benign and IBS trials have not reported treatment-related serious adverse events. The most concrete practical signal is adherence rather than harm: in the 90-patient MBSR trial, 44% of participants dropped out of the MBSR arm compared with 23% of controls, so the eight-week commitment with daily home practice is a real barrier for many people. A minority of people, particularly those with trauma histories, report distressing experiences during sustained meditation practice, including increased anxiety, agitation, dissociation or re-experiencing of traumatic memories; these are more often reported with intensive or unguided practice than with a supported eight-week group course. Teacher quality matters, and IBS-adapted or clinically supervised programmes are preferable to unguided apps for someone with significant psychological distress. Mindfulness is not a diagnostic process and must not delay medical assessment of alarm features such as unintentional weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia, nocturnal symptoms or new symptoms after age 50.

Who should be careful: Caution and specialist guidance are appropriate for people with active psychosis, bipolar disorder in an acute phase, severe untreated PTSD or dissociative disorders, where intensive meditative practice can destabilise symptoms. It is not a substitute for treatment of moderate-to-severe depression or for care of active suicidality. It is not appropriate as a stand-alone approach for undiagnosed abdominal symptoms or where inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease or malignancy has not been excluded.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Mindfulness has been studied alongside usual medical care and can be combined with dietary approaches or IBS medication. If practice reduces symptom burden, any change to prescribed medication should be discussed with the prescriber rather than made independently.

For high blood pressure

Mindfulness training does seem to bring clinic blood pressure readings down by a few points, and it clearly helps with anxiety, low mood and stress. But when researchers used 24-hour monitors instead of a single clinic reading, the blood pressure benefit mostly vanished — one careful trial in people with stage 1 hypertension found essentially no change at all. And in the wider review of relaxation therapies, the effect faded once the people taking the readings did not know who had been trained, which suggests a good part of it was expectation rather than the practice itself. It is worth doing for how it makes you feel; treat any blood pressure effect as unproven rather than promised.

Safety

Mindfulness programmes are generally well tolerated: the pooled meta-analysis found a 15% dropout rate from MBSR arms, similar to control arms, and the MB-BP trial reported 17.4% loss to follow-up. Meditation is not, however, universally benign — intensive or prolonged practice can surface distressing memories, anxiety, dissociation, depersonalisation or worsened mood in some people. Working with a trained teacher who screens for and responds to difficult experiences matters more than the technique itself.

Who should be careful: There is no absolute contraindication, but people with a history of trauma, PTSD, psychosis, bipolar disorder or dissociative symptoms should approach intensive silent or long-retreat practice only with clinician-informed guidance, and should not begin with high-intensity formats. Anyone whose distress worsens during practice should stop and speak to a clinician rather than pushing through.

    101 unmedicated adults with ambulatory-confirmed stage 1 hypertension (baseline 24-hour BP 135+/-7.9/82+/-5.8 mmHg) randomised to 8-week MBSR or wait-list. At week 12, change in 24-hour ambulatory BP was 0.4+/-6.7/0.0+/-4.9 mmHg with MBSR versus 0.4+/-7.8/-0.4+/-4.6 mmHg with control, with no significant differences on any ambulatory BP parameter; a secondary within-group analysis found a small reduction after MBSR limited to female subjects. Authors concluded MBSR did not lower ambulatory BP by a statistically or clinically significant amount, and that it leaves untested whether MBSR might lower BP by improving medication adherence in treated patients.

  3. Relaxation therapies for the management of primary hypertension in adults (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews)
    Dickinson HO et al., 2008 · PMID 18254065 · negative

    29 RCTs with 8 weeks to 5 years follow-up; 25 trials and 1,198 participants in the primary meta-analysis. Adequate randomisation was confirmed in only 7 trials and concealment of allocation in only 1. Relaxation produced a pooled -5.5 mmHg systolic (95% CI -8.2 to -2.8) and -3.5 mmHg diastolic, but the 9 trials reporting blinded outcome assessors found a non-significant -3.2 mmHg (95% CI -7.7 to 1.4) and the 15 sham-controlled trials a non-significant -3.5 mmHg (95% CI -7.1 to 0.2). Authors concluded the evidence for a causal association between relaxation and blood pressure reduction is weak and that some apparent benefit was probably due to aspects of treatment unrelated to relaxation.

  4. Effect of Adapted Mindfulness Training in Participants With Elevated Office Blood Pressure: The MB-BP Study: A Randomized Clinical Trial
    Loucks EB et al., 2023 · PMID 37218591 · positive

    201 adults with unattended office BP of 120/80 mmHg or above randomised to Mindfulness-Based Blood Pressure Reduction (n=101) or enhanced usual care (n=100), with blinded outcome assessors. At 6 months, MB-BP reduced systolic BP 5.9 mmHg from baseline (95% CI -9.1 to -2.8) and outperformed control by 4.5 mmHg (95% CI -9.0 to -0.1). Candidate mechanisms whose estimates excluded zero were reduced sedentary activity (-350.8 sitting min/wk, 95% CI -636.5 to -65.1) and increased mindfulness score (7.3, 95% CI 3.0-11.6); the DASH diet estimate crossed zero (0.32, 95% CI -0.04 to 0.67). Loss to follow-up was 17.4%.

  5. Effect of mindfulness-based interventions on people with prehypertension or hypertension: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
    Chen Q et al., 2024 · PMID 38350849 · positive

    12 trials, 715 participants, interventions of 8 weeks in 10 trials and 6 weeks in one. Pooled reductions of 9.12 mmHg systolic (95% CI -12.18 to -6.05) and 5.66 mmHg diastolic (-8.88 to -2.43), plus reductions in anxiety, depression and perceived stress. Effect sizes are roughly double those found in the better-controlled meta-analysis of the same literature, and the authors call for larger trials with sufficient statistical power and long-term follow-up.

  6. Benefits of mindfulness meditation in reducing blood pressure and stress in patients with arterial hypertension
    Ponte Marquez PH et al., 2019 · PMID 30425326 · mixed

    42 patients with high-normal BP or grade I hypertension (24 intervention, 18 control), 2 hours weekly over 8 weeks. At week 8 the mindfulness group had lower 24-hour ambulatory BP than controls (124/77 vs 126/80 mmHg, P<0.05) and lower night-time systolic BP (108/65 vs 114/69 mmHg, P<0.05), plus lower clinic systolic BP (130 vs 133 mmHg, P=0.02).   7. Beyond medications and diet: alternative approaches to lowering blood pressure: a scientific statement from the American Heart Association
    Brook RD et al., 2013 · PMID 23608661 · negative

    AHA scientific statement graded Transcendental Meditation as Class IIB, Level of Evidence B, and other meditation techniques as Class III, Level of Evidence C — the latter meaning not recommended. For migraine & headache

Mindfulness training has been studied for migraine, and the results are genuinely mixed rather than clear-cut. Some reviews find it does not reduce how many migraine days people get; the largest and newest one finds a small reduction. What shows up most consistently is not fewer attacks but a better relationship with the pain — less catastrophising, lower anxiety and depression scores. Be careful with the common claim that it reduces disability: when all the trials are pooled, that effect does not hold up. One larger trial in people with chronic migraine who were overusing painkillers did show a real drop in headache days.

Safety

Mindfulness-based programs were well tolerated in migraine trials. The largest single trial reported one adverse event, judged unrelated to the study protocol, and no safety signal has emerged across the pooled literature. Harms are generally under-reported in behavioural trials, so the honest statement is that no signal has appeared rather than that safety has been rigorously established. The realistic costs are time and access: standard MBSR is eight weekly two-hour classes plus daily home practice, a substantial commitment, and retention across trials was around 73%.

Who should be careful: No medical contraindications identified in the migraine trials. Intensive meditation practice is generally approached with caution and clinician involvement in people with psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe untreated PTSD or acute severe depression, since sustained internal attention can be destabilising for some; migraine trials did not enrol or specifically study these groups. A new or changing headache pattern needs a diagnosis before behavioural self-management. Standard MBSR includes yoga postures, so the physical cautions listed for yoga also apply.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. In every trial it was added to, not substituted for, usual medical care — including in the chronic migraine trial, where it was layered on top of medication withdrawal and tailored drug prophylaxis. Note that the largest pooled analysis found no significant reduction in analgesic or triptan use, so it should not be expected to replace acute medication.

What the evidence says

All evidence is PREVENTIVE; mindfulness has not been tested as an acute abortive treatment. The pooled picture on attack frequency depends heavily on which synthesis you read. Two small syntheses were null: a meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (185 participants) in chronic HEADACHE — only one of which was a migraine trial, two being tension-type headache and two mixed samples — found no improvement in headache frequency (SMD 0.00, 95% CI -0.33 to 0.32), duration (SMD -0.08, -1.03 to 0.87) or pain intensity (SMD -0.78, -1.72 to 0.16); and a migraine-specific meta-analysis of 4 RCTs (275 patients) found no significant benefit for migraine frequency (SMD -0.23, -0.79 to 0.32) while finding a significant benefit for depressive symptoms (SMD -0.59, -0.93 to -0.25), concluding the evidence base is insufficient to support recommendations. Against those, the largest and most recent migraine-specific meta-analysis (14 RCTs, 1027 participants) found small but statistically significant effects on pain frequency (SMD 0.33, 95% CI 0.11-0.54), pain intensity (0.23, 0.11-0.35), quality of life (0.24), anxiety (0.37), depression (0.26) and pain catastrophizing (0.57) — but NO significant effect on activity-related disability (0.18, -0.01 to 0.38), analgesic use (0.07) or triptan use (0.04). The largest single episodic-migraine trial (89 adults) found MBSR did NOT reduce migraine days more than headache education (-1.6 vs -2.0 per month, P=.50) but did improve disability (difference 5.92, 95% CI 2.8-9.0, P<.001), quality of life, self-efficacy, pain catastrophizing and depression out to 36 weeks. A 98-patient trial of enhanced MBSR against stress management for headache did meet its endpoint: headache days fell from 7.8 to 4.6 versus 7.7 to 6.0 (P=0.04), with 52% versus 23% achieving 50% reduction (P=0.004), though no group differences remained at 52 weeks. The strongest positive result is in a different population: a phase-III trial of 177 patients with chronic migraine AND medication overuse headache found 78.4% of those receiving six mindfulness sessions added to usual care achieved 50% or greater headache-frequency reduction at 12 months versus 48.3% with usual care alone (p<0.0001). Note the disability signal is genuinely contested — one trial and one evidence review favour mindfulness over education, while the largest pooled estimate is null. A 2025 evidence review rated mindfulness-based therapies as possibly reducing attack frequency at LOW strength of evidence. Standardized MBSR includes yoga postures, so this literature overlaps with the yoga evidence.

Doses studied: Standard MBSR: eight weekly 2-hour group classes plus a retreat day and daily home practice (median attendance 7 of 8 classes). Enhanced MBSR extended to weekly for 8 weeks then biweekly to 16-20 weeks. The chronic migraine trial used six weekly group mindfulness sessions plus 7-10 minutes of daily self-practice.

Why this grade: The migraine-specific randomized evidence is thin and internally inconsistent on the headline outcome. Two meta-analyses pooling only 4-5 RCTs (185-275 participants) found no significant reduction in headache frequency (SMD 0.00 and -0.23, both confidence intervals crossing zero), and the single best-known trial, published in JAMA Internal Medicine, missed its primary endpoint: MBSR did not reduce migraine days more than headache education. Against that, the largest and most recent meta-analysis (14 RCTs, 1027 participants) did find a small but significant effect on attack frequency (SMD 0.33) — while finding NO significant effect on activity-related disability or on analgesic and triptan use, which undercuts the common claim that mindfulness reliably reduces migraine's functional burden. One positive 98-patient trial on headache days and one strong phase-III trial (n=177) in chronic migraine with medication overuse headache are real. Graded early rather than moderate because the constituent trials are small and mostly at high risk of bias, the two syntheses that made migraine frequency their primary question were null, and the most rigorous strength-of-evidence rating remains LOW.
