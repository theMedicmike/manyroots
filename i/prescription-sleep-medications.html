Conventional medicine

Prescription sleep medications (z-drugs, benzodiazepines, orexin antagonists)

Moderate evidence · for insomnia

For insomnia

Prescription sleep medicines really do help most people fall asleep somewhat faster and stay asleep a bit longer, at least over weeks — but the improvement is smaller than many people expect, often 10 to 25 minutes. Sleep specialists consider a talking-and-habits therapy called CBT-I the first choice, with medication as a short-term backup decided together with your doctor. These medicines also carry real downsides, especially for older adults: next-day grogginess, memory and coordination problems, and for some of them, dependence.

Safety

Harms are the central issue with this class. In adults over 60, meta-analysis found adverse cognitive events 4.78 times and daytime fatigue 3.82 times more common than placebo, with concern for falls and cognitive impairment. Network meta-analysis found benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, zolpidem, and zopiclone produced more side effects than placebo, and zolpidem/zopiclone caused more adverse-event dropouts. Benzodiazepines and z-drugs carry known risks of tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, and next-day psychomotor impairment affecting driving; orexin antagonists showed placebo-comparable adverse-event rates in phase 3 trials but long-term safety data remain limited. Guidelines frame all of these as short-term options after CBT-I.

Who should be careful: Older adults are at elevated risk of falls, fractures, and cognitive harms and guidelines urge particular caution; people with untreated sleep apnea or severe respiratory disease, a history of substance-use disorder (benzodiazepines/z-drugs), or complex sleep behaviors on prior use should generally avoid these agents; orexin antagonists are not used in narcolepsy. Not established as safe in pregnancy.

Interactions: Additive central-nervous-system depression with alcohol, opioids, and other sedatives can be dangerous; several agents (e.g., z-drugs, suvorexant, daridorexant) are metabolized via CYP3A4, so strong inhibitors or inducers can markedly change drug levels. A prescriber or pharmacist should review the full medication list.

What the evidence says

The 2017 AASM clinical practice guideline suggests specific agents for chronic insomnia — suvorexant, eszopiclone, zaleplon, zolpidem, triazolam, temazepam, ramelteon, and doxepin — but grades every recommendation WEAK, and the 2016 ACP guideline strongly recommends CBT-I as initial treatment, reserving pharmacotherapy for shared decision-making when CBT-I alone fails. A 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis of 170 trials (47,950 participants) found benzodiazepines, doxylamine, eszopiclone, lemborexant, seltorexant, zolpidem, and zopiclone more efficacious than placebo acutely (SMD range 0.36-0.83), but with poor tolerability for several agents (e.g., zolpidem dropouts due to adverse events OR 1.79 vs placebo) and scarce long-term data. A meta-analysis of FDA-submitted z-drug trials found polysomnographic sleep latency reduced by 22 minutes versus placebo, an effect the authors judged small and of questionable clinical importance. For orexin antagonists, in the first of two phase 3 trials daridorexant 50 mg reduced wake-after-sleep-onset by 22.8 minutes and latency to persistent sleep by 11.4 minutes versus placebo at month 1, with adverse-event rates comparable to placebo across both trials, while the 10 mg dose tested in the second trial showed no significant polysomnographic benefit. Against this, a meta-analysis in adults over 60 found small benefit (total sleep time +25.2 minutes; sleep-quality effect size 0.14) against markedly increased harms — adverse cognitive events 4.78 times and daytime fatigue 3.82 times more common than placebo — concluding benefits may not justify risks in older people.

Doses studied: Agent-specific nightly dosing in trials, e.g., zolpidem 5-10 mg, eszopiclone 2-3 mg, zaleplon 5-20 mg, temazepam 7.5-30 mg, suvorexant 10-20 mg, daridorexant 25-50 mg; guidelines frame use as short-term.

Why this grade: Large RCT and meta-analytic evidence base shows consistent short-term benefit, but effect sizes are modest (z-drugs shorten objectively measured sleep latency by about 22 minutes; acute-treatment SMDs 0.36-0.83 in the 2022 Lancet network meta-analysis), every drug-specific AASM 2017 recommendation is graded WEAK, the ACP guideline positions all medication as second-line after CBT-I, long-term efficacy data are sparse, and harms are significant especially in older adults. Torn between strong and moderate, the conservative rule and the weak/second-line guideline positioning settle it at moderate.

Sources (6)
  1. Clinical Practice Guideline for the Pharmacologic Treatment of Chronic Insomnia in Adults: An American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guideline
    Sateia MJ, Buysse DJ, Krystal AD, Neubauer DN, Heald JL (2017) · PMID 27998379 · positive

    AASM suggests suvorexant, eszopiclone, zaleplon, zolpidem, triazolam, temazepam, ramelteon, and doxepin for sleep-onset and/or sleep-maintenance insomnia versus no treatment — but every recommendation is graded WEAK, reflecting low certainty in the evidence.

  2. Management of Chronic Insomnia Disorder in Adults: A Clinical Practice Guideline From the American College of Physicians
    Qaseem A, Kansagara D, Forciea MA, Cooke M, Denberg TD (2016) · PMID 27136449 · mixed

    ACP strongly recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for chronic insomnia; adding short-term medication is only a weak recommendation (low-quality evidence) via shared decision-making when CBT-I alone is unsuccessful — medications are not first-line.

  3. Comparative effects of pharmacological interventions for the acute and long-term management of insomnia disorder in adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
    De Crescenzo F, D'Alò GL, Ostinelli EG, et al. (2022) · PMID 35843245 · mixed

    Across 170 trials (47,950 participants), benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, lemborexant, zolpidem, zopiclone and others beat placebo acutely (SMD 0.36-0.83), but zolpidem and zopiclone caused more adverse-event dropouts than placebo (OR 1.79 and 2.00), long-term data were largely very low certainty, and many licensed drugs showed poor tolerability.

  4. Effectiveness of non-benzodiazepine hypnotics in treatment of adult insomnia: meta-analysis of data submitted to the Food and Drug Administration
    Huedo-Medina TB, Kirsch I, Middlemass J, Klonizakis M, Siriwardena AN (2012) · PMID 23248080 · mixed

    In 13 FDA-submitted trials (4,378 participants), z-drugs reduced polysomnographic sleep latency by 22 minutes versus placebo (95% CI -33 to -11); the drug effect was rather small and of questionable clinical importance, with no significant effects demonstrated on secondary outcomes.

  5. Safety and efficacy of daridorexant in patients with insomnia disorder: results from two multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 trials
    Mignot E, Mayleben D, Fietze I, et al. (2022) · PMID 35065036 · positive

    In study 1, daridorexant 50 mg reduced wake after sleep onset by 22.8 minutes and latency to persistent sleep by 11.4 minutes versus placebo at month 1 and improved self-reported total sleep time by 22.1 minutes, with adverse-event rates comparable to placebo (38% vs 34%); the 10 mg dose in study 2 showed no significant polysomnographic benefit.

  6. Sedative hypnotics in older people with insomnia: meta-analysis of risks and benefits
    Glass J, Lanctôt KL, Herrmann N, Sproule BA, Busto UE (2005) · PMID 16284208 · negative

    In 24 RCTs in adults 60 and over (2,417 participants), benefits were small (total sleep time +25.2 minutes; sleep-quality effect size 0.14) while adverse cognitive events were 4.78 times, psychomotor events 2.61 times (a comparison the abstract reports as P > 0.05), and daytime fatigue 3.82 times more common than placebo; the authors concluded benefits may not justify the risks in older people.
