Sleep habits (sleep hygiene, stimulus control and insomnia therapy)

Moderate evidence · for fatigue & low energy Moderate evidence · for insomnia Early evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For fatigue & low energy

There is a real difference between generic sleep tips and the specific behavioural methods sleep specialists use. Getting out of bed when you cannot sleep and keeping the same wake time every day (stimulus control) is a genuine treatment with guideline backing. A list of sleep hygiene advice on its own is not, and sleep organisations specifically advise against using it as the only treatment. Fixing sleep does help daytime energy, but usually less than it helps the nights — and in the first few weeks these methods can make you sleepier and slower before they make you better.

Safety

Behavioural sleep methods are low-risk overall, but not risk-free in the first weeks, and this is measured rather than theoretical: in a laboratory study of single-component sleep restriction, objective total sleep time fell by 69-91 minutes a night, psychomotor vigilance lapses and reaction times worsened, and daytime sleepiness scores rose across the first three weeks, all resolving by 3 months (Kyle 2014). That matters for driving, operating machinery, shift work and any safety-critical job. Getting out of bed repeatedly at night carries fall risk in frail or older adults and in anyone with orthostatic intolerance. If fatigue is the main complaint and sleep already looks adequate on a sleep diary, restricting time in bed is the wrong tool.

Who should be careful: Sleep restriction and aggressive stimulus control should be used cautiously or avoided without specialist supervision in bipolar disorder (sleep loss can precipitate mania), in epilepsy or other seizure disorders (sleep deprivation lowers seizure threshold), in untreated obstructive sleep apnoea, in parasomnias, and in people whose work already involves shift rotation or long-haul driving. Loud snoring with witnessed pauses, morning headaches or unrefreshing sleep despite adequate hours point to sleep apnoea and need a sleep study, not sleep advice.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions, but combining behavioural methods with sedative-hypnotics (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs) or sedating antihistamines increases next-day impairment during the early sleepiness phase, and any tapering of a hypnotic alongside behavioural treatment should be done with the prescriber rather than independently.

What the evidence says

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 guideline gives a strong recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I in chronic insomnia in adults, conditional recommendations for stimulus control, sleep restriction and relaxation as single-component therapies, and a conditional recommendation that clinicians NOT use sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy (Edinger 2021, guideline), supported by a systematic review that screened 1,244 studies, included 124 and analysed 89 (Edinger 2021, review). A meta-analysis of sleep hygiene education across 15 studies found significant but only small-to-medium pre-post improvements and significantly lower efficacy than CBT-I with medium-to-large differences; improvements averaged about 5% in sleep-diary sleep efficiency pre-post with an 8% gap to CBT-I and about two points on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, only subjective measures reached significance, and notably none of the 15 studies compared sleep hygiene with sham or no treatment (Chung 2018). On daytime outcomes specifically, a network meta-analysis of 86 studies (15,578 participants) found CBT-I significantly improved fatigue, daytime sleepiness, functioning and quality of life with Cohen's d ranging from -0.52 to 0.81, but the authors emphasise these are predominantly small-to-moderate effects compared with far stronger effects on the core symptoms of insomnia (Benz 2020). In cancer survivors, a meta-analysis of 19 RCTs (1,803 participants) found CBT-I improved insomnia severity by 4.4 points (95% CI -5.3 to -3.5; 13 trials) — short of the 6-point minimal clinically important difference but above half the minimal important change, which the authors read as an appreciable rather than a large number of patients benefiting — while fatigue (11 trials) improved without on average reaching its clinical-importance threshold, at low-to-very-low certainty (Cooper 2025). The daytime cost of these methods is real and measured: acute sleep restriction therapy cut objective total sleep time by 69-91 minutes a night, worsened psychomotor vigilance and raised daytime sleepiness scores for the first three weeks before all measures returned to baseline by 3 months (Kyle 2014). A public-health review notes that although each individual sleep hygiene recommendation has some epidemiologic or experimental support, the direct effects of the recommendations on sleep in the general population remain largely untested (Irish 2015).

Doses studied: Stimulus control is taught either alone or as one component of multicomponent CBT-I over a course of weekly sessions; the core instructions are to use the bed only for sleep and sex, get out of bed when unable to sleep, and keep a fixed wake time regardless of how the night went. Sleep hygiene education programmes commonly cover general knowledge about sleep, substance use, regular exercise and bedroom arrangement; sleep-wake regularity and avoidance of daytime naps featured in seven of fifteen programmes reviewed and stress management in only five (Chung 2018). Sleep restriction was delivered as a single-component 4-week protocol in the trial that measured its acute daytime costs (Kyle 2014).

Why this grade: These two things have different evidence and are graded together at the level of the weaker honest summary. Stimulus control carries a conditional guideline recommendation as a standalone therapy for insomnia and sits inside multicomponent CBT-I, which carries a strong guideline recommendation. But the target here is fatigue, not insomnia, and two gaps hold the grade down. First, the fatigue evidence belongs to CBT-I as a whole package (a network meta-analysis of 86 studies) — no cited trial tests stimulus control as a single component with fatigue as the primary outcome, so its fatigue benefit is inferred, not demonstrated. Second, effects on daytime fatigue are consistently smaller than effects on sleep itself, and one cancer-survivor meta-analysis found fatigue improvement that on average did not reach its clinical-importance threshold, at low-to-very-low certainty. Sleep hygiene education used on its own would be graded not_supported if graded separately: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine specifically recommends against it as a single-component therapy. Moderate for the package; the two components are not equivalent and should never be presented as such.

For insomnia

These are the habit-based pieces of insomnia care. Stimulus control — using your bed only for sleep, getting up when you can't sleep, and keeping a fixed wake time — has real trial evidence behind it on its own. Generic sleep-hygiene tips (avoid caffeine, keep the room dark, and so on) are sensible, but studies show that as a treatment by itself sleep hygiene doesn't do much, and guidelines actually advise against relying on it alone.

Safety

Both practices are benign and well tolerated in trials. The main documented harm of sleep hygiene is indirect: used alone it is largely ineffective for chronic insomnia, so relying on it can delay effective treatment (CBT-I). No adverse events attributable to stimulus control were highlighted in the AASM evidence review.

Who should be careful: Stimulus control instructs getting out of bed during night wakings, which carries a fall risk for older adults or anyone with impaired mobility or balance — adapt with counter-control (sitting up in bed) where needed. As with any approach that can reduce time asleep short-term, use caution where sleep loss is destabilizing (bipolar disorder, seizure disorders).

Interactions: None known; these are behavioral practices with no drug interactions.

What the evidence says

The evidence splits sharply between the two practices. The 2021 AASM guideline conditionally recommends stimulus control as a single-component therapy for chronic insomnia, while conditionally recommending AGAINST sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy — based on a systematic review of 124 studies with GRADE assessment. A meta-analysis of 15 sleep-hygiene-education studies found only small-to-medium pre-post improvements (about 5% in diary sleep efficiency, 2 points on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index), significantly worse than CBT-I (medium-to-large difference), with no sham- or no-treatment-controlled trials identified. A 241-trial component network meta-analysis found sleep hygiene education was not an essential component (incremental OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.77-1.32), whereas stimulus control was associated with remission (iOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.00-2.05) and with improved sleep efficiency and sleep latency. In practice, both are usually folded into multicomponent CBT-I rather than used alone.

Doses studied: Stimulus control: standard instruction set (bed only for sleep, leave the bedroom when unable to sleep, fixed wake time, no daytime napping) delivered over one to several sessions. Sleep hygiene education: typically 1-2 educational sessions covering sleep knowledge, substance use, exercise, and bedroom environment.

Why this grade: These two practices grade very differently and are graded together conservatively. Stimulus control has RCT support as a standalone therapy and a conditional AASM recommendation FOR its use. Sleep hygiene education alone, however, is conditionally recommended AGAINST by the AASM, was inferior to CBT-I in meta-analysis, and contributed essentially nothing (iOR 1.01) as a component in a 241-trial network meta-analysis. The moderate grade reflects stimulus control; sleep hygiene as a standalone treatment is closer to not_supported.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

People who regularly sleep less than six hours, or who struggle to stay asleep, are clearly more likely to develop type 2 diabetes, and cutting healthy volunteers to four hours a night measurably worsens how their bodies handle sugar. What we do not yet know is whether improving your sleep lowers your blood sugar. Small studies of insomnia therapy point in a helpful direction, but the pooled evidence is shaky and the most carefully run recent trial found no change in HbA1c at all. Good sleep is worth pursuing for many reasons; treat its blood-sugar benefit as promising rather than proven, and not as a reason to change any medication.

Safety

Improving sleep is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication and is not a reason to change any dose. Behavioural sleep interventions are generally well tolerated; the main adverse effects are transient sleepiness, irritability and reduced concentration in the first weeks. In the one recent RCT in type 2 diabetes the CBT-I group gained a small amount of weight (BMI +0.29 kg/m2) over 6 months. Sleep-extension approaches carry no known harm beyond the practical cost of time, but their glycaemic benefit is unproven.

Who should be careful: The stimulus-control and sleep-restriction components of CBT-I deliberately shorten time in bed at first and cause temporary daytime sleepiness, so they should be supervised or avoided in people who drive for a living or operate machinery, and are not recommended without specialist input in bipolar disorder, seizure disorders, parasomnias, or untreated obstructive sleep apnoea. Sleep apnoea is common in type 2 diabetes and obesity and should be screened for before poor sleep is put down to habits: loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses or morning headaches warrant assessment.

Interactions: The behavioural components themselves have no drug interactions. If sedating medicines (hypnotics, sedating antihistamines, some antidepressants) are already in use, do not stop them abruptly; ask the prescriber how to combine them with CBT-I. Long sleep (over 8-9 hours) is also associated with higher diabetes risk in cohort studies, so 'more sleep' should not be pursued beyond the 7-8 hour range.

What the evidence says

Everything measured in this evidence base is a surrogate — HbA1c, fasting glucose, insulin resistance, glucose tolerance, energy intake — and the human data linking sleep to diabetes risk are observational. Prospective cohort meta-analyses show sleep quantity and quality predict incident type 2 diabetes: across 10 studies (13 cohorts, 107,756 participants, 3,586 cases, follow-up 4.2-32 years) short sleep of 5-6 hours or less carried RR 1.28 (95% CI 1.03-1.60), long sleep over 8-9 hours RR 1.48 (1.13-1.96), difficulty initiating sleep RR 1.57 (1.25-1.97) and difficulty maintaining sleep RR 1.84 (1.39-2.43); a dose-response meta-analysis of 482,502 participants and 18,443 cases found a U-shape with lowest risk at 7-8 hours, RR 1.09 (1.04-1.15) per hour shorter below 7 hours and 1.14 (1.03-1.26) per hour longer above it. Experimentally, restricting 11 healthy young men to 4 hours in bed for 6 nights lowered glucose tolerance (P<0.02) and raised evening cortisol and sympathetic activity, so the mechanism is real in healthy people over days. Intervention data measuring glycaemia are limited and mixed: a 2025 meta-analysis of 24 studies found CBT-I and/or sleep hygiene lowered HbA1c by 0.27 percentage points (95% CI 0.07-0.47, I-squared 74%; 12 studies, n=2044) overall and by 0.43 (0.19-0.67, I-squared 59%; 9 studies, n=1911) in type 2 diabetes, but found no significant change in fasting glucose, no significant effect from any sleep-extension intervention, and rated only 9 of the 24 studies as low concern for quality. The largest recent individual RCT (57 adults with type 2 diabetes and insomnia symptoms, online CBT-I versus care as usual, 6 months) found no significant between-group difference in HbA1c or other glycaemic or metabolic outcomes by intention to treat, despite improvements in insomnia and depressive symptoms and a small BMI increase of 0.29 kg/m2; only half of the intervention group completed the therapy, and the per-protocol HbA1c change was also not statistically significant. A very small 6-week sleep-extension RCT in 18 overweight male short sleepers increased actigraphic sleep by 79 versus 6 minutes and improved HOMA-IR (-0.51, 95% CI -0.98 to -0.03, versus +0.28), fasting insulin and blood pressure, but had no HbA1c measure. An 80-person RCT in adults with overweight who habitually slept under 6.5 hours extended sleep by about 1.2 hours per night and cut objectively measured energy intake by 270 kcal/day (95% CI -393 to -147) with resulting weight reduction — a plausible indirect route to better glycaemia rather than a direct glycaemic effect. No trial has tested a sleep intervention against progression to diabetes, or against cardiovascular, kidney or mortality outcomes.

Doses studied: Habitual sleep of 7-8 hours per night is the range associated with lowest diabetes risk in cohort meta-analyses. Intervention trials used cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia delivered online or in person (sleep hygiene, stimulus control and sleep scheduling) over roughly 6 sessions; a 6-week CBT-based sleep-extension programme that added 79 minutes of actigraphic sleep per night; and a single individualised sleep-hygiene counselling session targeting an 8.5-hour bedtime, which added about 1.2 hours of sleep per night over 2 weeks.

Why this grade: The link between short or disrupted sleep and diabetes risk rests on large prospective cohort meta-analyses (relative risks 1.28 for short sleep, 1.84 for difficulty maintaining sleep) and on a laboratory study showing that restricting healthy young men to 4 hours in bed lowers glucose tolerance. That is association plus mechanism, not proof that fixing sleep improves anyone's blood sugar. The intervention evidence is thin and internally inconsistent: a 2025 meta-analysis of CBT-I and sleep-hygiene trials pooled a -0.43 percentage-point HbA1c change in type 2 diabetes but with high heterogeneity, a null result for fasting glucose, a null result for every sleep-extension intervention, and only 9 of 24 studies rated low concern for quality; the best-designed recent RCT (online CBT-I in type 2 diabetes) found no significant HbA1c difference by intention to treat, with only half of participants completing the therapy. Every outcome is a surrogate; no trial has tested progression to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney disease or death. Torn between moderate and early, the lower grade applies.
