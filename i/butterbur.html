Butterbur (Petasites hybridus)

Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache

For migraine & headache

Butterbur, taken every day, cut migraine attacks roughly in half in its biggest study — genuinely one of the better results for any plant in this condition. But it has injured people's livers at the ordinary doses used for migraine: in one published series nine people developed severe liver injury, and two of them needed a liver transplant. Current reviews therefore pair 'it appears to work' with a real warning and point people to national safety advice before using it at all; if it is ever considered, only a product certified free of the plant's liver-toxic alkaloids belongs in the conversation, and not without a doctor watching liver tests.

Safety

This is the entry where a real effect and a serious documented harm sit side by side, and both belong on the page. The hazard is not theoretical and not confined to animal doses: in a published case series of the branded extract, patients taking ordinary therapeutic doses of 50-225 mg/day for 4 to 730 days developed herb-induced liver injury; most had mild biochemistry changes, but nine developed severe injury (ALT 3-153-fold, AST 2-104-fold the upper limit of normal, average onset 103 days) and two required liver transplantation, with biopsies showing necrosis, steatosis, cholestasis and bile duct proliferation. Causality was rated likely in 3 and possible in 13 of the reported cases, and 22 patients were taking hepatotoxic co-medications, so the authors characterise the events as rare, idiosyncratic and often confounded — but they are real, human, and occurred at label-range doses (PMID 31083451). Separately, raw Petasites contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are hepatotoxic with genotoxic and carcinogenic potential; their presence is precisely why the raw plant cannot be used directly, and the pharmaceutical objective has been alkaloid-free extracts (PMID 23277154), with regulators requiring very low threshold concentrations and driving development of trace-level assays (PMID 26094206). Mechanistic work found no hepatocellular toxicity at an estimated therapeutic Cmax of 60 ng/mL but transaminase and bilirubin elevations at roughly 200-fold therapeutic dosing, with toxicity in human hepatocytes only from petasin-rich extracts (PMID 19770483). A more reassuring 2022 narrative review reported a no-observed-adverse-effect level at 15-fold the maximal clinical dose in rats, bile duct hyperplasia only at 45-90-fold, no hepatotoxicity in primary human hepatocytes at therapeutic levels, and no relevant liver-function abnormalities in the randomized trials (PMID 35585841); read it alongside the case series rather than instead of it, noting it is a narrative review of the branded product drawing partly on unpublished company study reports, and that the earlier paper concluding the extract 'is safe for the treatment in humans' was authored from the manufacturer, Weber & Weber (PMID 12864764). Day-to-day tolerability is otherwise good: mild gastrointestinal events, predominantly burping (PMID 15623680); in a 120-patient real-world series 28.3% reported a bitter taste and/or eructation lasting 6.4 +/- 2.7 days (PMID 41908273). The current review position is that efficacy evidence and hepatotoxicity concern coexist and national safety advisories should be consulted first (PMID 39853578).

Who should be careful: Do not use any butterbur product not certified free of pyrrolizidine alkaloids. Unstandardised butterbur, home preparations and teas made from the raw plant are unsafe, because the toxic alkaloid content is what limits direct use of the plant (PMID 23277154). Anyone with existing liver disease, or taking other drugs with hepatic toxicity, should not take butterbur outside medical supervision — and given that severe injury occurred at label-range doses with an average onset around 103 days, anyone using it needs baseline and ongoing liver-function monitoring and should stop immediately at any sign of liver trouble such as jaundice, dark urine, right-upper-quadrant pain or unexplained fatigue (PMID 31083451). It has not been established as safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Butterbur is in the Asteraceae (daisy) family, so it should be avoided by anyone with known daisy-family allergy. The single pediatric randomized trial did not show superiority to placebo at end of treatment, so there is no efficacy basis for accepting this hepatic risk in children in place of safer options (PMID 17659990).

Interactions: Formal drug-interaction studies in migraine patients have not been published. The principal concern is additive hepatic burden with other hepatotoxic medications or with alcohol — and this is not hypothetical: 22 of the reported liver-injury patients were taking hepatotoxic co-medications, especially during periods of pain, and human hepatocyte studies combining petasins with therapeutic ibuprofen, paracetamol and naratriptan concentrations produced transaminase and CYP-monooxygenase changes (PMID 31083451). That directly implicates the analgesics a migraine patient is most likely to be taking alongside it. Mechanistic work also identified impaired bile acid transport (PMID 19770483). Because current reviews route this decision through national safety advisories rather than treating it as a self-care choice (PMID 39853578), butterbur should be started only with clinician oversight and a defined liver-function monitoring plan.

What the evidence says

Butterbur has been studied purely as a daily preventive; no randomized trial has tested it as an acute treatment during an attack. In the pivotal three-arm trial (n=245), Petasites root extract 75 mg twice daily reduced attack frequency by 48% from baseline over four months versus 26% on placebo (p=0.0012), with 68% versus 49% of patients achieving a 50% or greater reduction (p<0.05); the 50 mg twice-daily arm fell 36% and did not separate significantly from placebo, even though an earlier 60-patient trial of the same product had reported a significant benefit at that dose. A systematic review found only two adult randomized trials, 293 patients in total, both of the same proprietary root extract, could not pool them, and concluded there was moderate evidence for the higher-than-recommended 150 mg/day dose pending further rigorous study of efficacy and long-term safety. The only pediatric randomized trial (n=58 across three arms) found butterbur was not superior to placebo at the end of 12 weeks — only music therapy was (p=0.005) — with butterbur separating from placebo only at six-month follow-up (p=0.044), and all groups improving substantially during baseline. The safety literature is where this entry turns: a dedicated case series of herb-induced liver injury with the branded extract found that while most affected patients had mild biochemistry changes at 50-225 mg/day, nine developed severe injury (ALT 3-153-fold and AST 2-104-fold the upper limit of normal, average onset 103 days), and two required liver transplantation, with causality rated likely in 3 and possible in 13 cases and 22 patients taking hepatotoxic co-medications. Supporting toxicology found no hepatocellular toxicity at an estimated therapeutic Cmax of 60 ng/mL but transaminase and bilirubin elevations at roughly 200-fold therapeutic doses, and toxicity in human hepatocytes only from petasin-rich (37% petasin) extracts; a 2025 review states the position bluntly, that strong efficacy evidence and hepatotoxicity concern coexist and prescribers should consult national safety advisories before recommending it.

Doses studied: Preventive only; no trial has tested butterbur taken during an attack. Petasites hybridus root extract 75 mg twice daily (150 mg/day) was the effective dose in the pivotal 245-patient trial; 50 mg twice daily (100 mg/day) reduced attacks 36% and did not separate from placebo there, although the earlier 60-patient trial reported a significant benefit at that same 100 mg/day dose. Treatment periods ran 3-4 months. The systematic review notes 150 mg/day is higher than the label-recommended dose. Reported liver injury occurred across a dose range of 50 to 225 mg/day.

Why this grade: The efficacy signal is real — a 245-patient RCT showed 48% versus 26% attack reduction and the 2012 AAN/AHS guideline rated Petasites Level A. But it rests on only two adult randomized trials (293 patients total, per systematic review) of a single proprietary product line, with no independent replication; the two trials disagree with each other on the 100 mg/day dose (significant in the 60-patient trial, not significant in the 245-patient trial); the single pediatric RCT was not superior to placebo at end of treatment; and the Level A rating is contested in the guideline literature and predates the 2019 liver-injury case series. Grading conservatively, that is moderate, not strong. Critically, this grade describes EFFICACY ONLY and is not a benefit-risk verdict: butterbur is the clearest case in this condition of a real effect and a serious documented harm coexisting, and the hepatotoxicity in the safety section — which includes two liver transplants at ordinary therapeutic doses — is not offset by the efficacy grade.

Sources (9)
  1. Petasites hybridus root (butterbur) is an effective preventive treatment for migraine.
    Lipton, Gobel, Einhaupl, Wilks & Mauskop, 2004 (Neurology) · PMID 15623680 · positive

    Three-arm randomized trial, 245 patients, Petasites extract 75 mg bid versus 50 mg bid versus placebo over four months. Per-protocol attack frequency fell 48% (75 mg bid, p=0.0012 vs placebo), 36% (50 mg bid, p=0.127, not significant) and 26% (placebo). At four months, 68% of the 75 mg arm versus 49% of placebo had a 50% or greater reduction (p<0.05). Most frequent treatment-related adverse reactions were mild gastrointestinal events, predominantly burping. Note the 50 mg bid arm's failure to separate from placebo conflicts with the earlier 60-patient trial of the same product at that dose.

  2. Hepatobiliary Events in Migraine Therapy with Herbs-The Case of Petadolex, A Petasites Hybridus Extract.
    Anderson & Borlak, 2019 (J Clin Med) · PMID 31083451 · negative

    Case series of herb-induced liver injury (HILI) reported with the branded butterbur extract at ORDINARY THERAPEUTIC doses of 50 to 225 mg/day over 4 to 730 days of treatment. Most patients had mild serum biochemistry changes (<3-fold upper limit of normal), but NINE developed severe HILI with ALT 3-153-fold and AST 2-104-fold the upper limit of normal and average time-to-onset of 103 days. Cases resolved after medication withdrawal, but TWO PATIENTS REQUIRED LIVER TRANSPLANTATION. Biopsies showed an inconsistent injury pattern: necrosis, macrovesicular steatosis, inflammation, cholestasis and bile duct proliferation. Causality was rated likely in 3 cases, possible in 13, unlikely in 8 and unclassifiable in 24; 22 patients reported hepatotoxic co-medications. Authors conclude the cases are rare, idiosyncratic and frequently confounded by co-medication, and call for a physician-supervised plan to minimise risk.

  3. An extract of Petasites hybridus is effective in the prophylaxis of migraine.
    Grossmann & Schmidramsl, 2000 (Int J Clin Pharmacol Ther) · PMID 11020030 · positive

    Randomized, group-parallel, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial of a CO2 extract from Petasites hybridus rhizome; after a 4-week run-in, 60 patients received two 25 mg capsules twice daily (100 mg/day) or placebo for 12 weeks. Migraine attack frequency decreased by a maximum of 60% from baseline, significant versus placebo (p<0.05); no adverse events were reported. This is the second and only other adult randomized trial, of the same proprietary product as the pivotal trial, so it does not constitute independent replication — and its positive result at 100 mg/day is inconsistent with the later, larger trial in which that dose failed to separate from placebo.

  4. Effectiveness of Petasites hybridus preparations in the prophylaxis of migraine: a systematic review.
    Agosti, Duke, Chrubasik & Chrubasik, 2006 (Phytomedicine) · PMID 16987643 · mixed

    Only two randomized controlled trials totalling 293 patients (60 and 233) met inclusion criteria, and both investigated the same proprietary root extract; data could not be pooled because of heterogeneity of results. Conclusion was moderate evidence of effectiveness for a higher-than-recommended dose (150 mg), with further rigorous studies required to confirm effectiveness and safety in long-term use before butterbur root extract can be recommended as an alternative option for migraine prophylaxis.

  5. Butterbur root extract and music therapy in the prevention of childhood migraine: an explorative study.
    Oelkers-Ax, Leins, Parzer, Hillecke, Bolay, Fischer, Bender, Hermanns & Resch, 2008 (Eur J Pain) · PMID 17659990 · mixed

    Randomized, partly double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in primary-school children: butterbur root extract (n=19), music therapy (n=20) or placebo (n=19) over 12 weeks. In the post-treatment period only music therapy was superior to placebo (p=0.005); butterbur was NOT. Butterbur separated from placebo only in the 8-week follow-up period six months later (p=0.044). All groups showed substantial reduction in attack frequency already during baseline, which the authors flag as complicating interpretation.

  6. Toxicogenomics applied to cultures of human hepatocytes enabled an identification of novel petasites hybridus extracts for the treatment of migraine with improved hepatobiliary safety.
    Anderson, Meier & Borlak, 2009 (Toxicol Sci) · PMID 19770483 · negative

    States that pharmacovigilance reports for butterbur extracts may be suggestive of rare hepatobiliary toxicity. No signs of hepatocellular toxicity at the estimated therapeutic Cmax of 60 ng/mL, but a 28-day study at approximately 200-fold therapeutic doses produced induced liver transaminases and bilirubin elevations, reproduced in a 6-month chronic study (with recovery by study end). In human hepatocytes above 170-fold therapeutic Cmax, liver toxicity was observed only with petasin-rich extracts (37% petasin), with toxicogenomics implicating impaired bile acid transport. Rat studies did not reliably predict the human hepatotoxicity signal, which the authors present as a reason to rely on human-based in vitro testing.

  7. Evidence-based guideline update: NSAIDs and other complementary treatments for episodic migraine prevention in adults: report of the Quality Standards Subcommittee of the American Academy of Neurology and the American Headache Society.
    Holland, Silberstein, Freitag, Dodick, Argoff & Ashman, 2012 (Neurology) · PMID 22529203 · positive

    Rated Petasites (butterbur) Level A — effective for migraine prevention and should be offered to reduce frequency and severity of attacks — the highest rating given to any complementary agent in the guideline, above feverfew, magnesium and riboflavin at Level B. This rating was based on efficacy data from studies published through May 2009, predates the 2019 hepatotoxicity case series by seven years, and is described in the later guideline-comparison literature as contested.

  8. Nutraceuticals in Migraine: A Summary of Existing Guidelines for Use.
    Rajapakse & Pringsheim, 2016 (Headache) · PMID 26954394 · mixed

    Comparison of nutraceutical recommendations across the American Academy of Neurology/American Headache Society, Canadian Headache Society and European Federation of Neurological Societies guidelines, which the authors state discuss these agents in varying degrees of detail and 'at times conflicting recommendations'. Explicitly addresses the current controversy regarding butterbur and its safety, alongside the weak regulation of nutraceuticals in North America. Establishes that the Level A rating should not be presented as settled or unanimous guidance.

  9. Nutraceuticals and Headache 2024: Riboflavin, Coenzyme Q10, Feverfew, Magnesium, Melatonin, and Butterbur.
    Tepper & Tepper, 2025 (Curr Pain Headache Rep) · PMID 39853578 · mixed

    Summarises butterbur/Petasites preparations as having strong evidence for efficacy alongside concern for hepatotoxicity, and directs readers to the NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health advisory page for up-to-date recommendations on whether to use it at all. Notes that nutraceuticals are unregulated by the FDA, making careful literature review essential before any clinical decision.
