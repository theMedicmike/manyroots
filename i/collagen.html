Minerals & nutrients

Collagen peptides (hydrolysed collagen and undenatured type II collagen)

Early evidence · for chronic pain

For chronic pain

Collagen supplements are a newer entry in the joint-pain aisle, and unlike some others there are real randomised trials behind them, several of which found less knee pain. The catch is that most of those trials were small and many were run by the companies selling the product, and one careful independent study found no difference from a placebo at all. It is a reasonable thing to watch, but the evidence is not yet solid enough to promise you a result.

Safety

Collagen peptides appear well tolerated in the trials conducted so far. In the 180-day undenatured type II collagen trial, safety outcomes did not differ between collagen, glucosamine/chondroitin and placebo groups, and the independent 12-week 10 g/day trial reported no safety concerns. Reported complaints are typically mild and gastrointestinal (fullness, an unpleasant taste, loose stools). Because collagen supplements are protein powders taken in gram quantities, they add meaningful protein load; that is worth discussing with a clinician if you have significantly reduced kidney function. Long-term safety beyond about 6 months has not been well characterised in these trials.

Who should be careful: Avoid sources you are allergic to: collagen supplements are derived from bovine hide or bone, porcine skin, chicken sternum cartilage or marine (fish) sources, so people with beef, pork, chicken or fish allergy must check the source. Marine collagen is unsuitable for people with fish allergy. Bovine and porcine sources may not be acceptable on religious or vegetarian grounds, and no genuine plant-based collagen exists. People with chronic kidney disease should confirm the added protein load with their clinician.

Interactions: No well-documented drug interactions have been established for collagen peptides. Because these are unregulated supplements, combination joint formulas frequently add glucosamine, chondroitin, boswellia or turmeric, so check the full label for ingredients that do interact with medications (for example, anticoagulants) rather than assuming a product contains collagen alone.

What the evidence says

Evidence for collagen peptides in knee osteoarthritis pain is positive on average but preliminary and internally inconsistent. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomised controlled trials (870 participants: 451 collagen, 419 placebo) found significant improvement in pain scores (mean difference -13.63, 95% CI -20.67 to -6.58) and function (-6.46, 95% CI -9.52 to -3.40), but with severe statistical heterogeneity (I-squared 88% and 75% respectively). A 180-day multicentre trial (n=191) of undenatured type II collagen 40 mg/day reported significantly better total WOMAC scores than placebo (p=0.002) and than glucosamine plus chondroitin (p=0.04), with the pain subscale favouring collagen (p=0.0003 vs placebo); two of its three authors were employed by the ingredient manufacturer. Against this, an independent 12-week trial of 10 g/day collagen peptides in 167 physically active middle-aged and older adults with self-reported knee pain found the VAS fell -1.6 with collagen and -1.9 with placebo (P=0.42), with no between-group difference in KOOS (P=0.28) or Lysholm (P=0.76) scores and no effect on inflammatory, cartilage or bone biomarkers. A 30-participant pilot trial found a dGEMRIC cartilage proteoglycan signal at 24 weeks but no significant difference in any clinical outcome. A 69-study supplement review rated collagen hydrolysate as having a large short-term pain effect (effect size above 0.80) at very low evidence quality, with no supplement retaining a clinically important effect at long-term follow-up.

Doses studied: Hydrolysed collagen peptides 10 g/day for 12 weeks; undenatured (native) type II collagen 40 mg/day for 180 days; a further 12-week trial of native type II collagen did not report its daily dose in the published abstract; collagen hydrolysate over 24 to 48 weeks in a pilot imaging trial.

Why this grade: The trial base is real but immature. A 2024 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials pooled only 870 participants in total — an average of about 79 people per trial — and reported severe heterogeneity (I-squared 88% for pain), which means the individual trials disagree substantially with one another. Several of the most positive trials were conducted or authored by the supplement manufacturers. A well-conducted independent trial in 167 people with self-reported knee pain found no difference from placebo, and a pilot imaging trial found a cartilage signal but explicitly no difference in clinical outcomes. A large supplement review found collagen hydrolysate had a large short-term pain effect but classified the underlying evidence quality as very low and found no supplement retained a clinically important effect at long-term follow-up. That combination — small trials, high heterogeneity, sponsor involvement, and an independent null — is exactly what 'early' describes. Note also that essentially all of this evidence is in knee osteoarthritis or general knee pain; there is no meaningful trial evidence for collagen in chronic low back pain or chronic widespread pain.

Sources (6)
  1. Effect of collagen supplementation on knee osteoarthritis: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials.
    Simental-Mendía M, Ortega-Mata D, Acosta-Olivo CA, et al., 2024 (Clinical and Experimental Rheumatology) · PMID 39212129 · positive

    Pooling 11 randomised controlled trials with 870 participants (451 collagen, 419 placebo), oral collagen supplementation improved pain scores (mean difference -13.63, 95% CI -20.67 to -6.58, I-squared 88%) and function (mean difference -6.46, 95% CI -9.52 to -3.40, I-squared 75%) in knee osteoarthritis. The very high heterogeneity and the small average trial size (about 79 participants) limit confidence in the pooled estimate.

  2. Effectiveness of collagen supplementation on pain scores in healthy individuals with self-reported knee pain: a randomized controlled trial.
    Bongers CCWG, Ten Haaf DSM, Catoire M, et al., 2020 (Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism) · PMID 31990581 · null

    In 167 healthy physically active adults (median age 63) with self-reported knee pain, 10 g/day collagen peptides for 12 weeks produced a VAS change of -1.6 versus -1.9 for placebo (P=0.42). KOOS and Lysholm scores improved in both groups with no between-group difference (P=0.28 and P=0.76), and collagen had no effect on inflammatory, cartilage or bone biomarkers (all P>0.05).

  3. Efficacy and tolerability of an undenatured type II collagen supplement in modulating knee osteoarthritis symptoms: a multicenter randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study.
    Lugo JP, Saiyed ZM, Lane NE, 2016 (Nutrition Journal) · PMID 26822714 · positive

    191 volunteers received undenatured type II collagen 40 mg/day, glucosamine 1,500 mg plus chondroitin 1,200 mg, or placebo for 180 days. At day 180 the collagen group had significantly greater reduction in total WOMAC than placebo (p=0.002) and than glucosamine/chondroitin (p=0.04), with improvements in the pain (p=0.0003 vs placebo), stiffness (p=0.004) and physical function (p=0.007) subscales. Safety outcomes did not differ among groups. Two of the three authors were employed by the ingredient manufacturer.

  4. Dietary supplements for treating osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    Liu X, Machado GC, Eyles JP, Ravi V, Hunter DJ, 2018 (British Journal of Sports Medicine) · PMID 29018060 · mixed

    Across 69 trials of 20 supplements in hand, hip or knee osteoarthritis, collagen hydrolysate was one of 7 supplements with a large short-term pain effect (effect size above 0.80), and undenatured type II collagen was one of only 2 with a clinically important medium-term effect. However, no supplement showed a clinically important effect on pain at long-term follow-up, and GRADE quality ranged from very low to high, with the overall analysis rated very low quality.

  5. Change in knee osteoarthritis cartilage detected by delayed gadolinium enhanced magnetic resonance imaging following treatment with collagen hydrolysate: a pilot randomized controlled trial.
    McAlindon TE, Nuite M, Krishnan N, et al., 2011 (Osteoarthritis and Cartilage) · PMID 21251991 · mixed

    In a 30-participant pilot trial of collagen hydrolysate for mild knee osteoarthritis, dGEMRIC T1 relaxation time increased in the medial and lateral tibial regions of interest (median +29 ms and +41 ms) while declining on placebo (median -37 ms and -36 ms), reaching significance at 24 weeks. No significant changes were seen in the other four regions, in any T2 values, or in any of the clinical outcomes, including symptoms and analgesic use.

  6. Efficacy and safety of native type II collagen in modulating knee osteoarthritis symptoms: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.
    Luo C, Su W, Song Y, Srivastava S, 2022 (Journal of Experimental Orthopaedics) · PMID 36562843 · positive

    In 101 participants aged 40-65 with knee osteoarthritis randomised for 12 weeks, native type II collagen changed total WOMAC by -32.47 (SD 19.51) and glucosamine plus chondroitin by -33.74 (SD 24.64) versus -13.84 (SD 17.61) for placebo (p<0.05), with knee pain changing -5.69 (SD 3.66) for collagen and -6.03 (SD 4.72) for glucosamine plus chondroitin versus -2.71 (SD 3.95) for placebo. The trial was small, 12 weeks long, and its first author was employed by the company producing the collagen ingredient.
