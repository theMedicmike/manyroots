Yoga

Moderate evidence · for anxiety & stress Moderate evidence · for chronic pain Moderate evidence · for depression & low mood Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache Moderate evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Yoga combines movement, breathing, and focused attention, and studies show it can lower anxiety and stress for many people, especially those with elevated worry that has not been formally diagnosed. In the biggest head-to-head study it helped people with generalized anxiety more than a stress-education class did, but it did not match CBT, it moved fewer of the symptom measures, and its edge over the control had faded by six months. It is a reasonable, low-risk option to try — particularly if standard therapy is not accessible or appealing — rather than a replacement for it.

Safety

Within the anxiety literature, safety data are thin but reassuring: the yoga-for-anxiety meta-analysis found no increase in injuries among the 3 RCTs that reported safety data, and in the GAD trial completion rates did not differ across arms (yoga 64.5%, CBT 74.4%, stress education 65.1%; chi-square = 2.39, P = .30). Broader harms data are less benign and belong here: a Cochrane review of 21 trials (2,223 participants) in chronic low back pain found low-certainty evidence that yoga increased adverse events compared with no exercise (RR 4.76, 95% CI 2.08-10.89; about 43 per 1,000 with yoga versus 9 per 1,000 without), primarily increased back pain, though the risk was similar to that of other exercise (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.56-1.53). Beginners should start with a qualified instructor and gentle styles rather than advanced or loaded postures.

Who should be careful: People with glaucoma, uncontrolled high blood pressure, cervical spine problems or severe osteoporosis should avoid inversions and load-bearing neck postures; pregnancy calls for prenatal-adapted classes and avoiding hot yoga; recent injury or surgery — and existing back pain, given the Cochrane harms signal — warrants clearance from a clinician or physical therapist first.

Interactions: No drug interactions. As with any relaxation-plus-exercise practice, people on antihypertensives who feel lightheaded moving between postures should transition slowly.

What the evidence says

Evidence for yoga in adult anxiety is positive but clearly second-line. In a 3-arm randomized trial of 226 adults with generalized anxiety disorder, twelve weekly 120-minute Kundalini yoga sessions produced a higher response rate than stress education (54.2% vs 33.0%; OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.12-5.42; number needed to treat 4.59), but yoga was not shown to be noninferior to CBT (response 70.8%; difference 16.6%, P = .42 for noninferiority), leading the authors to conclude CBT should remain first-line. A companion report on seven additional anxiety and depression symptom measures from the same trial sharpens that picture: at post-treatment CBT showed significantly lower symptom severity than control on 6 of the 7 measures while yoga did so on only 3 of 7 (with no significant CBT-versus-yoga differences), and at 6-month follow-up only the CBT group continued to show lower symptoms than control. A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (319 participants) found small short-term effects of yoga on anxiety versus no treatment (SMD = -0.43, 95% CI -0.74 to -0.11) and large effects versus active comparators (SMD = -0.86, 95% CI -1.56 to -0.15), but no effects in patients with anxiety disorders diagnosed by DSM criteria — benefit was confined to elevated anxiety without a formal diagnosis, and the authors called the evidence in anxiety disorders inconclusive. Supportive RCT evidence exists outside anxiety-disorder populations: in 138 analyzed adults with mild-to-moderate Parkinson disease, 8 weeks of mindfulness yoga reduced anxiety significantly more than stretching and resistance training (time-by-group beta = -1.79, 95% CI -2.85 to -0.69 at 8 weeks; -2.05 at 20 weeks), though that is a different population and should not be read as anxiety-disorder evidence.

Doses studied: Trial protocols ran 8-12 weeks: the GAD trial used twelve weekly 120-minute group Kundalini yoga sessions plus 20 minutes of daily home practice; the Parkinson disease trial used weekly 90-minute mindfulness yoga sessions for 8 weeks with outcomes reassessed at 20 weeks.

Why this grade: A rigorous 3-arm RCT in generalized anxiety disorder found yoga beat an active stress-education control, and meta-analysis shows small-to-moderate effects on elevated anxiety symptoms — but the same trial found yoga failed the noninferiority test against CBT, its companion report found yoga separated from control on only 3 of 7 additional symptom measures (versus 6 of 7 for CBT) with no advantage remaining at 6 months, and the meta-analysis found no effect at all in patients with DSM-diagnosed anxiety disorders. Positive, modest and clearly second-line to CBT: moderate.

For chronic pain

Yoga helps some people with long-standing back pain move more easily and hurt a little less - but in careful reviews the average improvement is smaller than what most people would notice as a real change, and yoga does not outperform other kinds of back exercise. It is a good choice if you enjoy it and will keep doing it. Go gently and use a teacher who knows about back pain: yoga does trigger more flare-ups than doing nothing at all.

Safety

Not risk-free, and this is the clearest harm signal among the mind-body practices. The 2022 Cochrane review found low-certainty evidence that yoga increased adverse events versus no exercise at 6-12 months (RR 4.76, 95% CI 2.08 to 10.89; roughly 43 per 1000 with yoga versus 9 per 1000 with no exercise), the events being primarily increased back pain. Against other back-focused exercise the risk was similar (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.56 to 1.53). The 2017 version of the same review, which found the same excess of adverse events over non-exercise controls (risk difference 5%, 95% CI 2% to 8%), concluded that yoga is not associated with serious adverse events. Increased soreness and short flare-ups in the first weeks are common.

Who should be careful: Avoid or substantially modify extreme end-range positions, deep forward folds, headstands and shoulderstands, and forceful spinal twists if you have osteoporosis or vertebral fracture risk, glaucoma or retinal disease, uncontrolled hypertension, recent spine or joint surgery, or a hypermobility disorder such as Ehlers-Danlos (where end-range stretching worsens joint instability). Stop and get assessed for new or worsening leg numbness, weakness, or any bowel or bladder change. Choose a teacher experienced with back pain rather than a general or heated power class.

Interactions: No drug interactions. Sedatives, opioids and medications causing orthostatic hypotension increase fall and faint risk during balance postures and on rising from inversions or forward folds. Anticoagulation is a reason to avoid positions with fall or impact risk.

What the evidence says

The 2022 Cochrane review of yoga for chronic non-specific low back pain included 21 trials with 2223 participants across seven countries, mostly Iyengar, hatha or viniyoga styles. Versus no exercise at three months, low-certainty evidence showed a small improvement in back-specific function (MD -1.69 on the 0- to 24-point Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire, 95% CI -2.73 to -0.65) and moderate-certainty evidence showed improvement in pain (MD -4.53 on a 0 to 100 scale, 95% CI -6.61 to -2.46) - both classed as clinically unimportant against the review's thresholds of 5 and 15 points. More favourably, low-certainty evidence from four trials showed participants were more likely to rate their back pain as improved or resolved (RR 2.33, 95% CI 1.46 to 3.71), and moderate-certainty evidence from six trials showed small improvements in physical and mental quality of life. Versus back-focused exercise, moderate-certainty evidence showed little or no difference in function (MD -0.38, 95% CI -1.33 to 0.62) and very low-certainty evidence showed little or no difference in pain (MD 2.68, 95% CI -2.01 to 7.36). No trials compared yoga with sham yoga, and the authors concluded that because all studies were unblinded and at high risk of performance and detection bias, blinded comparisons would be unlikely to find a clinically important benefit. In a 228-person randomised trial, yoga beat a self-care book for function at 12 weeks (mean difference -2.5, 95% CI -3.7 to -1.3) but was not superior to conventional stretching classes at any time point. A 176-person trial in adults aged 65 and over found neither yoga nor qigong reduced pain more than no intervention. In knee osteoarthritis, a meta-analysis of 8 trials and 756 patients reported reduced pain (SMD -0.92, 95% CI -1.64 to -0.20) but with I-squared of 94% and no significant improvement in activities of daily living or quality of life.

Doses studied: Trials typically ran about 12 weekly classes over 12 weeks with home practice, with the Cochrane review pooling outcomes at three months and adverse events at six to 12 months. Most used gentle, back-adapted Iyengar, hatha or viniyoga styles taught by instructors trained for this population, which is not the same as a general studio class.

Why this grade: The evidence base is substantial rather than preliminary - the 2022 Cochrane review covers 21 trials and 2223 participants - but this sits at the bottom of the moderate band: the pooled improvements versus no exercise were explicitly judged clinically unimportant (function MD -1.69 against a 5-point threshold; pain MD -4.53 against a 15-point threshold), there was little or no difference from other back-focused exercise, a 176-person trial in older adults was null, and yoga carried a roughly fivefold higher adverse-event risk than no exercise. The review authors stated that blinded comparisons would be unlikely to find a clinically important benefit, and no sham-yoga trial exists. It clears 'not_supported' because participant-rated clinical improvement and quality of life did improve, a knee-osteoarthritis meta-analysis found a pain effect, and guidelines list yoga as an option - but only just.

For depression & low mood

Yoga shows up consistently well in research on movement for depression, with effects in the same range as walking, jogging, or strength training, and people tend to stick with it. The catch is that most yoga trials are small and hard to run blind, so the numbers are probably optimistic - and in the best-designed test, where yoga was compared against a health-education class rather than nothing, it did no better on the main measure at ten weeks, though the yoga group did look better over the following six months. Doing it more often seems to matter more than which style you pick.

Safety

The best safety data come from 94 randomised trials covering 8,430 participants: yoga showed no difference from usual care or from exercise in intervention-related, non-serious, or serious adverse events, or in dropouts due to adverse events. Compared with psychological or educational interventions, yoga groups had more intervention-related adverse events (OR 4.21, 95% CI 1.01-17.67) and more non-serious adverse events (OR 7.30, 95% CI 1.91-27.92), with serious events comparable - consistent with the ordinary musculoskeletal strain of any physical practice rather than a distinctive hazard. Reporting in the depression literature specifically is poor: none of the 12 trials in the dedicated yoga-for-depression review reported safety data at all, and the Cochrane breast-cancer review found safety data inadequately reported in most comparisons. Practical risks are joint, back, and neck strain, particularly in unsupervised or advanced postures.

Who should be careful: Get clinical advice before starting if you have glaucoma or retinal detachment risk (inversions raise intraocular pressure), uncontrolled hypertension, osteoporosis or vertebral fragility, recent joint replacement or spinal surgery, or are pregnant - prenatal-specific classes exist for this reason. Hot or high-intensity styles add heat and dehydration risk not represented in the depression trials. Certain breathing practices can provoke dizziness or anxiety in people prone to panic. Trials in depression studied unipolar depressive symptoms and disorders; findings do not establish benefit in bipolar depression, and vigorous or sleep-disrupting practice there warrants clinical supervision alongside mood-stabilising treatment.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Yoga has not been shown to substitute for antidepressant medication or established psychotherapy, and the trials that produced these results generally added yoga to, or compared it against, usual care rather than replacing existing treatment - the largest attention-controlled depression trial studied it specifically as an add-on in people already taking antidepressants. Tell your yoga teacher about medications affecting balance, blood pressure, or dizziness so postural transitions can be adapted.

What the evidence says

Yoga performs consistently well across syntheses of movement-based approaches to depression. In a 218-study network meta-analysis of exercise for major depression, yoga arms (1,047 participants across 33 arms) showed a moderate reduction versus active controls (Hedges' g -0.55, 95% CI -0.73 to -0.36), placing it second only to walking or jogging, and yoga and strength training were the best tolerated of all modalities examined - though only one of the 218 included studies met Cochrane criteria for low risk of bias and confidence in the yoga estimate was rated very low. A frequently cited meta-analysis restricted to adults with a DSM-diagnosed mental disorder pooled 13 trials (632 participants, from 19 reviewed studies with 1,080 participants) and found physically active yoga reduced depressive symptoms more than waitlist, treatment as usual, and attention control (SMD 0.41, reported 95% CI -0.65 to -0.17, p<0.001), with greater reductions associated with higher weekly session frequency (beta=-0.44, p<0.01) - but that pooled sample spanned depression, post-traumatic stress, schizophrenia, anxiety, alcohol dependence, and bipolar disorder, so it is evidence about depressive symptoms across mental illness generally, not about depression specifically. An earlier dedicated review of 12 randomised trials (619 participants) found moderate evidence for short-term effects versus usual care (SMD -0.69, 95% CI -0.99 to -0.39, p<.001) and limited evidence versus relaxation (SMD -0.62, 95% CI -1.03 to -0.22) and aerobic exercise (SMD -0.59, 95% CI -0.99 to -0.18), with effects seen both in diagnosed depressive disorders and in elevated depressive symptoms - but only 3 of the 12 trials were at low risk of bias, long-term effects could not be pooled, and no included trial reported safety data at all. The most rigorous single test is less flattering: a randomised trial of 122 adults with persistent depression despite antidepressant treatment compared weekly hatha yoga against a health-education attention control and found no statistically significant difference in blind-rated depression severity at 10 weeks, its primary outcome (b=-0.82, s.e.=0.88, p=0.36), although yoga participants showed lower depression across the whole intervention and follow-up period (b=-1.38, s.e.=0.57, p=0.02) and a higher 6-month response rate (51% vs 31%, odds ratio 2.31, p=0.04). The signal is not uniform across populations either: Cochrane pooling of 7 trials (496 participants) in women diagnosed with breast cancer found yoga did not reduce depression versus no therapy (SMD -0.13, 95% CI -0.31 to 0.05, low-quality evidence), though in the same review yoga did reduce depression when compared with psychosocial or educational interventions (SMD -2.29, 95% CI -3.97 to -0.61, 4 studies, 226 participants). Nearly all trials study physically active postural yoga rather than meditation-only or breath-only formats, and which component carries the effect remains unidentified.

Doses studied: Trials studied physically active yoga (interventions comprising at least 50% physical activity in the mental-disorder meta-analysis) rather than meditation-only formats. Greater reductions in depressive symptoms were associated with higher frequency of yoga sessions per week (beta=-0.44, p<0.01). The attention-controlled trial in persistent major depression used weekly hatha yoga classes over 10 weeks. Style, duration, and intensity varied widely across trials and the active ingredient has not been isolated.

Why this grade: Multiple meta-analyses converge on a moderate benefit and yoga ranks among the better-performing and best-tolerated modalities in the largest exercise network meta-analysis (g -0.55). But the foundations are weak in ways that keep it at the bottom of this grade: only 3 of 12 trials in the dedicated review were at low risk of bias, only 1 of 218 studies in the network meta-analysis met Cochrane low-risk criteria, long-term effects could not be pooled, the most-cited mental-disorder meta-analysis pooled people with any DSM diagnosis rather than depression specifically, the best attention-controlled trial in persistent major depression missed its primary outcome at 10 weeks, and pooling in women with breast cancer found no effect on depression. Consistent direction, fragile evidence: moderate rather than strong.

For migraine & headache

Yoga added on top of usual migraine care has produced some of the largest reductions in headache days reported for any lifestyle practice. Be careful with that number, though: the studies are small and nobody was blinded, the reviews reach very different conclusions, several of those reviews are re-analysing the same few trials rather than adding new evidence, and one careful review found the apparent benefit came mostly from people with tension headaches rather than migraine. It is safe for most people, roughly as safe as ordinary exercise, and the trials that worked ran it for about three months.

Safety

Pooled safety data across 94 randomized trials and 8430 participants indicate yoga is about as safe as usual care and as safe as conventional exercise, with comparable rates of serious adverse events and of dropouts due to adverse events. It is not risk-free: compared with sitting-still comparators such as health education, yoga produced significantly more intervention-related adverse events (OR 4.21) and more non-serious adverse events (OR 7.30) — mostly musculoskeletal strain of the kind any physical practice can cause. The migraine trials reported no serious harms. Learning from a qualified instructor who knows about your migraines, and building intensity gradually, is the sensible approach.

Who should be careful: Avoid or modify inversions (headstand, shoulder stand) and strong neck-loading postures if you have neck pain, cervical spine disease, glaucoma or uncontrolled hypertension; migraine and neck pain frequently co-occur. Vigorous or heated styles may be poorly tolerated by people whose attacks are triggered by exertion or heat. People with osteoporosis, recent joint replacement, pregnancy, or balance impairment should work with an instructor experienced in adaptation. Do not attempt during an acute attack — exertion typically worsens migraine in progress. A new or changing headache pattern needs a medical diagnosis first.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. In every positive migraine trial yoga was tested as an ADD-ON to standard medical management, not as a replacement for it, and pill counts fell rather than acute medication being stopped.

What the evidence says

All evidence is PREVENTIVE; yoga has not been studied as an acute abortive treatment. The largest trial (CONTAIN, n=160 randomized, 114 completed) added a three-month yoga program to conventional medical management in episodic migraine and found significantly greater reductions than medical therapy alone in headache frequency (delta difference 3.53, 95% CI 2.52-4.54, p<0.0001), headache intensity (1.31, 0.60-2.01), HIT-6 score (8.0, 4.78-11.22), MIDAS score (7.85, 4.98-10.97) and pill count (2.28, 1.06-3.51); it was open-label with blinded endpoint assessment and rated Class III evidence. An earlier 72-patient trial in migraine without aura found yoga superior to self-care on headache intensity, frequency, pain rating indices, anxiety and depression scores and symptomatic medication use (all P<.001). A meta-analysis of 6 RCTs reported benefit across pain intensity (SMD -1.21, 95% CI -2.17 to -0.25), headache frequency (SMD -1.43, -2.23 to -0.64), duration (SMD -1.03), HIT-6 (SMD -2.28) and MIDAS (SMD -0.52), while cautioning about heterogeneity; a second meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (356 patients) reported the IDENTICAL frequency estimate (SMD -1.43, -2.23 to -0.64) plus a significant HIT-6 effect, but found NO significant influence on pain intensity (SMD -1.37, -2.76 to 0.01, P=0.05) or McGill Pain Questionnaire score (SMD -2.09, -6.39 to 2.22, P=0.34) — the shared estimate showing these are re-analyses of the same underlying trials, not independent replications. Against this, a 2019 meta-analysis of yoga for headache disorders found significant overall effects (frequency SMD -1.97, -2.75 to -1.20; intensity SMD -3.43, -6.08 to -0.70, I2=95%) but stated explicitly that these were mainly attributable to tension-type headache patients and that no statistically significant effect was observed for patients with migraine. In network meta-analyses yoga's standing is unsettled: one 28-study analysis found yoga significantly superior to pharmacological treatment alone for both migraine frequency (SMD -1.30) and intensity (SMD -1.40, the only modality to achieve this) at very low quality, while a 2025 multilevel network meta-analysis placed yoga's effect at a much smaller SMD -0.35 (95% CrI -0.63 to -0.06, SUCRA 0.49), behind combined aerobic-plus-resistance training (-1.85) and resistance exercise (-1.45), at very low to low overall certainty.

Doses studied: Trials ran predesigned yoga programs for 3 months, generally with several supervised sessions per week plus home practice. The Neurology trial used a 3-month intervention added to standard medical management; the earlier randomized trial also used 3 months versus self-care.

Why this grade: Several randomized trials and multiple migraine-focused meta-analyses report large reductions in attack frequency, intensity and disability, and one exercise network meta-analysis ranked yoga as the single modality significantly superior to drug treatment alone for both migraine frequency and intensity. The counterweights are serious. A well-conducted 2019 meta-analysis of yoga for headache found its significant overall effects were driven mainly by tension-type headache patients, with NO statistically significant effect observed in migraine specifically. Effect estimates across analyses are wildly inconsistent (SMD -0.35 to -1.97) with heterogeneity up to I2=95%, and the largest network meta-analysis places yoga mid-pack (SMD -0.35, SUCRA 0.49, roughly level with tai chi and far behind combined aerobic-plus-resistance training) at very low certainty. The supporting meta-analyses are also not independent: two of them report an identical headache-frequency estimate (SMD -1.43, 95% CI -2.23 to -0.64), because they re-pool the same small trial set. The flagship trial was open-label with 46 of 160 participants lost and rated Class III. There is a real case for early; it is held at moderate because migraine-specific pooled analyses do exist and mostly point one way, which is more than a preliminary evidence base.

For insomnia

Yoga has been studied for sleep more than most gentle exercise, and across many trials people say they sleep better after taking it up — that finding is fairly consistent. What is much shakier is whether it changes insomnia itself: when researchers used insomnia severity scores or wrist monitors instead of general sleep-quality questionnaires, the benefit often shrank or disappeared, and in one large trial in older adults, talk therapy improved sleep noticeably more than yoga did. It is a low-risk practice with real, if modest, evidence behind it, and the honest summary is that it helps how sleep feels more reliably than it changes how sleep measures.

Safety

Low risk overall but not risk-free. The Wang 2020 sleep meta-analysis reported no serious adverse events across 19 trials (PMID 32357858), and the 500-participant older-adult trial recorded 52 adverse events of which only 2 were possibly intervention-related, with none of its 26 serious adverse events related to the interventions (PMID 33107666). A systematic review of nine epidemiological studies covering 9,129 practitioners found adverse events during a class in 22.7% (95% CI 21.1-24.3%) and serious adverse events in 1.9% (95% CI 1.4-2.4%), overwhelmingly musculoskeletal sprains and strains; falls risk was comparable to non-practitioners (OR 0.90, 95% CI 0.76-1.08) but meniscus injury risk was higher (OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.23-2.41) (Cramer 2017, PMID 28958637). A review of 76 published case reports found headstand, shoulder stand, lotus position and forceful breathing were the practices most often implicated, and recommended beginners avoid them (Cramer 2013, PMID 24146758). The sleep trials used gentle, restorative or integrated styles, not vigorous or heated ones — the evidence above does not transfer to power or hot yoga. As with any sleep intervention, yoga is not a substitute for evaluating suspected obstructive sleep apnea, which can masquerade as insomnia; in one trial in patients with insomnia and mild OSA, surgical treatment of the apnea improved insomnia more than CBT-I alone (PMID 19014072).

Who should be careful: People with glaucoma should avoid inversions and those with compromised bone (osteopenia/osteoporosis) should avoid forceful practices, per the documented case-report pattern (PMID 24146758). Existing knee pathology warrants caution given the elevated meniscus-injury signal (PMID 28958637). People with serious acute or chronic illness, recent surgery, uncontrolled hypertension, or pregnancy should get medical advice and work with a qualified instructor before starting.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. If you take hypnotics or sedatives, note that the largest trial found reduced sleep-medication use in the yoga arm (PMID 23940231) — but any reduction should be made with the prescriber, not unilaterally, because abrupt discontinuation of sleep medication can cause rebound insomnia.

What the evidence says

The most sleep-specific synthesis (Wang 2020, 19 RCTs, 1,832 women) found an overall significant improvement in sleep, SMD -0.327 (95% CI -0.506 to -0.148, P<0.001), and a benefit on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index across 16 RCTs, SMD -0.54 (95% CI -0.89 to -0.19, P=0.003) — but no significant effect on the Insomnia Severity Index across 3 RCTs, SMD -0.13 (95% CI -0.74 to 0.48, P=0.69), none in breast cancer survivors (7 RCTs, SMD -0.15, 95% CI -0.31 to 0.01, P=0.5), and none in peri/postmenopausal women (4 RCTs, SMD -0.31, 95% CI -0.95 to 0.33, P=0.34). A meta-analysis of programmed exercise in middle-aged women (Rubio-Arias 2017, 4 RCTs, 660 women) found that moderate aerobic exercise improved sleep quality (PSQI MD -1.85) while the yoga subgroup specifically did not (MD -0.46, 95% CI -1.79 to 0.88, p=0.50), and insomnia severity was not significantly reduced overall (ISI MD -1.44, 95% CI -3.28 to 0.44, p=0.13). The strongest contrary evidence is a head-to-head: in a randomized preference trial of 500 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and over (Brenes 2020), participants randomized to telephone CBT had a significantly greater reduction in Insomnia Severity Index than those randomized to 20 biweekly yoga classes (adjusted intervention effect 2.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 3.7, p<.01), even though worry and anxiety outcomes did not differ between the two. On the other side, a 2026 network meta-analysis restricted to adults with insomnia (Bu, 22 RCTs, 1,348 participants) ranked yoga favorably, reporting an increase in total sleep time of 110.88 minutes (95% CI 58.66-163.09, moderate certainty), sleep efficiency +15.59% (low certainty), wake after sleep onset -55.91 minutes (low certainty) and sleep onset latency -29.27 minutes (low certainty) — all from sleep diaries, with only 4 of 22 trials at low risk of bias. The largest single trial (Mustian 2013, 410 post-treatment cancer survivors) found the 4-week YOCAS program improved global sleep quality, wake after sleep onset, sleep efficiency and sleep medication use versus standard care (all P<=.05). In diagnosed postmenopausal insomnia (Afonso 2012, 44 completers), yoga reduced insomnia severity significantly more than both a no-treatment control and a passive-stretching control. Against that, the MsFLASH secondary analysis (Buchanan 2017, 186 women aged 40-62) found that 12 weeks of yoga or supervised aerobic exercise produced small changes in actigraph-measured sleep and none differed significantly from usual activity.

Doses studied: Doses verifiable from the cited trials: two 75-minute sessions per week for 4 weeks in the largest cancer-survivor trial (Mustian 2013 — pranayama breathing, 16 gentle Hatha and restorative postures, and meditation); 12 weeks in the MsFLASH actigraphy trial (Buchanan 2017); 4 months in the postmenopausal insomnia trial (Afonso 2012); 12-16 week programs across the trials pooled by Rubio-Arias 2017; and 20 biweekly group classes in the older-adult trial (Brenes 2020). Where a style was specified, trials used gentle, restorative or integrated Hatha yoga including breathing and meditation components — not vigorous or heated styles.

Why this grade: Dozens of RCTs and multiple meta-analyses have measured sleep outcomes, and pooled self-reported sleep quality improves (Wang 2020: SMD -0.327). But the picture splits sharply by outcome and by comparator: insomnia severity (ISI) was NOT significantly improved in the same meta-analysis (SMD -0.13, p=0.69); a separate meta-analysis found the yoga subgroup specifically failed to improve PSQI (MD -0.46, p=0.50) while aerobic exercise did; objective actigraphy in the 186-woman MsFLASH trial showed no differences from usual activity; and in a 500-person randomized preference trial in older adults, CBT beat yoga on the Insomnia Severity Index (adjusted effect 2.4, 95% CI 1.2-3.7, p<.01). The recent insomnia network meta-analysis reports large yoga benefits but rates them low-to-moderate certainty from sleep diaries only, with just 4 of 22 trials at low risk of bias. Positive but modest and inconsistent, beaten by CBT head-to-head, no guideline endorsement — 'moderate', taking the lower of the two grades.
