Slow-paced breathing / breathwork

Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Early evidence · for high blood pressure Early evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For anxiety & stress

Breathwork means deliberately slowing or patterning your breathing — for example long slow exhales for five to ten minutes a day — to calm the body's stress response. Small studies suggest it can take the edge off stress and anxious feelings, and it is free, quick, and safe for most people. But the two largest and best-designed trials both found breathwork worked no better than a look-alike placebo breathing exercise, so the honest answer is that the science here is still young.

Safety

Breathwork was well tolerated in both placebo-controlled trials: the high-ventilation trial (n=200) reported comparable short- and long-term tolerability, credibility and adherence across the breathwork and placebo arms, and the coherent-breathing trial (n=400) reported both arms improving without notable harms. One grounded caution comes from a small randomized pilot (n=20): some people find slow-paced breathing at six breaths per minute uncomfortable and worry they are not getting enough oxygen, and reassurance plus a visible oximeter significantly improved comfort (p < 0.01). Easing in with short sessions helps.

Who should be careful: Intense fast-breathing and long-breath-hold styles are physiologically very different from slow diaphragmatic breathing and have been tested only in healthy young adults; they have not been evaluated in people with cardiovascular disease or epilepsy, in pregnancy, or in panic disorder, so avoid them in those situations and never practice breath holds in or near water. Simple slow-paced breathing has no established contraindication for generally healthy adults.

Interactions: None known with medications. Slow breathing acutely lowers blood pressure and heart rate slightly, which is generally desirable; people on blood-pressure medication who feel lightheaded should practice seated.

What the evidence says

Evidence for breathwork in adult stress and anxiety is preliminary and, at the highest level of rigor, negative. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials found breathwork associated with lower self-reported stress than non-breathwork controls (12 RCTs, 785 adults; g = -0.35, 95% CI -0.55 to -0.14), with similar effects for anxiety (k = 20; g = -0.32) and depressive symptoms (k = 18; g = -0.40), but most studies were at moderate risk of bias and the authors explicitly warned against a miscalibration between hype and evidence. A month-long remote RCT found 5 minutes per day of exhale-emphasized cyclic sighing produced greater improvement in mood and greater reduction in respiratory rate than time-matched mindfulness meditation (both p < 0.05); anxiety was a stated primary endpoint, but no between-group advantage for breathwork on anxiety is reported. Against these, two placebo-controlled trials are null. The largest parallel breathwork RCT randomized 400 adults to about 10 minutes daily of coherent breathing at ~5.5 breaths/min or a matched attention-placebo at 12 breaths/min for 4 weeks and found no significant group-by-time interaction for the primary outcome of subjective stress (F(1,377) = 0.089, p = 0.765), nor for anxiety, depression or wellbeing — both arms improved similarly. The largest blinded trial of fast breathwork randomized 200 healthy young adults to 3 weeks of 20 min/day high-ventilation breathwork with retention or a masked placebo comparator and likewise found no significant group-by-time interaction for stress (F(1,180) = 1.98, p = 0.16) or for anxiety, depression, wellbeing or sleep-related impairment. Taken together, expectation and engagement plausibly drive much of the benefit seen in uncontrolled comparisons.

Doses studied: Brief daily practice in all the cited trials: roughly 5 minutes/day of cyclic sighing for 1 month; ~10 min/day of coherent breathing at ~5.5 breaths/min for 4 weeks (null versus placebo); 20 min/day of high-ventilation breathwork with retention for 3 weeks (null versus placebo).

Why this grade: A meta-analysis of small RCTs shows small-to-medium reductions in stress and anxiety, but the included trials were mostly small and at moderate risk of bias, and the two largest placebo-controlled trials to date — coherent breathing (n=400) and high-ventilation breathwork with retention (n=200) — both found no benefit over a matched breathing placebo on stress, anxiety, depression or wellbeing. The one well-run positive trial separated from meditation on mood and respiratory rate, not on anxiety. Genuinely unsettled, so: early.

Sources (4)
  1. Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials.
    Fincham GW, Strauss C, Montero-Marin J, Cavanagh K (2023), Scientific Reports · PMID 36624160 · positive

    Across 12 RCTs (785 adults), breathwork was associated with lower self-reported stress than non-breathwork controls (g = -0.35, 95% CI -0.55 to -0.14, p = 0.0009), with similar effects on anxiety (g = -0.32, k = 20) and depressive symptoms (g = -0.40, k = 18); most studies were deemed at moderate risk of bias, and the authors urged caution about hype outpacing evidence.

  2. Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal.
    Balban MY, Neri E, Kogon MM, et al. (2023), Cell Reports Medicine · PMID 36630953 · mixed

    In a remote randomized controlled study (NCT05304000) comparing three 5-minute daily breathwork exercises with time-matched mindfulness meditation over 1 month, breathwork — especially exhale-focused cyclic sighing — produced greater improvement in mood (p < 0.05) and greater reduction in respiratory rate (p < 0.05) than meditation; anxiety was a stated primary endpoint, but no between-group advantage for breathwork on anxiety is reported.

  3. Effect of coherent breathing on mental health and wellbeing: a randomised placebo-controlled trial.
    Fincham GW, Strauss C, Cavanagh K (2023), Scientific Reports · PMID 38092805 · null

    In the largest parallel breathwork RCT to date (n=400, NCT05676658), ~10 min/day of coherent breathing at ~5.5 breaths/min for 4 weeks showed no significant group-by-time interaction for the primary outcome of subjective stress (F(1,377) = 0.089, p = 0.765) and no significant differences for anxiety, depression or wellbeing versus a matched breathing placebo at 12 breaths/min; both arms improved similarly from baseline, with equal credibility and expectancy of benefit.

  4. Effects of brief remote high ventilation breathwork with retention on mental health and wellbeing: a randomised placebo-controlled trial.
    Fincham GW, Epel E, Colasanti A, Strauss C, Cavanagh K (2024), Scientific Reports · PMID 39043650 · null

    In the largest blinded RCT of high-ventilation breathwork with retention to date (n=200 healthy young adults, NCT06064474), 3 weeks of 20 min/day fast breathing with long breath holds showed no significant group-by-time interaction for the primary outcome of stress (F(1,180) = 1.98, p = 0.16) and none for anxiety, depression, mental wellbeing or sleep-related impairment versus a masked placebo comparator; credibility, expectancy, adherence and tolerability were comparable between arms.

For high blood pressure

Slowing your breathing deliberately can nudge blood pressure down, but the research here is thin and inconsistent — the pooled review of the commercial breathing devices found no real effect, the earlier positive review lost its result once studies funded by the device makers were set aside, and an independent trial comparing the device against simply listening to music found no difference. The more promising and better-controlled work uses a small handheld device that makes you breathe in against resistance, which lowered top-number pressure by about 9 points in one careful 36-person study and held up on a 24-hour monitor. It is cheap, pleasant and low-risk, but treat it as a possible small extra, not as a replacement for anything.

Safety

Slow breathing practices are among the lowest-risk interventions in this area. The double-blind inspiratory muscle training trial reported the intervention was safe, well tolerated and achieved roughly 95% session adherence, and the independent device trial found no change in quality of life. Possible transient effects include lightheadedness, dizziness or tingling in the hands and face if breathing becomes forced or turns into hyperventilation — slow, unforced breathing avoids this. Techniques involving forceful breath-holding or strong bearing-down raise blood pressure sharply during the manoeuvre and are a different thing from slow breathing.

Who should be careful: Avoid forceful breath-hold or bearing-down styles of breathwork if you have uncontrolled hypertension, known aneurysm, glaucoma or retinal disease, or are pregnant. Resistance-loaded inspiratory muscle training devices should not be used after recent pneumothorax, with unstable asthma or COPD exacerbation, or after recent chest or abdominal surgery without clinical advice. Stop and sit down if you feel faint.

Interactions: No known drug interactions. The important practical caution is substitution: none of these trials tested breathwork as a replacement for antihypertensive medication, and it should never be used to justify stopping prescribed treatment. Because the pooled device evidence is null and an independent trial found the device no better than listening to music, be sceptical of marketing claims made for commercial breathing devices.

What the evidence says

The evidence divides sharply by what was tested. For commercial device-guided slow breathing, a meta-analysis of 8 Resperate trials (494 adults) found a pooled -3.67 mmHg systolic (95% CI -5.99 to -1.39) and -2.51 mmHg diastolic, but a sensitivity analysis excluding the five trials sponsored by or involving the manufacturer showed no overall effect on blood pressure, and the maximum trial duration was nine weeks. A 2022 review of 22 studies found device-guided slow breathing did not significantly reduce systolic BP (-2.13 mmHg, 95% CI -12.71 to 8.44) or diastolic BP (-0.90, -3.97 to 2.11), with high heterogeneity — though these pooled estimates rested on 288 individuals rather than the review's full 17,214-participant total. Independently, a single-blind randomised trial in 30 hypertensive patients comparing the Resperate device against a music control over 9 weeks found no significant difference in blood pressure (-4.2 mmHg systolic, 95% CI -12.4 to 3.9) or quality of life, unchanged after excluding non-compliant patients; the authors concluded the device has no added value. A separate trial found that 8 weeks of slow breathing lowered office but not 24-hour blood pressure, with resting muscle sympathetic nerve activity unchanged. Non-device breathing exercises pooled more favourably across 15 RCTs (-7.06 mmHg systolic, 95% CI -10.20 to -3.92; -3.43 mmHg diastolic), though the authors state plainly that the studies are not free of bias. The strongest single study is a double-blind, sham-controlled randomised trial of high-resistance inspiratory muscle strength training in 36 midlife and older adults with systolic BP of 120 mmHg or above: casual systolic BP fell from 135+/-2 to 126+/-3 mmHg (P<0.01) with about 75% of that reduction still present six weeks after training stopped, 24-hour systolic BP was lower than with sham (P=0.01), and sham training changed nothing. A meta-analysis of 12 inspiratory-muscle-training studies (386 participants) reported -7.93/-3.80 mmHg with trial sequential analysis judged conclusive for both, though its mean arterial pressure estimate crossed zero. No trial in this literature measured cardiovascular events.

Doses studied: Device-guided slow breathing: daily sessions; the longest trial in the pooled device analysis ran 9 weeks, and the independent Altena trial ran 9 weeks with a target breathing rate below 10 breaths/min. Non-device slow breathing and pranayama protocols varied widely across 15 RCTs. High-resistance inspiratory muscle strength training: 30 breaths/day, 6 days/week at 75% of maximal inspiratory pressure for 6 weeks.

Why this grade: Human trials are small, short (typically 6-9 weeks), heterogeneous, and split by what is being tested. The pooled analysis of device-guided slow breathing was null, the earlier positive device meta-analysis lost its effect entirely when manufacturer-sponsored trials were excluded, and an independent single-blind trial of the same device against a music control found nothing. Slow breathing also lowers office but not 24-hour blood pressure in the one trial that measured both. The most rigorous single study — a double-blind sham-controlled trial of inspiratory muscle strength training — is positive and confirmed on ambulatory monitoring, but enrolled only 36 people. Preliminary human evidence only; graded early rather than moderate, and not graded not_supported only because the inspiratory-training arm of this literature is genuinely positive.

  2. Device and nondevice-guided slow breathing to reduce blood pressure in hypertensive patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
    de Freitas Goncalves KS et al., 2022 · PMID 35601033 · null

    22 studies, 17,214 participants included overall. Device-guided slow breathing did not significantly reduce systolic BP (mean difference -2.13 mmHg, 95% CI -12.71 to 8.44; 288 individuals; I-squared 93%) or diastolic BP (-0.90 mmHg, 95% CI -3.97 to 2.11; 288 individuals; I-squared 63%) compared with usual care, and change-score analyses were also null (systolic -2.42, 95% CI -7.24 to 2.40; 443 individuals).   3. Effect of device-guided breathing exercises on blood pressure in patients with hypertension: a randomized controlled trial
    Altena MR et al., 2009 · PMID 19919399 · null

    Independent randomised single-blind trial in 30 non-diabetic hypertensive patients over 9 weeks; the Resperate device versus a control group who listened to music. No significant between-group difference in blood pressure change: -4.2 mmHg systolic (95% CI -12.4 to 3.9) and -2.6 mmHg diastolic (95% CI -8.4 to 3.3).   4. Effects of acute and long-term slow breathing exercise on muscle sympathetic nerve activity in untreated male patients with hypertension
    Hering D et al., 2013 · PMID 23385649 · mixed

    10 hypertensive men undergoing 8 weeks of device-guided slow breathing versus 12 matched hypertensive controls. Long-term slow breathing decreased office systolic BP (P<0.001), diastolic BP (P<0.01) and heart rate (P=0.004), but did not lower 24-hour ambulatory BP. Resting muscle sympathetic nerve activity was unchanged after long-term training (P=0.68), although it fell during acute slow breathing.

  5. Effect of breathing exercises on blood pressure and heart rate: a systematic review and meta-analysis
    Garg P et al., 2023 · PMID 38179185 · positive

    15 RCTs screened from January 2017 to September 2022, covering pranayama, alternate nostril breathing, and deep and slow breathing. Systolic BP fell 7.06 mmHg (95% CI -10.20 to -3.92, P<0.01), diastolic 3.43 mmHg (-4.89 to -1.97) and heart rate 2.41 bpm (-4.53 to -0.30, P=0.03). The authors explicitly note the included studies are not free of bias.

  6. Time-Efficient Inspiratory Muscle Strength Training Lowers Blood Pressure and Improves Endothelial Function, NO Bioavailability, and Oxidative Stress in Midlife/Older Adults With Above-Normal Blood Pressure
    Craighead DH et al., 2021 · PMID 34184544 · positive

    Double-blind, randomised, sham-controlled trial in 36 adults aged 50-79 with systolic BP of 120 mmHg or above (18 high-resistance, 18 sham). Six weeks of training at 75% maximal inspiratory pressure lowered casual systolic BP from 135+/-2 to 126+/-3 mmHg (P<0.01), with about 75% sustained six weeks after training ended; diastolic fell modestly (79+/-2 to 77+/-2 mmHg, P=0.03); 24-hour systolic BP was lower than sham (P=0.01).   7. A hypotensive protocol of inspiratory muscle strength training: systematic review and meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis
    Li W et al., 2023 · PMID 37803506 · positive

    12 articles from five countries, 386 participants, mean quality score 5.83. Authors call for interventions longer than 3 months.

  8. Beyond medications and diet: alternative approaches to lowering blood pressure: a scientific statement from the American Heart Association
    Brook RD et al., 2013 · PMID 23608661 · mixed

    AHA scientific statement classifying device-guided breathing as Class IIA, Level of Evidence B — better supported than acupuncture (Class III, Level of Evidence B) but weaker than aerobic exercise (Class I, Level of Evidence A). This 2013 classification predates the later null pooled analysis and the independent null device trial.

For insomnia

Slow, deliberate breathing before bed is one of the most widely recommended sleep tips there is, and the idea behind it is sound — slowing the breath does measurably calm the body's stress response. But the actual sleep research is thin: mostly small studies in healthy people, hospital patients, and people recruited for other conditions. The two most carefully controlled results are discouraging — people doing slow breathing slept about as well as people handed the same device playing music, and adding it to standard insomnia therapy did not make that therapy work better. It costs nothing and is very unlikely to hurt, but nobody has yet shown convincingly that it helps insomnia itself.

Safety

Safety of slow-paced breathing for sleep is essentially unstudied as a formal endpoint — none of the trials retrieved here reported adverse events for the breathing arms, which reflects absence of reporting rather than demonstrated absence of harm. Gentle slow breathing at around six breaths per minute is physiologically mild and there is no signal of harm in the trial literature. The caution belongs to the other end of the breathwork spectrum: forceful and hyperventilation-style breathing is a different practice with a different risk profile, and 'forceful breathing' was among the practices most commonly implicated in a systematic review of 76 published yoga-related adverse-event case reports (Cramer 2013, PMID 24146758). Rapid or breath-retention practices can cause lightheadedness, tingling, muscle spasm and fainting, and are not the technique studied for sleep. Practically: do breathwork sitting or lying down, never while driving; stop if you feel dizzy. And breathing exercises are not a substitute for evaluating snoring, witnessed breathing pauses or unrefreshing sleep — in one trial in patients with insomnia and mild obstructive sleep apnea, surgical treatment of the apnea improved insomnia more than CBT-I alone (PMID 19014072).

Who should be careful: No established contraindications to gentle slow-paced breathing. Forceful, rapid, or breath-retention styles should be avoided in pregnancy, uncontrolled hypertension, cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, epilepsy or seizure history, glaucoma or raised intraocular pressure, and by anyone prone to fainting — the yoga case-report literature specifically flags forceful breathing as a practice beginners should avoid (PMID 24146758). Do not use breathing practice in place of medical assessment for suspected sleep apnea.

Interactions: No pharmacological interactions. Slow-paced breathing lowers heart rate and is the mechanism behind device-based blood-pressure-lowering programs; if you take antihypertensives and practice regularly, mention it at your next blood-pressure review rather than adjusting medication yourself. Likewise, do not taper hypnotics on your own alongside a breathing practice — abrupt discontinuation can cause rebound insomnia.

What the evidence says

The evidence base for breathing exercises aimed specifically at sleep is small and, where best controlled, null. The best-controlled trial (Savoie 2020) randomized 161 community-dwelling women with overactive bladder syndrome — not diagnosed insomnia — to 12 weeks of a guided slow-paced respiration biofeedback device or an identical-appearing device playing nonrhythmic music; 70% had poor sleep quality at baseline. The breathing group improved by 1.1 points on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, 1.5% on wake after sleep onset and 0.4 nocturnal voids per night, but similar improvements occurred in the music control and there were no significant between-group differences. The authors concluded the results 'do not support clinician recommendation to use this technique over other behavioral relaxation techniques for improving sleep.' In the only randomized trial found in adults with diagnosed insomnia disorder (Lin 2025, n=44), heart-rate-variability biofeedback — paced slow breathing with a device — was added to CBT-I from session four; both arms improved significantly on insomnia severity, pre-sleep arousal and sleep-diary variables, the combined arm uniquely improved subjective total sleep time, and the authors concluded that incorporating HRV biofeedback 'did not significantly enhance the benefits of CBT-I alone in this study.' The only meta-analytic sleep estimate for breathing training that I could locate (Li 2025, 21 RCTs, 881 patients with multiple sclerosis) found no effect on sleep quality: PSQI WMD -0.68 (95% CI -2.04 to 0.68), P=0.33, I2=0%. The most-cited positive trial (Laborde 2019) randomized 64 healthy young adults (mean age 22.1, no insomnia diagnosis) to 15 minutes of app-guided slow-paced breathing each evening for 30 days versus 15 minutes of social media use, and found improved PSQI scores and increased overnight cardiac vagal activity in the breathing arm — but the sample was young and healthy and the comparator was arguably sleep-adverse rather than neutral. A three-arm trial (van der Zwan 2015, n=76) comparing HRV-biofeedback slow breathing against physical activity and mindfulness meditation found improved sleep quality across all three arms with no significant between-intervention differences and no inactive control. A small post-surgical RCT (Kurt 2025, n=66) found both pranayama and deep-breathing groups scored higher on the Richard-Campbell Sleep Scale than a no-intervention control (p<0.05) in the 24 hours after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Breathwork's broader evidence is stronger for stress than for sleep: a meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (Fincham 2023, n=785) found a small-to-moderate reduction in self-reported stress (g=-0.35, 95% CI -0.55 to -0.14) plus similar effects on anxiety and depressive symptoms, but sleep was not among the meta-analyzed outcomes and the authors warned of 'a miscalibration between hype and evidence.' The 2021 AASM guideline conditionally suggests relaxation therapy as a single-component treatment for chronic insomnia — breathing exercises are one ingredient of that broader category, and no guideline recommendation exists for breathwork on its own.

Doses studied: Slow-paced breathing is generally practiced in the resonance-frequency range (roughly six breaths per minute, about five seconds in and five out), though most of these trials' abstracts do not state the exact rate. Verified doses: 15 minutes each evening for 30 days, app-guided, in healthy young adults (Laborde 2019); daily use of a guided slow-paced respiration biofeedback device for 12 weeks (Savoie 2020); five weeks of daily home practice with a heart-rate-variability biofeedback device (van der Zwan 2015); pranayama or deep-breathing exercises across the first 24 hours after laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with session frequency not stated in the abstract (Kurt 2025); and HRV biofeedback introduced at session four of a seven-session weekly CBT-I course (Lin 2025).

Why this grade: Human trials with sleep endpoints do exist, so this is not 'traditional' — but they are few, small, and mostly in populations without insomnia: 64 healthy young adults, 66 post-surgical patients, 76 stressed adults with no inactive control, and 161 women recruited for overactive bladder. The two best-controlled results are null. In that bladder trial, a slow-paced respiration device was compared against an identical device playing music; both arms improved and there was no significant between-group difference, with the authors explicitly declining to recommend the technique over other relaxation methods. In the only randomized trial I found in adults with diagnosed insomnia disorder, adding heart-rate-variability biofeedback (paced slow breathing) to CBT-I did not significantly enhance CBT-I alone. A meta-analysis of breathing training that reported a sleep outcome (in multiple sclerosis) found no PSQI effect. Breathing-based relaxation therapy is conditionally suggested by the AASM, but as part of the broader relaxation-therapy category, not as breathwork per se. Small, few, preliminary, and null where best controlled = 'early'.
