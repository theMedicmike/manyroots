Catnip (Nepeta cataria)

Traditional use · for insomnia

For insomnia

People have brewed catnip as a bedtime tea for a very long time, and nobody has properly tested whether it helps humans sleep. We searched the medical literature for human trials of catnip for insomnia and found none — in fact, we found no human trial of catnip for anything. The only studies are in mice, and they disagree with each other: one found catnip made mice sleep longer, another found it acted more like a stimulant and made them sleep less.

Safety

Human safety data for catnip are essentially absent — no clinical trial has characterised adverse events, dosing ceilings, or tolerability in people, because no clinical trial of catnip exists. What exists is rodent toxicology: Harney et al. reported oral LD50 values in mice of 1300 mg/kg for catnip oil, 1550 mg/kg for a nepetalactone-enriched fraction, and 1050 mg/kg for nepetalic acid. A more concerning signal comes from Massoco et al., where acute catnip exposure increased susceptibility to seizures in mice and long-term exposure increased susceptibility to seizures induced by picrotoxin and strychnine, alongside tolerance to several behavioural effects. Because catnip acted as a stimulant rather than a sedative in that model — increasing locomotion and shortening pentobarbital sleeping time — a paradoxical effect on sleep cannot be ruled out. Safety in pregnancy, in children, and with chronic use is entirely unstudied.

Who should be careful: None formally established, because no adequate human safety study has been done. Given the rodent finding of increased chemically-induced seizure susceptibility, people with a seizure disorder should avoid catnip supplements pending human data. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and paediatric use are unstudied and should be discussed with a clinician. Anyone using catnip for sleep should be aware that the animal data raise the possibility of a stimulant rather than sedative effect.

Interactions: No human drug-interaction studies exist. Animal work shows catnip constituents alter barbiturate sleeping time in both directions (increased with catnip oil in one study, decreased with dietary catnip in another), suggesting possible interference with sedative-hypnotic drugs and with hepatic drug metabolism. Caution is reasonable with any prescription sedative, alcohol, or anticonvulsant, but this is inference from rodent data, not established interaction evidence in people.

What the evidence says

No human trial of Nepeta cataria for insomnia, sleep quality, or any other indication was identified in PubMed; a search limited to trial publication types across the whole genus returns only two studies, both of Nepeta menthoides for major depression. The animal evidence is limited and internally contradictory. Harney et al. (1978) reported that catnip oil at 500 mg/kg and nepetalic acid at 62.5 mg/kg significantly increased hexobarbital-induced sleeping time in mice, consistent with sedation, and established LD50 values of 1300 mg/kg for catnip oil, 1550 mg/kg for a nepetalactone-enriched fraction, and 1050 mg/kg for nepetalic acid. Massoco et al. (1995) reached the opposite conclusion: catnip fed as 10% of the diet for 2 h/day increased rearing and locomotion in an open field, increased stereotyped behaviour, and decreased sleeping time after sodium pentobarbital, with the authors proposing an amphetamine-like mechanism. A 2020 ethnopharmacological review of the genus documents extensive traditional use across the Himalayan region, Pakistan, Nepal, China, Turkey, and Iran for colds, coughs, asthma, respiratory and stomach disorders, and concludes that comprehensive clinical trials are still necessary. The two human trials that exist in this genus used a different species for a different condition and provide no evidence for catnip and none for sleep.

Doses studied: No human dose has been established for sleep, and no clinical trial of Nepeta cataria for insomnia exists. Doses in the cited animal work were catnip oil 500-750 mg/kg and nepetalic acid 62.5-250 mg/kg in mice and rats, or catnip supplied as 10% of the diet for 2 h/day — figures that do not translate to a human supplement or tea dose.

Why this grade: Verified in this session by independent search: restricting PubMed to Clinical Trial and Randomized Controlled Trial publication types across the entire genus Nepeta returns exactly two records, and both are Nepeta menthoides studied for major depression — a different species and a different condition. There is no human trial of Nepeta cataria for insomnia, sleep quality, or any sleep endpoint, and no human trial of catnip for any indication at all. The catnip-specific literature is rodent pharmacology from 1978 and 1995 that points in opposite directions on sedation. Long-documented traditional use with no adequate human trials for this condition is the definition of 'traditional'; grading this 'early' would misrepresent an empty human literature as a preliminary one.

Sources (5)
  1. Behavioral and toxicological studies of cyclopentanoid monoterpenes from Nepeta cataria.
    Harney JW, Barofsky IM, Leary JD, 1978 · PMID 672466 · positive

    In mice, catnip oil (500 mg/kg) and nepetalic acid (62.5 mg/kg) significantly increased hexobarbital sleeping time. LD50 values were 1300 mg/kg (catnip oil), 1550 mg/kg (nepetalactone-enriched fraction), and 1050 mg/kg (nepetalic acid). Rats on a Sidman avoidance schedule showed decreased performance after catnip oil 500-750 mg/kg and developed behavioural tolerance after daily 750 mg/kg dosing. Animal data only; no human sleep endpoint.

  2. Behavioral effects of acute and long-term administration of catnip (Nepeta cataria) in mice.
    Massoco CO, Silva MR, Gorniak SL, Spinosa MS, Bernardi MM, 1995 · PMID 8588288 · negative

    Catnip fed as 10% of the diet for 2 h/day increased rearing and locomotion frequencies, and acute exposure increased stereotyped behaviour, increased susceptibility to seizures, and DECREASED sleeping time after sodium pentobarbital in mice — the opposite of a sedative effect. Authors proposed an amphetamine-like mechanism for the acute effects. Long-term exposure induced tolerance to stereotypic behaviour, catalepsy and sleeping time, and increased susceptibility to seizures induced specifically by picrotoxin and strychnine.

  3. The genus Nepeta: Traditional uses, phytochemicals and pharmacological properties.
    Sharma A, Cooper R, Bhardwaj G, Cannoo DS, 2020 · PMID 33307050 · null

    Ethnopharmacological review documenting extensive traditional use of Nepeta species across India, Pakistan, Nepal, China, Turkey, and Iran for chicken pox, tuberculosis, malaria, pneumonia, influenza, measles, stomach disorders, respiratory disorders, asthma, colds, and coughs. Catalogues nepetalactones and other iridoids as principal constituents. Concludes that more comprehensive and inclusive clinical trials are necessary; the review reports no controlled human sleep trial for any Nepeta species and does not list insomnia among the documented traditional indications it catalogues.

  4. Nepeta menthoides Boiss. & Buhse freeze-dried aqueous extract versus sertraline in the treatment of major depression: A double blind randomized controlled trial.
    Kolouri S, Firoozabadi A, Salehi A, Zarshenas MM, Dastgheib SA, Heydari M, Rezaeizadeh H, 2016 · PMID 27261997 · positive

    Included to define the boundary of the human evidence: this 72-patient 4-week RCT used a DIFFERENT species (N. menthoides), studied major depression rather than sleep, and reported significantly greater Beck Depression Inventory-II score reduction than sertraline (p<=0.001) with a lower recurrence rate at 2-week follow-up. It provides no evidence for catnip (N. cataria) and no evidence for any sleep endpoint.

  5. Effectiveness of Cuscuta planiflora Ten. and Nepeta menthoides Boiss. & Buhse in major depression: a triple-blind randomized controlled trial study.
    Firoozabadi A, Zarshenas MM, Salehi A, Jahanbin S, Mohagheghzadeh A, 2014 · PMID 25416496 · positive

    The second and final clinical trial in the entire Nepeta genus indexed in PubMed, included here to show the complete human evidence base. 43 patients with major depression received N. menthoides 400 mg or Cuscuta planiflora 500 mg capsules plus conventional drugs, or conventional drugs alone, for 8 weeks; Beck and Hamilton depression scores fell more in the herb groups than controls (P<.01). Again a different species, a different condition, and no sleep outcome — confirming that no human trial of Nepeta cataria for sleep exists.
