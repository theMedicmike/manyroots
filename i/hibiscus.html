Herbs

Hibiscus tea (Hibiscus sabdariffa)

Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure

For high blood pressure

Drinking hibiscus tea seems to bring blood pressure down modestly in people whose pressure is mildly raised, on the order of a few points. The catch is that the studies behind that finding are mostly small and not especially rigorous: when reviewers applied strict quality standards they found only a single qualifying trial, which showed no benefit at all, and the one study that compared hibiscus head-to-head against a standard blood pressure drug found the drug clearly worked better. It is a pleasant, low-risk drink that may help a little, but it is not a replacement for medication that has been prescribed to you.

Safety

Hibiscus tea is a widely consumed beverage with a good tolerability record in trials, and pooled analysis found no increase in adverse event risk. One umbrella review did detect a minor, clinically insignificant rise in aspartate aminotransferase (a liver enzyme), which is worth noting for anyone taking concentrated extracts long term or with existing liver disease; those authors specifically cautioned that long-term and therapeutic dosing safety requires careful monitoring. Brewed tea at ordinary dietary amounts is a lower-exposure route than concentrated capsule extracts. Hibiscus has a measurable natriuretic (salt- and water-excreting) effect that ordinary antihypertensive drugs do not, so people on diuretics should be alert to dehydration or light-headedness. Because hibiscus can lower blood pressure additively, anyone already on antihypertensive drugs should monitor for symptoms of pressure dropping too low, such as dizziness on standing.

Who should be careful: Avoid concentrated hibiscus extracts in pregnancy, since traditional use includes emmenagogue and uterine-stimulant applications and adequate human safety data in pregnancy are lacking. Use caution with existing liver disease given the observed aspartate aminotransferase signal. People with already low blood pressure should be cautious. Not a substitute for prescribed antihypertensive therapy; in the one trial that compared them directly, hibiscus lowered systolic pressure significantly less than ramipril and got fewer patients to target.

Interactions: Additive effect with antihypertensive medications, which may cause pressure to fall further than intended, and a possible additive effect with diuretics given hibiscus's own natriuretic action. Hibiscus does appear to alter drug handling: in a human two-period crossover study in six volunteers, hibiscus beverage changed caffeine exposure enough that the point estimates fell outside the standard 0.8-1.25 bioequivalence limits, and the aqueous extract weakly inhibited eight cytochrome P450 isozymes in vitro, most notably CYP1A2. Animal work found hibiscus extract increased and prolonged plasma hydrochlorothiazide concentrations and reduced its clearance. Separate dosing from prescription drugs by a couple of hours and check with a pharmacist if you take a narrow-therapeutic-index medicine.

What the evidence says

Meta-analyses of the available RCTs are broadly positive but rest on a thin methodological base. A 13-RCT meta-analysis (1205 participants) found significant reductions versus placebo of 6.67 mmHg systolic (P=0.004) and 4.35 mmHg diastolic (P=0.02), with the effect significant in patients with hypertension alone but NOT in those with hypertension associated with metabolic syndrome, and NOT superior to antihypertensive drugs when compared head to head. An earlier 5-RCT meta-analysis (390 participants randomised) reported systolic -7.58 mmHg (95% CI -9.69 to -5.46) and diastolic -3.53 mmHg (95% CI -5.16 to -1.89), with effects inversely associated with baseline blood pressure. An umbrella review of 26 RCTs (1797 participants) found dose-dependent reductions with moderate credibility for a therapeutic reduction above 10 mmHg, particularly in people over 50 and trials longer than four weeks. Against this sits the negative evidence. The 2021 Cochrane review identified only one eligible parallel-group RCT, in 60 participants with type 2 diabetes, in which the systolic mean difference favoured placebo at +1.65 mmHg (95% CI -7.89 to 11.19) and diastolic at +4.60 mmHg (95% CI -1.38 to 10.58), both computed on 52 analysed participants and both rated very low certainty, in a trial at high risk of attrition, reporting and baseline-imbalance bias; the reviewers concluded evidence is insufficient to determine effectiveness. A 125-patient randomised trial comparing hibiscus calyx powder against ramipril found systolic pressure fell significantly less with hibiscus (-11.2 +/- 3.3 vs -16.7 +/- 8.4 mmHg, P=0.001), with 21% versus 39% reaching target blood pressure. A well-conducted 65-participant RCT in pre- and mildly hypertensive adults found systolic -7.2 +/- 11.4 mmHg versus -1.3 +/- 10.0 mmHg for placebo (P=0.030), but the diastolic change did not differ significantly from placebo (P=0.160). An acute crossover study in 20 overweight adults found no change in blood pressure at all. All outcomes are blood pressure measurements; no cardiovascular event data exist.

Doses studied: Verified regimens include three 240 mL servings per day of brewed hibiscus tea (tisane) for 6 weeks in pre- and mildly hypertensive adults; hibiscus calyx powder capsules at 320 mg twice daily for 4 weeks in the ramipril comparison trial; and Roselle extract capsules at a total dose of 5600 mg over 8 weeks in the single trial included by Cochrane. Umbrella review analysis reported dose-dependent blood pressure reduction, with effects more evident in trials lasting longer than four weeks and in people over 50.

Why this grade: Several meta-analyses of RCTs report reasonably consistent systolic reductions in the 4-8 mmHg range in people with mild-to-moderate hypertension, and an umbrella review assigned moderate credibility to a reduction above 10 mmHg in older participants and longer trials. It is held at moderate rather than strong for three decisive reasons: the 2021 Cochrane review applying strict criteria found only ONE eligible RCT, which showed no benefit (systolic mean difference +1.65 mmHg) and was rated very low certainty; a direct head-to-head trial found hibiscus significantly INFERIOR to ramipril on systolic reduction and on the proportion of patients reaching target blood pressure; and no trial has measured cardiovascular events. The gap between the pooled estimates and the Cochrane result indicates most contributing trials are small and methodologically limited.

Sources (7)
  1. Roselle for hypertension in adults.
    Systematic reviewPattanittum P, Ngamjarus C, Buttramee F, Somboonporn C (Cochrane), 2021 · PMID 34837382 · null

    Applying strict inclusion criteria, only one RCT (60 participants with type 2 diabetes) qualified. Roselle extract capsules (5600 mg total) versus placebo at eight weeks gave a systolic mean difference of +1.65 mmHg (95% CI -7.89 to 11.19) and diastolic of +4.60 mmHg (95% CI -1.38 to 10.58), both based on 52 analysed participants and both very low-certainty evidence. The trial was at low risk of selection, performance and detection bias but at HIGH risk of attrition bias, reporting bias and other bias (baseline imbalance). Withdrawals due to adverse effects, pulse pressure and heart rate were not reported. Reviewers concluded the evidence is currently insufficient to determine the effectiveness of Roselle for controlling or lowering blood pressure.

  2. Clinical efficacy of African traditional medicines in hypertension: A randomized controlled trial with Combretum micranthum and Hibiscus sabdariffa.
    Randomised trialSeck SM, Doupa D, Dia DG, Diop EA, Ardiet DL, Nogueira RC, Graz B, Diouf B, 2018 · PMID 29311704 · mixed

    Randomised trial in 125 adults with uncomplicated hypertension (>140/90 mmHg) comparing Hibiscus sabdariffa calyx powder 320 mg twice daily, Combretum micranthum, and ramipril 5 mg daily over four weeks. Pressure fell in all three groups, but the mean systolic decrease was significantly SMALLER with hibiscus than with ramipril (-11.2 +/- 3.3 vs -16.7 +/- 8.4 mmHg, P=0.001); the diastolic difference was not statistically significant (-6.0 +/- 4.7 vs -6.7 +/- 3.6 mmHg, P=0.271). By four weeks only 21% (95% CI 11.7-35.9) of the hibiscus group had normalised blood pressure versus 39% (95% CI 25.7-54.3) on ramipril. A significant natriuretic effect was seen with hibiscus but not ramipril.

  3. Efficacy of Hibiscus sabdariffa on Reducing Blood Pressure in Patients With Mild-to-Moderate Hypertension: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Published Randomized Controlled Trials.
    Meta-analysisAbdelmonem M et al., 2022 · PMID 34694241 · mixed

    Meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1205 participants) found Hibiscus sabdariffa significantly reduced systolic blood pressure by 6.67 mmHg (P=0.004) and diastolic by 4.35 mmHg (P=0.02) versus placebo. Subgroup analysis showed changes were significant in patients with hypertension alone but NOT significant in patients with hypertension associated with metabolic syndrome, and when compared against active controls (antihypertensive drugs or other herbals) the difference was not statistically significant. Authors called for further RCTs to determine long-term efficacy and identify who benefits.

  4. Effect of sour tea (Hibiscus sabdariffa L.) on arterial hypertension: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.
    Meta-analysisSerban C, Sahebkar A, Ursoniu S, Andrica F, Banach M, 2015 · PMID 25875025 · positive

    Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs (7 treatment arms, 390 participants randomised, 225 to supplementation and 165 to control) found significant lowering of systolic blood pressure by 7.58 mmHg (95% CI -9.69 to -5.46) and diastolic by 3.53 mmHg (95% CI -5.16 to -1.89). Effects were inversely associated with baseline blood pressure values and robust in sensitivity analyses; authors noted further well-designed trials are necessary to validate the results.

  5. Hibiscus sabdariffa L. tea (tisane) lowers blood pressure in prehypertensive and mildly hypertensive adults.
    Randomised trialMcKay DL, Chen CY, Saltzman E, Blumberg JB, 2010 · PMID 20018807 · mixed

    Randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 65 pre- and mildly hypertensive adults aged 30-70 not on BP medication, using three 240 mL servings of hibiscus tea daily for 6 weeks. Systolic pressure fell 7.2 +/- 11.4 mmHg versus 1.3 +/- 10.0 mmHg for placebo (P=0.030). Diastolic pressure was lower but did NOT differ significantly from placebo (-3.1 +/- 7.0 vs -0.5 +/- 7.5 mmHg, P=0.160), and the mean arterial pressure change was of borderline significance (-4.5 +/- 7.7 vs -0.8 +/- 7.4 mmHg, P=0.054). Participants with higher baseline systolic pressure responded more.

  6. Efficacy and safety of Hibiscus sabdariffa in cardiometabolic health: An overview of reviews and updated dose-response meta-analysis.
    Meta-analysisNorouzzadeh M, Hasan Rashedi M, Azizi MH, Teymoori F, Maghsoomi Z, Shidfar F, 2025 · PMID 39870328 · positive

    Umbrella review and dose-response meta-analysis of data from 26 RCTs (1797 participants) found hibiscus dose-dependently reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressure versus placebo and other teas, with moderate credibility for a therapeutic reduction above 10 mmHg, especially in individuals over 50 years, in trials lasting over four weeks, and in those at low risk of bias. No significant difference was found between hibiscus and antihypertensive drugs, which in small trials is absence of evidence of a difference rather than evidence of equivalence. A minor, clinically insignificant increase in aspartate aminotransferase was observed without an increase in adverse event risk, and the authors cautioned that long-term and therapeutic dosing safety requires careful monitoring.

  7. Acute effects of Hibiscus sabdariffa on blood pressure and cognitive function.
    Randomised trialEllis L, Aragon-Rojas S, Dye L, Boesch C, 2025 · PMID 40009760 · null

    Randomised placebo-controlled crossover pilot in 20 overweight adults (mean age 35, BMI 30) given a hibiscus-containing beverage or inert placebo alongside a high-carbohydrate breakfast. The hibiscus drink significantly reduced the postprandial glucose response at 30 and 45 minutes and modestly affected memory measures, but produced NO change in blood pressure over the two-hour postprandial period. Note the PubMed record renders the title with the italicised species name stripped.
