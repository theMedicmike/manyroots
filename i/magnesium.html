Magnesium

Moderate evidence · for blood sugar & type 2 diabetes Moderate evidence · for high blood pressure Moderate evidence · for migraine & headache Early evidence · for anxiety & stress Early evidence · for depression & low mood Early evidence · for insomnia

A grade belongs to a connection, not to a substance — the evidence can be decent for one condition and absent for another.

For blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Magnesium is a mineral that helps insulin do its job, and people with diabetes are often low in it. In trials it nudges fasting blood sugar down a little, and it helps most clearly in people who start out magnesium-deficient, especially those with prediabetes; the effect on the three-month average (HbA1c) is small and often absent. It is generally safe if your kidneys work normally, but no study has shown that taking it prevents diabetes or any of its complications.

Safety

Magnesium is not a substitute for prescribed glucose-lowering medication. Nothing on this page is a reason to stop, reduce or delay a prescribed drug; any change belongs to the prescriber. In RCTs at 250-500 mg/day, adverse reactions did not differ from placebo (PMID 42534812) and a prediabetes meta-analysis described safety and tolerability as excellent (PMID 41641401); loose stools are the expected dose-limiting effect given magnesium's laxative action. Benefit in trials was largest when baseline serum magnesium was low, so a blood test is a sensible way to decide whether supplementation is likely to help.

Who should be careful: Reduced kidney function is the key contraindication: clinically serious hypermagnesaemia is almost exclusively seen with substantially decreased kidney function plus high magnesium intake from supplements, cathartics or antacids (PMID 30220246); a case series described symptomatic and one fatal hypermagnesaemia in elderly patients with renal dysfunction taking magnesium oxide (PMID 30136128), and magnesium oxide raised serum magnesium highest at eGFR 15-29, prompting a caution below eGFR 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 (PMID 26081346). The 12-month diabetes RCT excluded creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min (PMID 42534812). A fatal case in a child on unsupervised high-dose magnesium oxide illustrates the danger of megadoses (PMID 10654978).

Interactions: Magnesium oxide is itself prescribed as a laxative (PMID 30136128), so stacking a supplement with magnesium-containing antacids or laxatives raises the total load. Timing matters with fluoroquinolone antibiotics: in a human crossover study, a magnesium and aluminium antacid taken 5-10 minutes, 2 hours or 4 hours before ciprofloxacin reduced its relative bioavailability to 15.1%, 23.2% and 70% of control respectively, while dosing 6 hours before or 2 hours after had no effect (PMID 2598571), so separate magnesium from a fluoroquinolone by several hours. Fluoroquinolones are not the only drug magnesium binds this way. Tetracycline antibiotics: in a randomised crossover study in 10 healthy volunteers, an aluminium-magnesium hydroxide antacid cut the absorption of doxycycline 200 mg from an AUC of 38.6 to 6.0 mg.h/L, which the authors call subtherapeutic levels (PMID 2610502); minocycline belongs to the same tetracycline class, though the human absorption data here are for doxycycline. Levothyroxine: in a randomised crossover trial in 15 healthy adults, magnesium aspartate taken with 1 mg levothyroxine reduced thyroxine AUC by 12% (geometric mean ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.81-0.95, P=0.002), and magnesium citrate by a non-significant 7%, leading the authors to advise taking levothyroxine separated from magnesium-containing products (PMID 41221788). Oral bisphosphonates for osteoporosis: their labels warn that metal cations including magnesium reduce absorption, although the measured magnesium effect is much smaller than calcium's - in rats, magnesium chloride solution lowered risedronate absorption by 12% versus 54% for calcium chloride at the same water hardness (PMID 26934925). The practical rule is the same for all of them: do not swallow magnesium at the same time as a fluoroquinolone, a tetracycline, levothyroxine or an oral bisphosphonate. Space them apart by several hours, and ask your pharmacist or prescriber to confirm the right gap against your own medication list - the necessary separation differs by drug, and of these only the ciprofloxacin timing has actually been measured. With glucose-lowering drugs the trial effect on glucose was small, so hypoglycaemia is not an expected problem.

What the evidence says

In established type 2 diabetes, meta-analyses of short RCTs agree on a modest fall in fasting glucose but disagree on HbA1c: Song 2006 (9 double-blind RCTs, n=370, median 360 mg/day, 4-16 weeks) found fasting glucose -0.56 mmol/L but HbA1c -0.31% (95% CI -0.81 to 0.19, not significant); Asbaghi 2022 (18 trials) modelled HbA1c -0.73% at 500 mg/day and -0.48% at 24 weeks; Maqrashi 2025 (23 RCTs, n=1,345) found fasting glucose WMD -0.58 (-0.87 to -0.28, units not stated in the source) but HbA1c only -0.16% (-0.32 to 0.00) (PMID 40641714); and Simental-Mendia 2016 found HOMA-IR improved (-0.67) but glucose and HbA1c did not overall (PMID 27329332). The largest and longest single trial (n=247, magnesium oxide 302 mg elemental/day, 12 months) missed its primary endpoint (HbA1c -0.30% vs -0.05%, P=0.145) with a larger but still non-significant effect in those with low baseline magnesium (-0.60% vs +0.25%, P=0.074), and a 3-month trial in 50 insulin-requiring patients was flatly null (HbA1c 8.9% vs 9.1%). In prediabetes the signal depends on magnesium status: 382 mg/day for 4 months in 116 hypomagnesaemic adults lowered fasting glucose to 86.9 vs 98.3 mg/dL and improved glucose status in 50.8% vs 7.0%, and 360 mg/day in 71 older hypomagnesaemic adults cut fasting glucose by 0.50 mmol/L without changing HbA1c, insulin or HOMA-IR (PMID 41756632), whereas 250 mg/day in unselected prediabetes raised HDL but did not change HOMA-IR (PMID 36307427), and a 2026 meta-analysis of 5 prediabetes RCTs (n=384) found 2-h OGTT glucose -0.99 mmol/L and HOMA-IR -1.10 but a non-significant fasting glucose change of -4.13 mg/dL (PMID 41641401). No RCT has tested progression to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney outcomes or death; the link to lower diabetes incidence (RR 0.81 per 100 mg/day across more than 1 million cohort participants) is observational and concerns magnesium from food, not supplements.

Doses studied: 250-500 mg elemental magnesium per day (most commonly about 360 mg as magnesium chloride, oxide or other salts), for 4 weeks to 12 months. The positive prediabetes trials used 360-382 mg/day for 4 months in people with documented low serum magnesium.

Why this grade: Multiple RCTs and several meta-analyses exist and split cleanly by endpoint: fasting glucose falls consistently and modestly (-0.56 mmol/L in Song 2006, -0.58 in Maqrashi 2025, -0.50 in the 2026 prediabetes trial), while HbA1c does not move reliably (non-significant in Song, Simental-Mendia and Maqrashi, and the largest and longest single RCT missed its primary HbA1c endpoint at 12 months). Benefit concentrates in people with documented low serum magnesium. Every endpoint on this page is a surrogate: no trial has tested progression to diabetes, cardiovascular events, kidney failure or death, and there is no guideline first-line status, so 'strong' is unavailable regardless of the numbers. A body of 18-23 RCTs is also too large to call 'early'. That leaves moderate.

For high blood pressure

Magnesium pills nudge blood pressure down by roughly 2 mmHg on average - real in the statistics, but small compared with cutting salt, losing weight or taking medication. The trial results disagree with each other more than most people realise, and a careful Cochrane review concluded the effect might largely be an artefact of weaker studies. What these trials measured was the cuff reading; none of them looked at whether magnesium prevents heart attacks or strokes.

Safety

Oral magnesium at the doses used in these blood-pressure trials was generally tolerated, and serum magnesium rose only slightly (0.05 mmol/L in the largest meta-analysis), indicating limited systemic loading in people with normal kidney function. The practical limit for most people is the laxative effect of magnesium salts - loose stools, cramping and abdominal discomfort - which is dose-related and more pronounced with oxide and citrate forms. Because the pooled blood-pressure effect is around 2 mmHg and disputed, and because none of these trials measured cardiovascular events, magnesium should not be substituted for treatment that has been shown to prevent them.

Who should be careful: Avoid supplemental magnesium without medical supervision if you have chronic kidney disease or reduced kidney function, since magnesium is cleared renally and can accumulate to dangerous levels. Also seek advice if you have heart block or myasthenia gravis.

Interactions: Magnesium binds several oral drugs in the gut and reduces their absorption - tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates in particular - so separate doses by several hours. Combining magnesium with other magnesium-containing products (many antacids and laxatives) can push intake higher than intended. Effects may add to those of blood pressure medication.

What the evidence says

Evidence for magnesium in hypertension rests entirely on blood pressure as a surrogate; the trials pooled in these reviews report no cardiovascular events. The largest synthesis, 34 randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trials in 2028 normotensive and hypertensive adults, found that a median dose of 368 mg/day for a median of 3 months reduced systolic pressure by 2.00 mmHg (95% CI 0.43-3.58) and diastolic by 1.78 mmHg (95% CI 0.73-2.82), alongside a 0.05 mmol/L rise in serum magnesium. The Cochrane review restricted to adults with raised blood pressure and at least 8 weeks of treatment (12 RCTs, 545 participants) found no significant systolic reduction (-1.3 mmHg, 95% CI -4.0 to 1.5, I2=67%) and a 2.2 mmHg diastolic reduction, concluding the evidence for a causal association was weak and probably attributable to bias, and calling for better trials assessing blood pressure and cardiovascular outcomes. An earlier meta-analysis of 20 trials (1220 participants, 10-40 mmol/day) found essentially no overall effect (-0.6 mmHg systolic, 95% CI -2.2 to 1.0; -0.8 mmHg diastolic, 95% CI -1.9 to 0.4) despite an apparent dose gradient. In the Trials of Hypertension Prevention Phase 1, magnesium supplementation produced no significant blood pressure change in 2182 adults with high-normal pressure at 6 or 18 months. Larger effects have been reported in specific populations - 5.78 mmHg systolic and 2.50 mmHg diastolic across 7 RCTs in type 2 diabetes, and around 3 mmHg in normotensive adults at doses at or below 360 mg/day for more than 3 months - but these subgroup estimates come from smaller, more heterogeneous trial sets.

Doses studied: Trials clustered around 300-400 mg/day of elemental magnesium (median 368 mg/day in the largest meta-analysis) for at least 4-12 weeks; the Cochrane review required at least 8 weeks of treatment. Subgroup analyses suggest doses at or below 360 mg/day sustained for more than 3 months, or above 300 mg/day in type 2 diabetes, are where effects were detected. Older trials used 10-40 mmol/day (roughly 240-970 mg/day).

Why this grade: Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials exist in reasonable numbers (34 trials, 2028 participants in the largest meta-analysis), and pooled results are positive but small - about 2 mmHg systolic. The evidence is genuinely mixed rather than merely modest: an earlier meta-analysis of 20 trials found no significant overall effect, and the Cochrane review of 12 RCTs found no significant systolic reduction and judged the apparent association weak and probably attributable to bias in low-quality studies. Every one of these syntheses reports blood pressure only; none reports cardiovascular events, and the Cochrane review explicitly called for trials that assess them. Graded moderate rather than strong because of the small effect size, unresolved heterogeneity and the absence of outcome data.

For migraine & headache

Taking magnesium every day may cut down how often migraines come, though the studies genuinely disagree with each other and the effect is modest rather than dramatic. It is cheap, widely available, and headache specialists consider it a reasonable thing to try for prevention — but reviewers who have looked at the same trials have landed anywhere from 'probably works' to 'possibly works'. A separate question is whether magnesium helps once an attack has already started; the evidence there is weaker, and that version is given by IV in a hospital, not taken at home.

Safety

The dose-limiting problem is gastrointestinal: loose stools, diarrhea and stomach upset. In the trials, adverse events occurred in 45.7% on magnesium versus 23.5% on placebo in one study, and 18.6% reported diarrhea in another. Salt form matters for tolerability — oxide and citrate are the most laxative. Intravenous magnesium given for acute attacks carried 37% more adverse events than comparators in meta-analysis and is a clinician-administered treatment, not a home option.

Who should be careful: Do not take oral magnesium supplements without medical supervision if you have significant kidney disease or reduced kidney function — impaired clearance can cause dangerous magnesium accumulation. Also avoid without clinician input in heart block, bradyarrhythmias, or myasthenia gravis. Discuss with a clinician before use in pregnancy or breastfeeding, and before giving it to a child.

Interactions: Magnesium binds several oral drugs and reduces their absorption: tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, bisphosphonates, and levothyroxine should be separated from magnesium by several hours. Effects are additive with magnesium-containing antacids and laxatives. Potassium-sparing diuretics and some proton pump inhibitors can alter magnesium levels.

What the evidence says

PREVENTIVE (daily): Peikert et al. randomized 81 adults to 600 mg (24 mmol) daily oral trimagnesium dicitrate or placebo for 12 weeks and reported attack frequency reduced 41.6% versus 15.8% on placebo in weeks 9-12 (p < 0.05), with diarrhea in 18.6% and gastric irritation in 4.7%. The same year, Pfaffenrath et al. stopped a multicenter trial at interim analysis (n = 69) because responder rates were identical — 28.6% on magnesium versus 29.4% on placebo — with no benefit on migraine days or attacks and adverse events in 45.7% versus 23.5%. Reviews since then disagree: a 2024 dose-response meta-analysis of 22 supplement trials found magnesium reduced attacks (MD -2.51), severity (MD -0.88) and monthly migraine days (MD -1.66), while a 2019 systematic review pooling 3 trials (226 participants) found no significant effect on severity (RoM -0.17, 95% CI -0.36 to 0.02) and rated the evidence low strength, and a 2017 systematic review of 5 randomized placebo-controlled trials — only one of two Class I trials positive — graded magnesium C, possibly effective. A pediatric trial of magnesium oxide 9 mg/kg/day in 118 children found headache frequency fell significantly over time on magnesium (p = 0.0037) but not on placebo (p = 0.086) while the two slopes did not differ from each other (p = 0.88), and a 130-adult trial of a magnesium-riboflavin-CoQ10 combination did not significantly reduce migraine days versus placebo (6.2 to 4.4 versus 6.2 to 5.2, p = 0.23) although pain intensity and HIT-6 improved. The 2012 AAN/AHS guideline classifies magnesium as Level B, probably effective for episodic migraine prevention. ACUTE (during an attack): a meta-analysis of 5 randomized trials (295 patients) of intravenous magnesium found headache relief at 30 minutes was 7% lower than controls (pooled risk difference -0.07, 95% CI -0.23 to 0.09), no reduction in rescue medication, and 37% more adverse events (risk difference 0.370, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.68).

Doses studied: Prevention: 600 mg (24 mmol) daily as trimagnesium dicitrate for 12 weeks in the positive trial, whose reported benefit is for weeks 9-12, so an adequate trial is roughly three months; the null trial used 10 mmol magnesium twice daily for 12 weeks. Magnesium oxide 500 mg has been tested only against sodium valproate in a crossover trial with no placebo arm. A pediatric trial used magnesium oxide 9 mg/kg/day divided three times daily. Acute: intravenous magnesium sulfate, clinician-administered only; doses varied across the five pooled trials.

Why this grade: Preventive evidence is genuinely split and the reviews disagree with each other. Two placebo-controlled trials published the same year reached opposite conclusions (Peikert positive, Pfaffenrath null and stopped for futility); a 2024 dose-response meta-analysis of 22 supplement trials found meaningful reductions in attacks and migraine days; a 2019 systematic review found no significant effect on severity and rated the evidence low strength; and a 2017 systematic review restricted to randomized placebo-controlled trials graded magnesium only C, possibly effective, with just one of two Class I trials positive. The AAN/AHS guideline rates it Level B, probably effective, which is the high end of what the literature will bear rather than a consensus. A pediatric trial and a combination-supplement trial both missed on their frequency endpoints. ACUTE intravenous magnesium is a separate and weaker literature: a meta-analysis of 5 RCTs found no benefit at 30 minutes and significantly more adverse events. Positive but mixed and modest fits 'moderate'; the acute evidence alone would not support it.

For anxiety & stress

Magnesium is a mineral your body needs for nerve and muscle function, and several small studies suggest it may take the edge off everyday anxiety and stress, especially in people whose magnesium levels run low. The honest picture is that those studies were small, often tested magnesium mixed with other ingredients, and mostly lacked a proper dummy-pill comparison; the one careful placebo-controlled study we found showed no difference on an anxiety questionnaire. It is inexpensive and usually gentle, but it is not a proven answer for anxiety.

Safety

Generally well tolerated at the doses studied, roughly 250-300 mg elemental magnesium/day. The most common side effects are dose-related: loose stools, diarrhoea and stomach upset. In the Pouteau trial 17.4% of magnesium-treated participants experienced adverse events considered potentially treatment-related, and in the Tarleton trial supplements were well tolerated without any need for toxicity monitoring. Supplemental magnesium above roughly 350 mg/day of elemental magnesium is more likely to cause diarrhoea in people with normal kidney function.

Who should be careful: People with kidney disease or reduced kidney function should not take magnesium supplements without medical supervision: impaired excretion can lead to magnesium accumulation and toxicity, which presents as weakness, low blood pressure, nausea and heart rhythm changes. Avoid stacking supplements with magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids, which can push the total dose far higher than intended. Anyone with heart block or myasthenia gravis should check with a clinician first.

Interactions: Magnesium can bind and reduce absorption of tetracycline and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, bisphosphonates and levothyroxine; separate dosing by several hours. It may add to the blood-pressure-lowering effect of antihypertensives and to the effects of muscle relaxants. Potassium-sparing diuretics can raise magnesium levels, while loop and thiazide diuretics and proton pump inhibitors can lower them.

What the evidence says

A 2017 systematic review (Boyle et al.) of 18 intervention studies in anxiety-vulnerable samples found positive effects on subjective anxiety in 4 of 8 studies of mildly anxious samples, 4 of 7 premenstrual-syndrome samples and 1 of 2 hypertensive samples, with no effect on postpartum anxiety, and judged the quality of the existing evidence poor. A 2024 systematic review (Rawji et al.) of 15 interventional trials found 5 of 7 anxiety-outcome studies reported improvement in self-reported anxiety, with both negative trials in populations with endocrine contributors. The largest supporting trial (Tarleton 2017) was an open-label randomized crossover study in adults with mild-to-moderate depression, 126 randomized and 112 analysed, in which 248 mg/day elemental magnesium for 6 weeks produced a net GAD-7 improvement of -4.5 points (95% CI -6.6 to -2.4; P<0.001) against a no-treatment control, with anxiety as a secondary outcome. In 264 stressed healthy adults with low magnesemia, magnesium 300 mg/day reduced DASS-42 stress scores by 42.4% at 8 weeks, but the comparator was magnesium plus vitamin B6 rather than placebo and the trial was designed and analysed by the manufacturer of both products. Cutting the other way, a 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of magnesium-L-threonate in 109 patients after breast cancer surgery found no significant difference from placebo on the GAD-7 at any timepoint. Placebo-controlled trials with anxiety as the primary outcome remain lacking, and the 2022 WFSBP/CANMAT guideline does not endorse magnesium for anxiety disorders.

Doses studied: Approximately 248-300 mg/day of elemental magnesium for 6-8 weeks in the supportive trials: magnesium chloride in Tarleton 2017, and a magnesium salt preparation in Pouteau 2018, where the comparator arm combined magnesium with vitamin B6. Forms, doses and durations varied widely across the reviewed studies; the null placebo-controlled trial used magnesium-L-threonate for 12 weeks.

Why this grade: Two systematic reviews (Boyle 2017; Rawji 2024) report improvement in subjective anxiety in most but not all included studies, and both rate the evidence as limited by heterogeneous dosing, frequent multi-ingredient formulations, and small samples. The supporting trials have design problems that matter: Tarleton 2017 was open-label against a no-treatment control with anxiety as a secondary outcome, and Pouteau 2018 had no placebo arm and was designed, run and analysed by the manufacturer of the products compared. The one randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial we located that reported a validated anxiety scale (Ni 2023, GAD-7) found no difference from placebo. The 2022 WFSBP/CANMAT taskforce rated magnesium 'not recommended' for unipolar depression and identified no nutraceutical other than N-acetyl cysteine, in OCD-related disorders, with sufficient evidence in any anxiety-related disorder. Placebo-controlled trials with anxiety as the primary outcome are still lacking; torn between moderate and early, graded lower per convention.

For depression & low mood

Magnesium is cheap, widely available, and there is a real biological story behind why it might matter for mood — but the human evidence is thin. The study most often cited as proof was open-label, meaning everyone knew who was taking the magnesium, which reliably makes a treatment look better than it is; in the properly blinded trials with a dummy pill, magnesium has generally not outperformed placebo, with one small exception among people whose blood magnesium was genuinely low to start with. Getting enough magnesium from food is sensible and supplements are low-risk for most people, but claims that magnesium fixes depression deserve real skepticism.

Safety

Magnesium supplements are generally well tolerated in people with normal kidney function. The most common problem is a laxative effect — loose stools, cramping and nausea — which is strongest with magnesium oxide, citrate and sulfate and milder with glycinate, malate or chloride forms. In the open-label trial, supplements were well tolerated, adherence was 83% by pill count, and 61% of participants said they would use magnesium in the future. Supplemental intake above about 350 mg/day of elemental magnesium raises the chance of diarrhea. Very high blood magnesium (from supplements plus impaired kidney clearance, or from magnesium-containing laxatives and antacids) causes low blood pressure, muscle weakness, confusion and irregular heartbeat, and can be life-threatening. Magnesium is not a substitute for antidepressant treatment or psychotherapy, and no adequately powered blinded trial has shown benefit for depression in people who are not magnesium deficient. There is no specific bipolar-related concern with magnesium, but supplement changes should still be discussed with the prescribing clinician.

Who should be careful: Do not use magnesium supplements without medical supervision if you have chronic kidney disease, reduced kidney function, or are on dialysis — magnesium accumulates and can reach dangerous levels. Also avoid in heart block and myasthenia gravis without clinician input. Anyone with persistent diarrhea, bowel obstruction, or who takes magnesium-containing antacids or laxatives should count total magnesium from all sources.

Interactions: Magnesium binds several drugs in the gut and reduces their absorption: separate doses by at least 2 hours (4 hours for some) from tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics, bisphosphonates, levothyroxine and gabapentin. Long-term proton pump inhibitor use can lower magnesium levels. Loop and thiazide diuretics increase magnesium loss, while potassium-sparing diuretics reduce it. High doses can add to the blood-pressure-lowering effect of antihypertensives and to the effect of muscle relaxants.

What the evidence says

The most-cited trial randomized 126 primary-care adults with PHQ-9 scores of 5-19 to 248 mg/day elemental magnesium as magnesium chloride versus no treatment for 6 weeks in an open-label cross-over design, reporting a net PHQ-9 improvement of -6.0 points (95% CI -7.9 to -4.2, P < 0.001) and GAD-7 improvement of -4.5 points; the absence of a placebo, of blinding, and of a washout period makes that effect size unreliable. A 2023 meta-analysis pooled 7 RCTs totalling only 325 adults with depressive disorder and reported SMD -0.919 (95% CI -1.443 to -0.396, p = 0.001), an effect larger than that seen for most antidepressants and inconsistent with the blinded trial data; its authors called for larger high-quality RCTs before the effect can be interpreted clinically. The blinded trials are mixed and small. In 37 patients with recurrent depressive disorder, magnesium aspartate 120 mg/day added to fluoxetine produced no significant difference from placebo in HDRS scores or serum magnesium at any stage over 8 weeks. In a four-arm trial of 108 women with obesity and mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms, magnesium 250 mg/day with or without vitamin D produced no significant difference in BDI-II scores among the four groups, with depression scores falling in the placebo arm as well. However, in 60 depressed adults selected for hypomagnesemia, 8 weeks of 500 mg/day magnesium oxide produced a significantly greater BDI-II reduction than placebo (15.65 ± 8.9 versus 10.40 ± 7.9, P = 0.02), alongside correction of serum magnesium in 88.5% versus 48.1% (P = 0.002). A perinatal meta-analysis found elemental metals (iron, zinc, magnesium) not superior to placebo (SMD -0.42, 95% CI -1.05 to 0.21), and observational data are weak: across 18 studies serum magnesium was only slightly lower in depression (weighted mean difference -0.088), and removing either of 2 of the 11 serum studies eliminated the difference entirely.

Doses studied: 248 mg/day of elemental magnesium as magnesium chloride for 6 weeks (Tarleton 2017, open-label, positive); 500 mg/day magnesium oxide (two 250-mg tablets) for 8 weeks in depressed adults with hypomagnesemia (Rajizadeh 2017, blinded, positive); 120 mg/day magnesium aspartate added to fluoxetine for 8 weeks (blinded, null); 250 mg/day magnesium for 8 weeks with or without vitamin D (blinded, null). The adult RDA is roughly 310-420 mg/day from all sources; the tolerable upper limit for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg/day of elemental magnesium.

Why this grade: The human evidence is small in both trial count and participant numbers. The only meta-analysis restricted to adults with depressive disorder pooled just 7 RCTs and 325 participants; its large effect (SMD -0.919) is implausibly big for a nutrient and rests heavily on unblinded work — the most-cited positive trial was open-label with no placebo and no washout period in a cross-over design. Among the blinded placebo-controlled trials retrieved here, magnesium added to fluoxetine (n=37) and magnesium with or without vitamin D in women with depressive symptoms (n=108) both found no significant difference from placebo, while one small blinded trial restricted to depressed adults with confirmed hypomagnesemia (n=60) did find a significant advantage (P = 0.02); a perinatal meta-analysis found elemental metals including magnesium not superior to placebo (SMD -0.42, 95% CI -1.05 to 0.21). Graded early rather than moderate because no adequately powered blinded trial supports benefit in unselected depression, and the one positive blinded result is small, single-site and limited to people who were deficient to begin with. Not graded not_supported, because the blinded null trials are individually small and underpowered and the deficiency-correction question has never been tested at scale.

For insomnia

Magnesium is a mineral involved in calming the nervous system, and a handful of small studies suggest it may help people — especially older adults — fall asleep a bit faster and feel their sleep is better. The honest picture is that the studies so far are small and not very rigorous, so we can't yet say how well it works or which form is best. It is inexpensive and generally safe for people with healthy kidneys, with loose stools being the main side effect.

Safety

Generally well tolerated in trials at the doses studied. The most common side effect is dose-related loose stools or diarrhea, more likely with oxide and citrate forms; bisglycinate is marketed as gentler on the gut. The 2025 bisglycinate RCT and 2024 L-threonate RCT reported the supplements were safe and well tolerated.

Who should be careful: People with kidney disease or reduced kidney function should not take magnesium supplements without medical supervision — impaired excretion can lead to magnesium accumulation (hypermagnesemia). Anyone with heart block or severe GI disease should check with a clinician first.

Interactions: Magnesium binds some medicines in the gut: separate dosing from fluoroquinolone and tetracycline antibiotics, levothyroxine, and bisphosphonates by at least 2-4 hours. May add to the blood-pressure-lowering effect of antihypertensives and interact with some diuretics.

What the evidence says

Evidence for magnesium in insomnia is limited in quantity and quality. The 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis by Mah & Pitre pooled 3 RCTs in 151 older adults and found sleep-onset latency 17.36 minutes shorter with magnesium versus placebo (95% CI -27.27 to -7.44, p=0.0006), with a non-significant 16.06-minute gain in total sleep time; all trials were at moderate-to-high risk of bias and GRADE quality was low to very low. In the largest single older-adult trial (Abbasi 2012; n=46), 500 mg/day for 8 weeks significantly reduced Insomnia Severity Index scores (p=0.006) and sleep-onset latency (p=0.02) and increased sleep efficiency (p=0.03), though between-group total sleep time was not significant. A 2025 RCT of magnesium bisglycinate (250 mg elemental; n=155 adults with poor sleep) found a small ISI advantage over placebo (-3.9 vs -2.3 points at week 4, p=0.049; Cohen's d=0.2). A 21-day RCT of magnesium L-threonate 1 g/day (n=80, industry-affiliated authors) reported improved wearable-derived deep-sleep and REM scores and daytime functioning (p<0.05). Formulations differ meaningfully — oxide and citrate are common and cheap but more laxative; bisglycinate and L-threonate are marketed for tolerability and brain uptake respectively — but no head-to-head sleep trials exist.

Doses studied: Trials used roughly 250-500 mg elemental magnesium daily (e.g., 500 mg/day for 8 weeks in older adults; 250 mg/day bisglycinate for 4 weeks; magnesium L-threonate 1 g/day of the salt for 3 weeks). The 2021 meta-analysis noted regimens under 1 g up to three times daily.

Why this grade: RCT and meta-analytic evidence exists but is thin and low quality: the only systematic review with meta-analysis (Mah & Pitre 2021) pooled just 3 RCTs (n=151, older adults), all at moderate-to-high risk of bias, with GRADE quality rated low to very low despite a statistically significant latency benefit. Newer RCTs of bisglycinate (d=0.2) and L-threonate (industry-involved, n=80) are small with modest effects, and different formulations have not been compared head-to-head. Torn between moderate and early, the lower grade was taken.
