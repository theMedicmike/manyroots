Anxiety & stress

Anxiety is your body's alarm system going off when there's nothing to run from — a racing heart, a tight chest, a mind that keeps scanning for what's about to go wrong. If this is you, you are in very ordinary company: the best global estimate is that around 7 in every 100 people have an anxiety disorder at any given moment. There is real good news here, because anxiety is one of the better-studied problems in medicine — a specific kind of talk therapy (CBT) and certain prescription medications have genuine trial evidence behind them. What that evidence shows is meaningful help, not a switch that turns anxiety off, and everything else on this page gets measured against that same honest bar.

Strongest evidence here: Cognitive behavioral therapy, SSRIs/SNRIs.

When to stop and get real care

These come before anything else on this page.

emergencyThoughts of suicide, self-harm, or not wanting to be here, alongside the anxietyStop reading and talk to a person now. Call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. It is free, confidential, and available 24/7, and you do not have to be in immediate danger to use it — "I'm having thoughts I don't like" is exactly the right reason to call. Veterans, service members, and their families: call 988 and press 1. If you have already hurt yourself, or you don't feel safe being alone right now, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency room. Nothing on this page, and nothing you could take or try, is a substitute for that call.
emergencyChest pain, pressure, or other heart-attack symptoms that could be mistaken for a panic attackCall 911 now. Do not assume it is anxiety. Chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to your arm, jaw, neck, or back, shortness of breath, a cold sweat, nausea, or feeling like you might pass out all need emergency evaluation — especially if this is new for you, started with exertion, or you have risk factors like high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, or heart disease in your family. A panic attack and a heart attack can feel identical from the inside; an ECG and a blood test are the only things that reliably tell them apart. If it turns out to be panic, you lost a few hours. If you guess wrong the other way, you may not get a second chance.
urgentAnxiety alongside daily alcohol use or a benzodiazepine prescription (Xanax/alprazolam, Ativan/lorazepam, Klonopin/clonazepam, Valium/diazepam) — where stopping suddenly is the dangerDo not quit cold turkey, and do not design your own taper. Alcohol and benzodiazepines are two of the very few substances where stopping abruptly after regular use can cause seizures and delirium and can kill you — this is not the same as feeling awful for a week. Keep taking your prescription as prescribed until a clinician gives you a plan. Call your prescriber or primary care provider and ask specifically for a supervised taper; if you don't have one, SAMHSA's free, confidential National Helpline is 1-800-662-4357, 24/7. If you have already stopped and you are shaking, sweating, vomiting, confused, seeing or hearing things, or your heart is pounding, that is a medical emergency — call 911.
routineNew, severe anxiety that seemed to come out of nowhere — worth ruling out a physical cause firstBook a regular medical appointment and ask to rule out physical causes before treating this as "just anxiety." Anxiety that starts suddenly in adulthood, or arrives with a racing heart, tremor, unintended weight loss, heat intolerance, diarrhea, or new insomnia, deserves a basic workup — thyroid function tests at minimum. Bring a complete list of everything you put in your body: prescriptions, over-the-counter products, supplements, caffeine and energy drinks, nicotine, alcohol, cannabis, and stimulants such as ADHD medication, decongestants, or weight-loss products — plus anything you recently started, stopped, or changed the dose of. This is a routine appointment, not an emergency, but make it before you spend months working on this from the mind side alone.
routineAnxiety that has lasted months and is interfering with work, sleep, or the people you loveThis is the point where a clinician belongs in the picture — not more self-management. When anxiety is persistent and impairing, the treatments with the strongest evidence behind them are cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and/or an SSRI or SNRI medication; these are what international treatment guidelines name as first-line, and they are the bar every other option on this site is graded against. Ask your primary care provider for a referral to a CBT therapist, or look for one directly. You are welcome to keep exploring what is here, but treat it as something that sits alongside first-line care, never as a replacement for it or a reason to keep postponing the appointment.

Clinical detail

"Anxiety & stress" spans normal stress reactivity through the diagnosable anxiety disorders — generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder/agoraphobia, social anxiety disorder, specific phobia, separation anxiety — characterized by excessive, persistent fear or worry disproportionate to the situation and producing functional impairment. PREVALENCE: a systematic review and meta-regression drawing prevalence from 87 studies across 44 countries estimated global current prevalence of anxiety disorders at 7.3% (95% CI 4.8-10.9%) after adjustment for methodological differences, ranging from 5.3% (3.5-8.1%) in African cultures to 10.4% (7.0-15.5%) in Euro/Anglo cultures (PMID 22781489). FIRST-LINE TREATMENT PER GUIDELINES: the WFSBP Version 3 guidelines — a consensus panel of 33 international experts from 22 countries evaluating 1,007 RCTs — name SSRIs and SNRIs as first-line medications and cognitive behavioural therapy as the first-line psychotherapy for anxiety disorders (PMID 35900161). MAGNITUDE, HONESTLY: a meta-analysis of 27 randomized placebo-controlled trials found CBT superior to placebo, pooled Hedges' g 0.73 for continuous anxiety severity and a completer response odds ratio of 4.06 (95% CI 2.78-5.92), with the strongest effects in OCD and acute stress disorder, the weakest in panic disorder, and the authors explicitly noting "considerable room for improvement" (PMID 18363421). CONTRARY/NULL EVIDENCE: a network meta-analysis of 91 RCTs (14,812 participants) in GAD found SSRIs beat placebo (SMD -0.67, 95% CrI -0.90 to -0.43) and benzodiazepines beat placebo (-0.40, -0.65 to -0.15), but reported that no psychological intervention showed greater effect than a psychological placebo — only than waitlist (PMID 31494377). That is the single most important caveat on this page: waitlist-controlled results for any talk-based or behavioural intervention systematically overstate what the intervention itself adds beyond attention, expectancy and the passage of time, and ManyRoots grades accordingly. Anxiety commonly co-occurs with depression and substance use; new-onset or atypical presentations warrant screening for medical mimics (thyroid dysfunction, stimulants, prescription and OTC medication effects, caffeine, withdrawal states) before attribution to a primary anxiety disorder.

Sources for this condition (4)
  1. Global prevalence of anxiety disorders: a systematic review and meta-regression.
    · PMID 22781489 · mixed

    Prevalence was obtained from 87 studies across 44 countries; current-prevalence estimates ranged from 0.9% to 28.3% and past-year from 2.4% to 29.8%. Adjusted for methodological differences, global current prevalence of anxiety disorders was 7.3% (95% CI 4.8-10.9%), ranging from 5.3% (3.5-8.1%) in African cultures to 10.4% (7.0-15.5%) in Euro/Anglo cultures. Direction: descriptive/epidemiological — establishes how common anxiety disorders are.

  2. World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) guidelines for treatment of anxiety, obsessive-compulsive and posttraumatic stress disorders - Version 3. Part I: Anxiety disorders.
    · PMID 35900161 · mixed

    A consensus panel of 33 international experts representing 22 countries evaluated 1,007 RCTs of medications, psychotherapy and other non-pharmacological interventions. SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line medications; CBT is the first-line psychotherapy for anxiety disorders. The panel also issued explicit recommendations AGAINST interventions with insufficient evidence. Direction: positive for SSRI/SNRI and CBT as first-line; negative for unproven interventions.

  3. Cognitive-behavioral therapy for adult anxiety disorders: a meta-analysis of randomized placebo-controlled trials.
    · PMID 18363421 · mixed

    27 of 1,165 screened studies met inclusion (adults randomized to CBT vs placebo). Random-effects completer analysis: pooled Hedges' g 0.73 for continuous anxiety severity and 0.45 for depressive severity; pooled odds ratio for treatment response 4.06 (95% CI 2.78-5.92). No significant attrition difference between CBT and placebo. Strongest effects in OCD and acute stress disorder; weakest in panic disorder. Authors conclude CBT is efficacious but state there is "considerable room for improvement." Direction: positive but bounded — placebo-controlled, not waitlist-controlled.

  4. Pharmacological and psychological interventions for generalized anxiety disorder in adults: A network meta-analysis.
    · PMID 31494377 · mixed

    91 RCTs, 14,812 participants with GAD. Versus placebo: SSRIs SMD -0.67 (95% CrI -0.90 to -0.43), azapirones -0.58 (-1.00 to -0.17), anticonvulsants -0.56 (-0.85 to -0.28), SNRIs -0.54 (-0.79 to -0.30), benzodiazepines -0.40 (-0.65 to -0.15); serotonin modulators and second-generation antipsychotics did NOT beat placebo. Critically, most psychological and self-help interventions beat waitlist, but no psychological intervention had greater effect than a psychological placebo. Direction: MIXED/NEGATIVE — the key null finding constraining every talk-based or behavioural claim on this page.
