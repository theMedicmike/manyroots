Gut & digestion

Gut trouble — bloating, cramping, urgency, constipation, mapping every bathroom before you leave the house — is exhausting, and it is also one of the most common health problems there is: in a survey of 73,076 adults across 33 countries, more than 40% met criteria for a long-term gut condition like IBS. What you are dealing with is real and recognised, not imagined, and most of the time nothing dangerous is found. But the gut is also where several serious conditions first show up wearing ordinary clothes, so the honest first step is not a new diet — it is getting the right tests, in the right order, before you change how you eat. The evidence for what helps afterwards is genuinely useful but modest, and it works best once the things that need ruling out have been ruled out.

Strongest evidence here: Linaclotide and other secretagogues for IBS-C, Low-FODMAP diet.

When to stop and get real care

These come before anything else on this page.

emergencyVomiting blood (or material that looks like coffee grounds), passing a large amount of blood from the bowel, or feeling faint, dizzy, clammy or short of breath alongside any bleeding.Stop here and call 911 (or your local emergency number) now. Do not drive yourself, do not wait to see whether it settles, and do not eat or drink until you have been assessed. If you take a blood thinner, aspirin, or an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen or naproxen, tell the crew or the emergency team and bring the packets if you can.
emergencyFever with severe abdominal pain; a belly that is rigid, board-like, or exquisitely painful to touch; or pain with vomiting and no bowel movement and no passing of gas.Go to the emergency department now, or call 911 if the pain is severe or you cannot get there safely. Do not eat or drink, do not take a laxative, do not use an enema, and do not take painkillers to see whether it passes — each of those can worsen an obstruction or a perforation and can mask what the surgical team needs to be able to feel.
emergencySevere, new, or worsening abdominal pain or vomiting in pregnancy — especially pain high in the abdomen or under the right ribs.Contact your maternity unit or obstetric team the same day. Go to the emergency department or call 911 if the pain is severe or constant, if it comes with headache, visual changes or sudden swelling, or if you cannot keep fluids down. Do not treat this as reflux or indigestion, and do not start a supplement or an elimination diet in pregnancy without your maternity team's input.
urgentAny blood in or on the stool, blood on the paper, or stools that are black and tarry — even once, even if you are sure it is haemorrhoids.Book a doctor's appointment now, and use the words "rectal bleeding" or "black stools" when you book — that phrase changes how quickly you are seen. Do not start a new diet, supplement, fibre regimen or cleanse while you wait. If the bleeding becomes heavy, or you feel faint, go to the emergency department instead.
urgentLosing weight without trying, or new gut symptoms that started after age 50.Ask your doctor for a workup, not a diet trial. Say how much weight you have lost and over what period, and that the symptoms are new. Ask directly whether you need a stool test or a colonoscopy, and hold off on any elimination diet until that conversation has happened.
urgentPain or diarrhoea that wakes you from sleep.Tell your doctor specifically that symptoms wake you at night. That single detail points away from IBS and can change the tests you are offered. Ask about a stool test for inflammation (faecal calprotectin), and — if diarrhoea is persistent and watery — ask whether you need a colonoscopy with biopsies even if the bowel looks normal.
urgentAnaemia, or persistent fatigue, breathlessness or pallor alongside gut symptoms.Ask for blood tests — at minimum a full blood count and ferritin — before you try to fix the fatigue with supplements or a diet. If you are told you are iron deficient, ask what the plan is to find out why, not only how to replace the iron.
urgentGet tested for coeliac disease BEFORE you remove gluten — including before starting low-FODMAP, which cuts wheat too. The order is not optional.If you are thinking about going gluten-free, or starting a low-FODMAP diet, ask your doctor for coeliac blood tests FIRST, while you are still eating gluten normally. If you have already stopped, do not quietly start eating gluten again on your own — tell your clinician you have been gluten-free and for how long, and let them decide whether you need a supervised gluten challenge before testing.
routineA parent, sibling or child with bowel cancer, coeliac disease, or inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's or ulcerative colitis) — and you now have new gut symptoms.Tell your clinician the family history explicitly: who, what condition, and at what age they were diagnosed. It changes which tests you should have, when you should have them, and the age at which screening should start. Write it down before the appointment — this is the detail people most often forget to mention, and it is the one that most often changes the plan.
routineStaying on a restrictive elimination diet long-term without a dietitian — and treating low-FODMAP as a permanent way of eating rather than a temporary test.If you are more than 4-6 weeks into a low-FODMAP or elimination diet, stop expanding the restriction and ask for a referral to a registered dietitian to run the reintroduction phase. If your list of safe foods keeps shrinking, if restriction has started to feel like the only thing keeping you safe, or if you now avoid eating out or eating with other people, say that out loud to a clinician. That is a recognised risk of these diets, not a personal failing, and it is something they are meant to screen for.

Clinical detail

DEFINITION. "Gut and digestion" spans the disorders of gut-brain interaction (DGBI, formerly functional gastrointestinal disorders) defined by the Rome IV criteria — principally irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional dyspepsia and functional constipation — together with the organic diseases that mimic them and must be excluded: coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, microscopic colitis and colorectal neoplasia. The British Society of Gastroenterology's 2021 guideline formally reclassified IBS as a disorder of gut-brain interaction rather than a functional gastrointestinal disorder (PMID 33903147). PREVALENCE. In the Rome Foundation Global Study (73,076 adults, 33 countries), at least one FGID was present in 40.3% (95% CI 39.9-40.7) of internet-survey respondents and 20.7% (95% CI 20.2-21.3) of household-interview respondents; prevalence was higher in women (OR 1.7 internet, 1.3 household) and was associated with lower quality of life and more frequent doctor visits (PMID 32294476). For IBS specifically, pooled prevalence was 9.2% (95% CI 7.6-10.8) across 53 Rome III studies versus 3.8% (95% CI 3.1-4.5) across six Rome IV studies, and 12.0% in women versus 8.6% in men (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.33-1.59) (PMID 32702295). The 9.2% versus 3.8% gap between criteria sets is the single most important caveat on any prevalence figure quoted here: no global IBS number should be published without naming the criteria that produced it. DIAGNOSIS AND FIRST-LINE MANAGEMENT PER GUIDELINES. The ACG (2021, GRADE methodology across 25 clinical questions) suggests a positive, symptom-based diagnostic strategy rather than diagnosis by exclusion; serologic testing to rule out coeliac disease and faecal calprotectin to rule out inflammatory bowel disease in patients with suspected IBS and diarrhoea; a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet to improve global symptoms; chloride channel activators and guanylate cyclase activators for IBS with constipation; rifaximin for IBS with diarrhoea; and gut-directed psychotherapy for global symptoms (PMID 33315591). The AGA Clinical Practice Update adds that soluble fibre is efficacious for global IBS symptoms and specifies the low-FODMAP diet as three phases — restriction lasting no more than 4-6 weeks, reintroduction, then personalisation — ideally delivered with a registered dietitian nutritionist, with routine screening for disordered eating by careful dietary history before any restrictive intervention (PMID 35337654). EVIDENCE LIMITS, STATED PLAINLY. Many of the ACG's IBS statements are suggestions rather than strong recommendations, reflecting limited certainty of evidence. Although observational studies find that most patients with IBS improve on a gluten-free diet, randomised controlled trials have yielded mixed results, and there is insufficient evidence to use biomarkers to predict diet response in routine practice (PMID 35337654). Order of operations matters clinically: coeliac antibody testing and duodenal biopsy both lose sensitivity once gluten has been removed, so investigation must precede dietary restriction.

Sources for this condition (4)
  1. Worldwide Prevalence and Burden of Functional Gastrointestinal Disorders, Results of Rome Foundation Global Study.
    · PMID 32294476 · mixed

    Across 73,076 adult respondents in 33 countries on 6 continents, diagnostic criteria were met for at least one functional gastrointestinal disorder (disorder of gut-brain interaction) by 40.3% (95% CI 39.9-40.7) of those completing internet surveys and 20.7% (95% CI 20.2-21.3) of those completing household surveys. FGIDs were more prevalent among women (OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.6-1.7 internet; OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.3-1.4 household) and were associated with lower quality of life and more frequent doctor visits. Proportions with IBS were lower under Rome IV than Rome III criteria (4.1% vs 10.1% internet; 1.5% vs 3.5% household).

  2. Global prevalence of irritable bowel syndrome according to Rome III or IV criteria: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
    · PMID 32702295 · mixed

    Pooled IBS prevalence was 9.2% (95% CI 7.6-10.8) in 53 studies using Rome III criteria (38 countries, 395,385 participants) but only 3.8% (95% CI 3.1-4.5) in six studies using Rome IV criteria (34 countries, 82,476 participants). Prevalence was higher in women than men (12.0%, 95% CI 9.3-15.0 vs 8.6%, 95% CI 6.3-11.2; OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.33-1.59). The authors conclude that prevalence varies substantially between countries even when uniform criteria and identical methodology are applied — a direct caution against quoting any single global IBS figure without naming the criteria behind it. DIRECTION: this is a methodological caution as much as a prevalence estimate; it undercuts the higher figures commonly cited for IBS.

  3. ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome.
    · PMID 33315591 · mixed

    GRADE-based guideline covering 25 clinically important questions (9 diagnostic, 16 therapeutic). Endorsements include: a positive diagnostic strategy rather than a strategy of exclusion; serologic testing to rule out coeliac disease in patients with IBS and diarrhoea; faecal calprotectin in suspected IBS with diarrhoea to rule out inflammatory bowel disease; a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet to improve global symptoms; chloride channel activators and guanylate cyclase activators for IBS with constipation; rifaximin for IBS with diarrhoea; and gut-directed psychotherapy for global symptoms. DIRECTION: several statements are worded as suggestions rather than recommendations, indicating limited certainty of evidence rather than uniform strong support.

  4. AGA Clinical Practice Update on the Role of Diet in Irritable Bowel Syndrome: Expert Review.
    · PMID 35337654 · mixed

    Best Practice Advice states that soluble fibre is efficacious for global IBS symptoms and that the low-FODMAP diet is currently the most evidence-based diet intervention for IBS, but specifies it as three phases: restriction lasting no more than 4-6 weeks, reintroduction of FODMAP foods, and personalisation. Poor candidates for restrictive diet interventions include those consuming few culprit foods, those at risk for malnutrition, those who are food insecure, and those with an eating disorder or uncontrolled psychiatric disorder; routine screening for disordered eating by careful dietary history is called critical because it is common and often overlooked in gastrointestinal conditions. NEGATIVE/CONTRARY DIRECTION EXPLICITLY INCLUDED: although observational studies found that most patients with IBS improve with a gluten-free diet, randomised controlled trials have yielded mixed results; and there is insufficient evidence to support routine use of biomarkers to predict diet response.
