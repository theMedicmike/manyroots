Fatigue & low energy

Being tired all the time is one of the most common reasons people see a doctor, and it is real — you are not imagining it and you are not failing at something. Most of the time the cause turns out to be ordinary and fixable: sleep that isn't working, low mood, an infection you haven't shaken, a thyroid that's drifted, or low iron. Sometimes no single cause is ever found, and that's common too — around half of people who see a doctor about new fatigue never get one clear label, which is frustrating but not a sign you're being dismissed. The most useful first step is almost never a supplement; it's a conversation and a small handful of basic blood tests, so you find out what you're actually dealing with before you spend money guessing.

When to stop and get real care

These come before anything else on this page.

emergencyFatigue with chest pain, breathlessness at rest or on mild exertion, fainting, or a racing/pounding heartStop here and get emergency care now. If your tiredness comes with chest pain or pressure, breathlessness when you are resting or doing something light, fainting or nearly fainting, or a racing or pounding heartbeat, call 911 (or your local emergency number) or go to an emergency department. These can be signs of a heart problem or of severe anaemia. Both need treating today, and neither is something a supplement can touch.
emergencyFatigue alongside hopelessness or thoughts of suicideIf you are feeling hopeless, or having thoughts of hurting yourself or of ending your life, please stop reading and reach a person now. In the US, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Veterans: call 988 and press 1. You can also go to any emergency department. Deep exhaustion and despair travel together far more often than people realise — that is a reason to get support, not evidence that something is wrong with you.
urgentFatigue with unexplained weight loss, drenching night sweats, recurrent fevers, or a new lumpBook an urgent appointment — this week, not next month. Weight you did not try to lose, night sweats that soak your bedding or nightclothes, fevers that keep coming back, or a new lump anywhere need to be looked at by a clinician alongside your tiredness. Most of the time it will not be cancer. But this is exactly the combination clinicians are trained to investigate quickly, and being seen sooner costs you nothing except an appointment.
urgentFatigue with unusual bruising or bleeding, or looking very paleAsk for a full blood count within the next few days. If you are bruising without knocking into anything, your gums bleed, you are getting nosebleeds that will not settle, or people are telling you that you look very pale, contact your clinician and say you would like a full blood count soon. A problem with how your blood is being made is found on a simple, cheap, widely available test — not by trying another supplement.
urgentNew fatigue with excessive thirst and passing a lot of urineGet your blood sugar checked this week. New tiredness together with constant thirst and needing to pee far more than usual should prompt a blood glucose or HbA1c test — ask for it directly. If you also have vomiting, stomach pain, fast deep breathing, or breath that smells sweet or fruity, go to an emergency department now rather than waiting for an appointment.
urgentLoud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, or falling asleep during the dayAsk about a sleep apnea assessment before you try anything else. If you snore loudly, if someone has seen you stop breathing during sleep, or if you fall asleep during the day when you did not mean to, that pattern needs a proper sleep evaluation. No supplement, vitamin, herb or adaptogen treats sleep apnea. Please also be careful about driving while you are this sleepy, and tell your clinician if you drive for work.
routineHeavy periods with fatigue — common, very treatable, and routinely dismissedIf your periods are heavy and you are exhausted, ask specifically for a ferritin test, not just a haemoglobin. Soaking a pad or tampon every hour or two, passing clots bigger than a coin, or bleeding for more than about a week counts as heavy — even if it is what you have always had. Heavy periods are the single most common reason people run low on iron, low iron is very treatable, and this gets brushed off constantly. Ask anyway, and ask again if you are told your bloods are 'normal' without a ferritin being run.
routineCORE MESSAGE — persistent fatigue deserves basic blood tests before any supplementBefore you buy a single supplement, get the basic blood tests. Persistent tiredness deserves a full blood count, ferritin or iron studies, thyroid function, glucose or HbA1c, vitamin B12, and a coeliac screen if your clinician thinks it fits your picture. The most common treatable causes of fatigue show up on that panel. Supplements taken blind can shift your numbers just enough to hide the answer — and six months later you will have spent the money and still be tired. Get the tests first. Then come back and read.
routineTaking iron without a confirmed deficiency is not harmlessDo not start iron 'just in case'. Ask for a ferritin test first. There is evidence that iron helps tiredness in people who are genuinely low on it — and no good evidence that it does anything for tiredness in people who are not. Iron that your body does not need accumulates in the liver, heart and pancreas over years and causes real organ damage. And if you do turn out to be low, the important next question is why you are low, because in men and in women past menopause that often means bleeding somewhere that needs finding.
routineFatigue that began after an infection and worsens after exertion (post-exertional malaise)If your fatigue started after an infection and gets dramatically worse for a day or more after you exert yourself — physically or mentally — that pattern has a name: post-exertional malaise. Do not try to push through it with exercise. Look for a clinician who works with ME/CFS or long COVID, and read about pacing before you start any activity or training plan. Advice to 'just build up your fitness gradually' is the specific thing that can set you back, and it is no longer what the guidelines say.

Clinical detail

Fatigue is persistent tiredness, exhaustion or lack of energy that is not relieved by rest and is disproportionate to exertion. It is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and is distinguished clinically from sleepiness and from true muscle weakness. Prevalence: fatigue is a main or secondary reason for 10-20% of all primary care consultations (Maisel 2021, PMID 34196270). The differential is unusually broad — in an English population-based cohort of 304,914 patients presenting with new-onset fatigue compared with 423,671 who did not, the most strongly associated conditions were depression, respiratory tract infections, insomnia and sleep disturbance, and hypo/hyperthyroidism in women (White 2024, PMID 39084871). Serious pathology is uncommon: in the review informing the German DEGAM fatigue guideline, previously undiagnosed cancer accounted for 0.6% of cases (95% CI 0.3-1.3) and anaemia plus other organic causes for 4.3% (95% CI 2.7-6.7) (PMID 34196270); in a Dutch primary care cohort followed for one year, only 47 of 571 patients (8.2%) had clear somatic pathology and roughly half received no diagnosis that explained the fatigue at all (Nijrolder 2009, PMID 19858240). First-line management per guidelines is therefore diagnostic rather than pharmacological: a full history, cardiopulmonary/neurological/skin examination and simple laboratory tests, with investigation beyond that only where additional symptoms or findings are present; treat any identified underlying cause; and where fatigue remains unexplained, use watchful waiting with scheduled follow-up rather than escalating tests, specifically to avoid overdiagnosis and an exclusively somatic focus (PMID 34196270). Where post-exertional malaise is present, the 2021 NICE ME/CFS guideline permits diagnosis after 3 months, states that any programme based on fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise (graded exercise therapy) should not be offered as a treatment, and positions CBT as a supportive intervention only (Kingdon 2022, PMID 36553962). No supplement is first-line for undifferentiated fatigue in any of these guidelines.

Sources for this condition (4)
  1. Fatigue as the Chief Complaint-Epidemiology, Causes, Diagnosis, and Treatment.
    · PMID 34196270 · mixed

    Fatigue is a main or secondary reason for 10-20% of all primary care consultations. Sleep disorders and sleep-related breathing disorders, depression (18.5%) and excessive psychosocial stress are the most common causes of persistent fatigue. Previously undiagnosed cancer is a rare cause at 0.6% (95% CI 0.3-1.3), and anaemia and other organic causes are also rare at 4.3% (95% CI 2.7-6.7). Investigations beyond history, physical examination and simple laboratory tests are needed only in the presence of additional symptoms or findings; where the diagnosis remains unclear, watchful waiting with regularly scheduled follow-up helps prevent an excessive somatic focus and overdiagnosis.

  2. Underlying disease risk among patients with fatigue: a population-based cohort study in primary care.
    · PMID 39084871 · mixed

    Cohort of 304,914 patients presenting in English primary care with new-onset fatigue (2007-2017) versus 423,671 presenting without fatigue, examining 237 diseases. Diseases most strongly associated with fatigue were depression, respiratory tract infections, insomnia and sleep disturbances, and hypo/hyperthyroidism (women only). By age 80 years, cancer risk in men presenting with fatigue was 7.01% (95% CI 6.54-7.51) versus 3.36% (95% CI 3.08-3.67) in those who did not present with fatigue, an absolute excess risk of 3.65%; in women, cancer remained relatively infrequent and ranked only thirteenth by excess risk. Findings support prioritising cancer investigation in older men (aged 70+) with fatigue, but not in women at any age based solely on the presence of fatigue.

  3. Diagnoses during follow-up of patients presenting with fatigue in primary care.
    · PMID 19858240 · mixed

    Prospective cohort of adults presenting to primary care with a new episode of fatigue, followed for one year. Of 571 patients with diagnostic data, 268 (46.9%) received one or more diagnoses that could be associated with the fatigue, mostly symptom diagnoses — musculoskeletal (19.4%) and psychological (16.5%). Clear somatic pathology was diagnosed in only 47 patients (8.2%). Half of patients received no diagnosis that could explain their fatigue, and most diagnoses were not made at the consultation where fatigue was first presented.

  4. What Primary Care Practitioners Need to Know about the New NICE Guideline for Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome in Adults.
    · PMID 36553962 · mixed

    The NICE ME/CFS guideline published October 2021 recognises post-exertional malaise as a core symptom, allows diagnosis after just 3 months, and requires individually tailored management by a multidisciplinary team. It makes clear that any programme based on fixed incremental increases in physical activity or exercise — for example graded exercise therapy (GET) — should not be offered as a treatment for ME/CFS, and that cognitive behavioural therapy should only be offered as a supportive intervention.
