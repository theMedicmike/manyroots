Blood sugar & type 2 diabetes

Blood sugar problems usually build quietly, often for years, before anyone puts a name to them — so if you have just been told you have prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, you are not behind, you are early. Prediabetes means your blood sugar sits higher than it should but has not crossed the diabetes line yet; type 2 diabetes means it has. You are in very large company: in US national survey data, prediabetes rose to about 48% of adults and diabetes to about 15% by 2017-2018. The honest good news is that this is one of the most heavily studied conditions in all of medicine, so unlike a lot of what you will read online, here we can actually tell you which things have been tested in real trials and which have not.

Strongest evidence here: GLP-1 receptor agonists, Metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, Diabetes Prevention Program-style intensive lifestyle intervention.

When to stop and get real care

These come before anything else on this page.

emergencySTANDING INTERRUPT: never stop, skip, stretch or ration insulin or diabetes medication to try a supplement, food or protocol from this pageNEVER STOP INSULIN OR DIABETES MEDICATION TO TRY A SUPPLEMENT. Nothing on this site — no herb, no nutrient, no eating pattern, no protocol — replaces insulin or prescribed diabetes medicine. Stopping insulin is the single most common reason people end up in hospital with diabetic ketoacidosis, and it can happen inside a day. If you want to try something here, bring it to your prescriber, keep your medication running, and change one thing at a time. If the real reason you are skipping, stretching or rationing doses is cost, say that out loud to your prescriber or pharmacist TODAY — there are patient assistance programmes and lower-cost options, and rationing is far more dangerous than the conversation. If you have already stopped and you feel sick, are vomiting, or have stomach pain: stop reading and get emergency care now.
emergencySigns of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): vomiting, stomach pain, fast or deep breathing, sweet/fruity breath, drowsiness or confusionSTOP. Get emergency care now. If you have diabetes and you are vomiting, have stomach pain, are breathing fast or unusually deeply, have breath that smells sweet or fruity, or feel confused or very sleepy — call emergency services or go to an emergency department. This can be diabetic ketoacidosis. It gets worse over hours, not days. Check ketones if you have a meter, but do not wait for a result to get help. Do not talk yourself out of going because your glucose reading looks acceptable — a reading in the 200s, or even under 200, does not rule this out. And do not skip your insulin because you cannot eat; that makes DKA worse, not better.
emergencyEUGLYCAEMIC DKA on an SGLT2 inhibitor — ketoacidosis with a normal-looking glucose readingREAD THIS IF YOU TAKE AN SGLT2 INHIBITOR (empagliflozin/Jardiance, dapagliflozin/Farxiga, canagliflozin/Invokana, ertugliflozin/Steglatro, or a combination pill containing one). You can be in diabetic ketoacidosis while your meter reads normal. A reading of 130 does not mean you are safe. If you feel sick, are vomiting, have stomach pain, are unusually breathless or breathing hard, or just feel badly 'off' — get emergency care and say this out loud: 'I take an SGLT2 inhibitor. Please check me for euglycaemic DKA and test my ketones, not just my glucose.' Ask specifically for a blood ketone (beta-hydroxybutyrate) test. If you are sent home without one and still feel unwell, go back.
emergencySevere hypoglycaemia — the person cannot treat themselves, is confused, seizing, or unconsciousTHIS IS AN EMERGENCY. FOR WHOEVER IS WITH THEM: Call emergency services. Do NOT put food or drink into the mouth of someone who is not fully awake and able to swallow — they can choke. Give glucagon if there is any in the house: nasal glucagon is a single spray into one nostril (no need to inhale), or use the auto-injector or pre-filled syringe into the outer thigh. Turn them on their side and stay with them until help arrives. When they wake and can swallow, give fast sugar (juice, glucose tablets) followed by a snack with protein or starch. FOR THE PERSON WITH DIABETES: if you use insulin, or have ever had a low you could not handle alone, ask your prescriber for a glucagon prescription and physically show at least one person you live with, work with or travel with where it is and how to use it — an unopened kit in a drawer nobody knows about saves no one. After ANY severe low, book a medication review within days; do not let it be filed as a one-off. If the low involved a sulfonylurea, read the next flag now — the timescale there is hours, not days.
emergencyA severe low caused by a sulfonylurea comes back — one correction is not the end of itIf the low happened to someone taking a sulfonylurea (glipizide, glyburide/glibenclamide, gliclazide, glimepiride), they need to be assessed in hospital and watched, not treated at home and put back to bed. These drugs go on forcing insulin out for many hours — far longer than the sugar you just gave them lasts — so the person can wake up, seem completely fine, and go down again hours later, usually overnight and alone. Do not accept 'they're fine now, no need to go in.' Say to the clinician: 'This was a sulfonylurea hypo — does this need extended observation?' There is a specific treatment (octreotide) for lows that keep coming back, and it is only given in hospital. Say the same thing if the low started within days of a new prescription: some antifungals and antibiotics push sulfonylurea levels up sharply.
emergencyHyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS): very high sugar with extreme thirst, constant urination, dehydration, confusion or unusual drowsiness — most often in an older adultCall emergency services. Very high blood sugar together with relentless thirst, urinating constantly, a dry mouth and skin, weakness, confusion, slurred speech or unusual sleepiness — particularly in someone older, or someone who has been unwell for several days — can be hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state. It builds over days to weeks, so by the time it is obvious the person is usually severely dehydrated. Do not try to fix it at home with fluids and waiting. If you are the family member noticing that an older relative with diabetes 'isn't themselves', that observation is the emergency. Say it plainly at the door: 'They have diabetes, they have been unwell for days, and they are not themselves.'
emergencyChest, jaw, arm or upper-back discomfort, sudden breathlessness, drenching sweat or unexplained collapse in someone with diabetes — a heart attack that does not feel like oneCall emergency services and say: 'I have diabetes and I am worried this is my heart. Please do an ECG.' Do not wait for classic crushing chest pain. In diabetes a heart attack often arrives instead as sudden breathlessness, drenching sweat, nausea and vomiting, upper abdominal pain, jaw or arm ache, sudden exhaustion, or simply feeling profoundly unwell for no reason. If the pain sits high in your stomach and your sugars are also high, do not let 'this is probably my diabetes' end the conversation — ask for the ECG anyway. Chew aspirin only if emergency services tell you to. And hand over a list of every supplement you take, especially anything containing biotin (see the biotin flag below), because it can distort the blood test used to diagnose a heart attack.
emergencySevere, unrelenting abdominal pain boring through to the back on a GLP-1 or tirzepatide, or during rapid weight loss — possible pancreatitis or a blocked bile ductStop the injection and get emergency care the same day. Say out loud: 'I take a GLP-1 medication' (semaglutide/Ozempic/Wegovy, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide/Mounjaro) or 'I am on a rapid weight-loss programme', and then: 'Please check a lipase and look at my gallbladder.' On these drugs nausea and stomach upset are so common that everything abdominal gets filed as expected — which is exactly why this gets missed. The pain that matters is severe and constant rather than coming in waves, sits high in the abdomen, drills through to your back, is worse lying flat, eases when you lean forward, and does not settle between doses. Yellowing of the eyes or skin, pale stools, dark urine, or fever with pain under the right ribs need the same-day assessment too. A normal blood sugar and a negative ketone test do NOT rule this out, and neither does one normal blood test — if the pain is unchanged or worse, go back. Do not take your next weekly dose while waiting to be seen, and do not restart it on your own.
emergencySudden change in vision: new floaters, a curtain or shadow, sudden blurring, or loss of sight in one eyeGet emergency eye care today — an ophthalmologist, urgent eye clinic, or emergency department. A sudden shower of new floaters or dark spots, a curtain or shadow moving across your vision, a sudden blur, or losing sight in one eye can mean bleeding into the eye or the retina being pulled away. The window to save vision is short and closes in days, not months. Do not wait for your scheduled diabetic eye screening, and do not assume it is 'just the sugars being high.' If you take semaglutide or another GLP-1 receptor agonist, say so explicitly when you are seen: sudden painless loss of vision in one eye has been reported in connection with an optic-nerve condition called NAION in people taking semaglutide, and the treating team needs to know about the drug as well as about your diabetes.
emergencyOne-sided facial or sinus pain, nasal blackening, eyelid droop or bulging eye in someone with very high sugars or recent ketoacidosisGo to an emergency department immediately and use these words: 'I have diabetes and I am worried about an invasive fungal sinus infection.' Warning signs are one-sided facial pain or swelling, a blocked or bloody nose, black or dark discoloured tissue inside the nose or on the roof of the mouth, a drooping eyelid, double vision, or an eye that looks pushed forward — especially during or shortly after a period of very high blood sugars or ketoacidosis. This is rare. It also moves within days and is frequently fatal when it is mistaken for ordinary sinusitis, so ask to be examined and imaged rather than sent home with antibiotics for a 'sinus infection.'
urgentIllness, fasting, surgery, heavy alcohol or a very low-carbohydrate diet while taking an SGLT2 inhibitorCall your prescriber BEFORE you go ahead. If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor and you are sick, vomiting, not eating, dehydrated, drinking heavily, starting a very low-carb or fasting protocol, or scheduled for surgery or a procedure — these are the exact situations that tip people into ketoacidosis on this drug class. Your team may tell you to hold the medicine for a few days and restart it when you are eating and drinking normally. Do not decide that on your own, and never hold your insulin at the same time. Write 'I take an SGLT2 inhibitor' on your surgical and anaesthesia paperwork and say it to the anaesthetist.
urgentVomiting, diarrhoea, fever or any illness that stops you drinking, while taking metformin — sick-day rulesContact your prescriber or pharmacist the same day and ask one question: 'Should I pause my metformin while I am unwell?' If you are vomiting, have diarrhoea, a high fever, or simply cannot keep fluids down, your kidneys are working with less blood flow than usual and a drug they clear can build up. Standard practice is to hold metformin for the few days you are ill and restart once you have been eating and drinking normally again — but that instruction has to come from your prescriber, so ask for your own written metformin sick-day rules NOW, while you are well, and keep them with the tablets. This never applies to your insulin: do not hold insulin. Get emergency care if you develop deep or fast breathing, severe muscle aches, unusual drowsiness or marked weakness while unwell on metformin. Briefly holding metformin on a clinician's instruction during illness is not the same thing as stopping your diabetes treatment.
urgentEating much less than usual, fighting an infection, or drinking alcohol while on insulin or a sulfonylureaCall your prescriber the same day and ask one specific question: 'Should my dose come down while I am eating less?' If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea (glipizide, glyburide/glibenclamide, gliclazide, glimepiride) and your food intake has dropped — because you are sick, nauseated, fasting, low on money for groceries, or just have no appetite — the unchanged dose is now too big for the food going in. This is the single most common route to a hospital-level low. Do not assume someone will notice and adjust it for you. Older adults, and anyone with kidney disease, are at highest risk.
urgentAny foot ulcer, non-healing wound, or a red/hot/swollen/smelly foot — or a foot that suddenly changes shapeLook at the bottoms of your feet today, with a mirror if you need one. Any open sore, blister, callus with dark staining, cut that is not healing, redness, swelling, warmth, foul smell or drainage — or a foot that has suddenly changed shape or become hot and swollen without a wound — needs to be seen by a clinician within 24 hours, not at your next routine appointment. If you also have fever, chills, spreading redness, or the wound suddenly looks or smells worse, go to an emergency department. Keep weight off it until you are seen. Nerve damage means a limb-threatening wound can be completely painless, so trust your eyes over how it feels.
urgentA large, fast fall in HbA1c can itself cause harm — get your eyes checked BEFORE you start, and report new nerve pain or eye changes afterBEFORE you start anything expected to pull your blood sugar down fast — a very-low-calorie or total-diet-replacement programme, a strict low-carbohydrate diet, insulin, a GLP-1 injection — ask for a retinal examination and say why: 'I am about to improve my control quickly. Do my eyes need looking at first?' If you already know you have retinopathy, treat that as urgent rather than optional and ask whether laser treatment should happen before you start. AFTER a big improvement — roughly 2 percentage points or more of HbA1c in about three months — tell your prescriber this week if you develop new burning, shooting or electric-shock pain in your feet or legs, new light-headedness on standing, or any new blurring or eye change, and ask for an eye examination. Do NOT stop or cut your treatment on your own: the long-term benefit of getting the number down outweighs this, the nerve pain is usually temporary, and stopping carries its own dangers. Read this as a reason to be watched while you get your sugars down, not a reason to avoid getting them down. Sudden vision loss, a curtain across your sight or a shower of new floaters is an emergency — see the vision flag.
urgentPregnant or planning pregnancy with diabetes — including that ketoacidosis in pregnancy can occur at a near-normal glucoseTell your diabetes team now — before you change any medication, supplement or diet. Targets, medicines and monitoring all change in pregnancy, and some diabetes medications are not used in pregnancy. If you are pregnant and become unwell, are vomiting, or your ketones are raised — even if your glucose readings look normal — get emergency obstetric care the same day and ask specifically about ketoacidosis. In pregnancy this can develop with a near-normal blood sugar and can harm the baby quickly, so 'my numbers are fine' is not reassurance here. If you are sent home and the symptoms continue or you lose weight, go straight back.
urgentNew or rapidly worsening thirst, urination, unintended weight loss, blurred vision or exhaustionGet tested this week — and today if you also feel sick, are vomiting, or have stomach pain. Drinking and urinating far more than usual, losing weight without trying, new blurred vision, or a level of exhaustion that is new for you are the classic signs that blood sugar has climbed high enough to need medical treatment now. If you have already been diagnosed, these mean your current plan is not holding and needs a clinician, not a supplement. If these come on fast, or you are a lean adult, ask your clinician directly whether you should be tested for type 1 diabetes rather than assumed to be type 2 — the treatment is different and the wrong assumption is dangerous.
urgentBiotin in a supplement can falsify emergency blood tests, including the one used to diagnose a heart attackRead the label of everything you take and look for biotin (sometimes listed as vitamin B7 or vitamin H). It is a standard ingredient in chromium 'blood sugar' formulas, alpha-lipoic acid combinations, and hair-skin-nail products. If you take any, tell the emergency department, your cardiologist, your GP and the person drawing your blood — say it unprompted, because nobody will ask. Take the actual bottles with you. Before planned blood tests, ask your clinician or the laboratory how long to pause biotin beforehand; many laboratories have a set interval. Never stop a prescribed medicine over this — the fix is disclosure and timing, not stopping treatment.
urgent'Natural' blood-sugar supplements can contain undeclared prescription drugsIf a blood-sugar supplement is dropping your readings dramatically, or you feel shaky, sweaty, confused, weak or unwell after starting one — stop it, and tell your prescriber and pharmacist, taking the actual bottle with you. Independent laboratory testing has repeatedly found real prescription drugs hidden inside botanical supplements sold as natural, including a sulfonylurea — the drug class that puts people in hospital with low blood sugar. A product that works suspiciously well is a reason for suspicion, not confidence. Never stack an unknown supplement on top of insulin or a sulfonylurea without telling your team first, and never let a supplement replace a prescribed medicine.
routineGood numbers are not a clean bill of health — eye, kidney and nerve damage progress silently, and only screening finds them in timeKeep booking the three checks that find damage before you can feel it, even when your HbA1c looks good: a dilated or photographic retinal screen, a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio together with an eGFR blood test, and a foot examination that includes testing sensation. Ask your own clinician what interval applies to you. And be clear about what this page can and cannot tell you: almost everything on it was tested on blood numbers only, so a better number is not evidence that your eyes, kidneys or nerves have been protected. If you are choosing between options because one 'lowers blood sugar more', that is not the same question as which one prevents damage.
routineDriving on insulin or a sulfonylurea — especially in the first weeks after adding anything from this pageIf you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, check your glucose before you drive and again on long journeys, and keep fast-acting sugar inside the car within reach, not in the boot. Do not drive below 5.0 mmol/L (90 mg/dL): treat first, then wait 45 minutes after you are back above it before setting off, because your judgement and reaction time recover more slowly than your blood sugar does. Pay particular attention in the first few weeks after you add anything from this page on top of your medication, or start a diet that cuts carbohydrate — that is exactly when your usual dose is most likely to have become too big for you. Tell your prescriber, and check your licensing authority's rules; in many countries you are legally required to declare insulin treatment.

Clinical detail

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic disorder of glucose regulation driven by insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell insufficiency; prediabetes is the intermediate dysglycaemic state that precedes it. Using American Diabetes Association cut points as applied in US national surveillance, diabetes is identified by fasting plasma glucose >=126 mg/dL or HbA1c >=6.5%, and prediabetes by HbA1c 5.7-6.4% or fasting plasma glucose 100-125 mg/dL (PMID 36586345); the ADA 'Standards of Care in Diabetes-2025' section on Diagnosis and Classification is the current US reference document for these criteria, confirmatory testing, and distinguishing type 1, type 2 and other forms (PMID 39651986). PREVALENCE: in NHANES data from 21,600 US adults, age-standardised to the 2010 census, total (diagnosed plus undiagnosed) diabetes rose from 9.17% in 1999-2000 to 14.7% in 2017-2018 (difference 5.52%, 95% CI 2.69-8.35; P-trend <0.001) and prediabetes rose from 29.5% to 48.3% (difference 18.8%, 95% CI 13.3-24.4; P-trend <0.001), with substantial and persistent disparities by obesity, race and ethnicity, income, education and household food insecurity (PMID 36586345). Note that measured prevalence is highly definition-dependent, so figures quoted from different criteria are not interchangeable. FIRST-LINE TREATMENT: for prediabetes, structured intensive lifestyle intervention is first-line and outperformed drug therapy head-to-head — in the Diabetes Prevention Program (n=3,234, mean follow-up 2.8 years), a lifestyle programme targeting >=7% weight loss and >=150 min/week of activity reduced diabetes incidence by 58% (95% CI 48-66%) and metformin 850 mg twice daily by 31% (95% CI 17-43%) versus placebo, with lifestyle significantly more effective than metformin (NNT over 3 years 6.9 vs 13.9) (PMID 11832527). For established type 2 diabetes, first-line care combines diabetes self-management education and support, nutrition and physical activity with glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy selected around cardiovascular and kidney comorbidity, weight, hypoglycaemia risk and cost, as set out in ADA 'Standards of Care in Diabetes-2025' Section 9 (PMID 39651989) — note that the indexed abstract of that section is a scope statement and the agent-level recommendations sit in the full section text, which should be consulted directly rather than paraphrased from memory. Screening, staging and the choice among agents are clinician decisions; this page is not a substitute for them.
