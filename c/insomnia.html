Insomnia

Insomnia means trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early — often enough that it drags on your days. It is very common: about 1 in 10 adults lives with it as an ongoing condition, so if you're reading this at 2am, you are far from alone. The good news is that the best-studied treatment isn't a pill — it's a structured skills program called CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia), and every major medical guideline recommends it first. A regular doctor's visit is the right next step; the red flags below are the situations that deserve faster attention.

Strongest evidence here: CBT-I, Sleep restriction therapy, CPAP.

When to stop and get real care

These come before anything else on this page.

emergencyInsomnia along with hopelessness, feeling like a burden, or thoughts of suicide or self-harmIf you're having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or sleepless nights are coming with hopelessness, stop here and reach out now: call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, available 24/7). Veterans: call 988, then press 1. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.
urgentFalling asleep while driving, or fighting to stay awake at the wheel, at work with machinery, or while caring for a childIf you have nodded off while driving — even for a second — do not drive until you've been evaluated. Ask someone else to drive or use other transport, and contact your doctor this week about urgent evaluation for a sleep disorder.
urgentSigns of obstructive sleep apnea: loud snoring, gasping or choking awake, or someone noticing you stop breathing in your sleepIf you snore loudly, wake up gasping or choking, or a bed partner has seen you stop breathing during sleep — especially with morning headaches, high blood pressure, or heavy daytime sleepiness — see your doctor soon and ask about testing for sleep apnea. This is not something to self-manage with sleep tips or supplements.
urgentUsing sleeping pills or sedating sleep aids together with alcohol, opioid pain medicines, or other sedativesDo not combine sleep medications (prescription or over-the-counter) with alcohol, opioid painkillers, or other sedatives — the combination can dangerously slow your breathing. If you're already mixing them, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before your next dose, and never stop a long-term prescription sedative abruptly without medical guidance.
routineTrouble sleeping at least 3 nights a week for 3 months or moreIf poor sleep has been happening 3 or more nights a week for 3 months or longer, you've crossed into chronic insomnia — a real medical condition with a well-proven first-line treatment. Ask your doctor or health plan for a referral to CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia); in-person and digital programs both count.

Clinical detail

Chronic insomnia disorder is defined (DSM-5; ICSD-3) as dissatisfaction with sleep — difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or early-morning awakening — with daytime impairment, occurring at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months despite adequate opportunity for sleep. According to a PubMed-retrieved epidemiologic review (Morin & Jarrin 2022, PMID 35659072), approximately 10% of the adult population suffers from an insomnia disorder and another 20% experiences occasional insomnia symptoms; insomnia is frequently persistent, with a 40% persistence rate over 5 years. First-line treatment per guidelines is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I): the American College of Physicians recommends CBT-I as the initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia disorder (strong recommendation; PMID 27136449); the American Academy of Sleep Medicine strongly recommends multicomponent CBT-I and conditionally recommends against sleep hygiene as a stand-alone therapy (PMID 33164742); and the 2023 European Insomnia Guideline recommends CBT-I as first-line for chronic insomnia in adults of any age, delivered in person or digitally, with pharmacotherapy offered only when CBT-I is not sufficiently effective and generally limited to short-term use (PMID 38016484).

  2. Management of Chronic Insomnia Disorder in Adults: A Clinical Practice Guideline From the American College of Physicians.
    · PMID 27136449 · mixed

    ACP recommends that all adult patients receive cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the initial treatment for chronic insomnia disorder (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence); adding medication is a shared decision reserved for when CBT-I alone was unsuccessful (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence). (Qaseem et al., Ann Intern Med 2016; doi:10.7326/M15-2175)

  3. Behavioral and psychological treatments for chronic insomnia disorder in adults: an American Academy of Sleep Medicine clinical practice guideline.
    · PMID 33164742 · mixed

    AASM issues a STRONG recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I for chronic insomnia disorder in adults, conditional recommendations for brief therapies, stimulus control, sleep restriction, and relaxation as single components, and a conditional recommendation AGAINST sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy. (Edinger et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2021; doi:10.5664/jcsm.8986)

  4. The European Insomnia Guideline: An update on the diagnosis and treatment of insomnia 2023.
    · PMID 38016484 · mixed

    CBT-I is recommended as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults of any age, including patients with comorbidities, in person or digitally (Grade A); pharmacological intervention is offered when CBT-I is not sufficiently effective, with benzodiazepines and benzodiazepine receptor agonists limited to short-term use (≤4 weeks), and antihistamines, antipsychotics, fast-release melatonin, and phytotherapeutics not recommended. Polysomnography is indicated when other sleep disorders such as sleep-related breathing disorders are suspected. (Riemann et al., J Sleep Res 2023; doi:10.1111/jsr.14035)
